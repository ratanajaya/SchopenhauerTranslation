# §. 61.

We recall from Book Two that throughout nature, at every grade of the will's objectivation, individuals of every kind necessarily struggle against one another. This expresses an inner conflict of the will to live with itself. At the highest grade of objectivation this phenomenon, like everything else, becomes more distinct and can be deciphered further. For that purpose, let us first trace *egoism*, the starting point of all struggle, to its source.

We called time and space the *principium individuationis* (principle of individuation) because a multiplicity of things of the same kind is possible only in and through them. They are the essential forms of natural cognition, which has sprung from the will. The will therefore appears everywhere in a multiplicity of individuals. This multiplicity, however, affects only its appearances, not the will as thing in itself. The will is present wholly and undivided in each, seeing around it countless repetitions of its own essence. Yet it finds that essence—the truly real—directly only within itself. Thus each individual wants everything for himself, wants to possess everything or at least to rule it, and would like to destroy whatever resists him.

In beings with cognition something further is added. The individual is the bearer of the knowing subject, and that subject is the bearer of the world. All nature outside the individual, including every other individual, exists only in his representation. He is conscious of them only as representations, thus only indirectly and as something dependent on his own essence and existence. With his consciousness the world necessarily perishes for him: its being and nonbeing then become equivalent and indistinguishable. Every knowing individual therefore really is, and finds himself to be, the whole will to live, the in-itself of the world, and also the complementary condition of the world as representation: a microcosm to be valued as highly as the macrocosm. Nature herself, always true everywhere, gives him direct certainty of this from the outset, without any reflection.

These two necessary conditions explain why every individual, though vanishingly small and reduced to nothing in the boundless world, nevertheless makes himself the world's centre. He puts his own existence and welfare ahead of everyone else's and, from the natural standpoint, is ready to sacrifice everything else to them. He would destroy the world merely to preserve his own self, that drop in the ocean, a little longer. This disposition is *egoism*, essential to every thing in nature. It is precisely through egoism that the will's inner conflict with itself receives its terrible revelation.

Egoism has its basis and nature in the opposition of microcosm and macrocosm, or in the fact that the will's objectivation takes the *principium individuationis* as its form. The will thereby appears in the same way in countless individuals, wholly and completely in each on both sides, will and representation. Each individual is directly given to himself as the entire will and the entire representing subject, while the others are first given to him only as his representations. His own being and its preservation therefore take precedence over all of them together. Each looks upon his own death as the end of the world, while receiving news of acquaintances' deaths with comparative indifference unless personally affected.

At the highest degree of consciousness, the human one, egoism must reach its highest degree, just as cognition, pain, and pleasure do. The conflict among individuals arising from it must emerge most terribly. We see it everywhere, in small things and great. Its fearful side appears in the lives of great tyrants and villains and in wars that devastate the world. Its ridiculous side becomes a theme of comedy, emerging particularly in conceit and vanity, which La Rochefoucauld apprehended and presented *in abstracto* (in the abstract) as no one else has. We see it in world history and personal experience.

It appears most distinctly whenever a group of people is released from every law and order. Then Hobbes's `bellum omnium contra omnes` (war of all against all), admirably described in the first chapter of *De Cive* (On the Citizen), becomes immediately plain. We see not only each person trying to take from another what he wants, but often one person destroying another's entire happiness or life for a trivial increase in his own welfare. This is egoism's highest expression. In this respect its manifestations are surpassed only by those of actual malice, which seeks another's harm and pain wholly without self-interest or any advantage of its own. We shall discuss it shortly. Compare this account of egoism's source with its treatment in § 14 of my prize essay *On the Basis of Morality*.

A chief source of the suffering we found essential and unavoidable in all life, once suffering actually appears in a definite form, is this *Eris* (strife), the struggle among all individuals. It expresses the contradiction within the will to live, made visible through the *principium individuationis*. Animal fights are a cruel means of showing it directly and starkly. This original division is an inexhaustible source of suffering despite the precautions taken against it, which we shall now examine more closely.
