# §. 59.

Through the most general reflections on the elementary features of human life, we have become convinced in a sense *a priori* (prior to experience) that its whole constitution makes true happiness impossible: it is essentially many-sided suffering and a thoroughly unhappy condition. We could make this conviction more vivid by proceeding *a posteriori* (from experience), examining particular cases, bringing images before the imagination, and describing in examples the nameless misery that experience and history offer wherever and however one looks. But such a chapter would have no end and would leave the general standpoint essential to philosophy. It could also easily be taken for a mere declamation on human misery, of which there have been many, and accused of one-sidedness because it began with particular facts. Our cold, philosophical demonstration proceeding from the general and *a priori*—of the inevitable suffering grounded in life's essence—is free of that reproach and suspicion.

Confirmation *a posteriori* is readily found everywhere. Anyone awakened from the first dreams of youth who attends to personal experience and that of others, looks at life and at history past and present, and reads the great poets will, unless an indelible prejudice paralyzes judgment, recognize that this human world is the realm of chance and error. They rule it mercilessly in great and small matters, while folly and wickedness wield their scourges alongside them. Anything better wins through only with effort. What is noble and wise appears very rarely, and still more rarely is effective or heard. In thought the absurd and mistaken, in art the shallow and tasteless, and in action the evil and treacherous generally hold sway with only brief interruptions. Excellence of every kind is always an exception, one case among millions. Even when it finds expression in a lasting work, that work stands isolated after surviving its contemporaries' resentment, preserved like a meteorite originating in an order of things different from the one that rules here.

As for an individual life, every life story is a story of suffering. A life course is generally a continuous series of accidents large and small. Each person conceals them as far as possible, knowing that others will seldom sympathize or show compassion and will almost always find satisfaction in thinking of the afflictions from which they themselves are now spared. Perhaps no one at the end of life, if reflective and sincere, would wish to go through it again. Given that choice, he would much rather choose complete nonexistence.

The essential content of Hamlet's world-famous monologue can be summed up thus: our condition is so wretched that complete nonexistence would plainly be preferable. If suicide really offered us that, so that the alternative “to be or not to be” were before us in the full sense, we would unconditionally choose it as a most desirable end—`a consummation devoutly to be wish'd` (an end devoutly to be wished). Yet something in us says that this is not so, that death is not the end and not absolute annihilation. Likewise, what the father of history observed[^1] has probably never been refuted: no one has lived who has not wished more than once to avoid seeing the following day. The brevity of life, so often lamented, may therefore be its best feature.

If we could show each person the dreadful pains and torments to which life continually exposes him, he would shudder. Lead the most hardened optimist through hospitals, infirmaries, and surgical torture rooms; through prisons, torture chambers, and slave quarters; across battlefields and execution grounds; open to him all the dark dwellings of misery where it hides from cold curiosity; and finally let him look into Ugolino's tower of hunger. He would surely recognize at last the nature of this `meilleur des mondes possibles` (best of all possible worlds). Where else did *Dante* obtain the material for his Hell but from our real world? He made a very convincing hell of it. When he came to describe heaven and its joys, however, he faced an insuperable difficulty: our world offers no material for them. All he could do, instead of portraying paradise's joys, was repeat the instruction given him there by his ancestor, Beatrice, and various saints. This makes the nature of our world clear enough.

Human life, like every shoddy article, has an outside covered in false glitter. Suffering always hides, while everyone displays whatever pomp and splendour he can afford. The less inward contentment he has, the more he wishes to appear happy in others' opinion. Folly goes so far that others' opinion becomes a main goal of everyone's striving, though its utter worthlessness is already evident in the fact that in almost every language the word vanity, `vanitas` (emptiness), originally means emptiness and nothingness.

Even under all this show, life's torments can easily grow so great—and do so daily—that death, otherwise feared above everything, is seized with eagerness. If fate wishes to show its full malice, even this refuge may be barred. Under the power of enraged enemies, a sufferer may be left to cruel, slow torments without rescue. He calls vainly on his gods for help and remains mercilessly exposed to his fate. This helplessness merely mirrors the indomitability of his will, of which his person is the objecthood. An external power can no more change or cancel that will than a foreign power can release him from the torments arising from life, its appearance. In the central matter as in every other, the human being is referred back to himself. In vain does he make gods from whom to beg or coax what only his own will's power can bring about.

The Old Testament made the world and humanity the work of a god. The New Testament, to teach that salvation and redemption from this world's misery can come only from the world itself, had to make that god become human. It is and remains the human being's will upon which everything depends for him. Sannyasis, martyrs, and saints of every faith and name have freely and gladly endured every torment because the will to live had been cancelled in them. Even the slow destruction of its appearance was then welcome to them. But I do not wish to anticipate the account that follows.

I cannot refrain, however, from saying here that *optimism*, when it is more than thoughtless talk by people whose flat foreheads harbour nothing but words, seems to me not merely absurd but positively *wicked*: a bitter mockery of humanity's nameless sufferings. Nor should anyone suppose that Christian doctrine favours optimism. On the contrary, the Gospels use “world” and “evil” almost as synonyms.[^2]
