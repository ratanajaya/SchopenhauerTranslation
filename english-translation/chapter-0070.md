# §. 68.

After this digression on the identity of pure love and compassion, whose turning back toward one's own person has weeping as its sign, I take up again our account of the ethical significance of action. I shall now show how, from the same source as all goodness, love, virtue, and nobility, there finally emerges what I call denial of the will to live.

We saw earlier that hatred and malice depend on egoism, and that egoism rests on cognition's captivity to the *principium individuationis* (principle of individuation). We then found the origin and nature of justice and, beyond it, of love and nobility, even at their highest degrees, in seeing through that principle. This alone removes the distinction between oneself and other individuals and makes possible, and explains, a completely good disposition reaching the most unselfish love and most generous self-sacrifice for others.

When this insight into the *principium individuationis*, this immediate cognition of the will's identity in all its appearances, is present with great clarity, it has a still further effect on the will. Maya's veil may have lifted so far from a person's eyes that he no longer makes the egoistic distinction between himself and others, but takes as much part in their sufferings as in his own. He is helpful in the highest degree and is even prepared to sacrifice his own person when that will save several others. Since such a person recognizes himself, his innermost and true self, in all beings, he must also regard the endless sufferings of everything living as his own and make the whole world's pain his own. No suffering is foreign to him. The torments of others that he sees and can so seldom relieve, those he hears of indirectly, even those he knows only as possible, affect him like his own. His concern is no longer the changing welfare and distress of his own person, as it is for someone still captive to egoism. Having seen through the *principium individuationis*, he finds everything equally close. He knows the whole, apprehends its essence, and finds it caught in ceaseless passing away, vain striving, inner conflict, and constant suffering. Wherever he looks he sees suffering humanity and suffering animals and a vanishing world. All this is now as close to him as his own person is to the egoist.

How could he, knowing the world in this way, continue to affirm this life by constant acts of will and thereby bind himself ever more firmly to it and embrace it more tightly? The person still captive to egoism and the *principium individuationis* knows only individual things and their relation to himself; these become ever renewed *motives* for his willing. But knowledge of the whole and of the essence of things in themselves, as just described, becomes a *quietive* of every kind of willing. The will now turns away from life. It shudders at life's pleasures, recognizing in them its affirmation. The person reaches voluntary renunciation, resignation, true composure, and complete freedom from willing. For the rest of us still surrounded by Maya's veil, our own keenly felt suffering or another's vividly recognized suffering may at times bring us close to knowing life's emptiness and bitterness. We may wish to break the sting of desire through complete and permanent renunciation, shut suffering out, purify ourselves, and become holy. Yet appearance's illusion soon entangles us again, and its motives set the will in motion anew. We cannot tear ourselves free. The allure of hope, the flattery of the present, the sweetness of enjoyment, and the well-being that falls to our person amid a suffering world ruled by chance and error draw us back to life and fasten its bonds afresh. Thus Jesus says: “It is easier for a ship's cable to pass through the eye of a needle than for a rich man to enter the kingdom of God.”

Imagine life as a circular track of glowing coals with a few cool spots, which we must run around without cease. The person captive to illusion takes comfort in the cool spot on which he now stands or sees close ahead, and keeps running. The person who sees through the *principium individuationis* and knows the essence of things in themselves and thereby the whole is no longer open to that consolation: he sees himself at every point at once and steps off the track. His will turns. It no longer affirms its own nature mirrored in appearance but denies it. The phenomenon revealing this is the passage from virtue to *asceticism*. It no longer satisfies him to love others as himself and do as much for them as for himself. He becomes repelled by the being expressed in his own appearance, the will to live, the core and essence of the world he now knows to be wretched. He therefore disowns this being, which appears in him and is already expressed by his body. His conduct now contradicts his appearance openly. Although he is essentially nothing but an appearance of the will, he ceases to will anything, takes care not to attach his will to anything, and seeks to establish the greatest indifference to everything within himself. His healthy, strong body expresses the sexual drive through its genitals, yet he denies the will and contradicts his body: he refuses sexual satisfaction under every condition.

Voluntary and complete chastity is the first step in asceticism, or denial of the will to live. It thereby denies affirmation of the will beyond the individual's life and signals that with this body's life the will whose appearance it is also comes to an end. Nature, always truthful and candid, declares that if this maxim were universal, the human race would die out. From what was said in the second book about the connection among all appearances of the will, I believe we may assume that with its highest appearance the weaker reflection of it, animal life, would fall away too, just as partial shadows disappear with full light. With the complete abolition of cognition, the rest of the world would then vanish into nothing of itself, for without a subject there is no object. I would even relate this to a passage in the Vedas: “As hungry children in this world crowd around their mother, so all beings await the holy sacrifice” (*Asiatic Researches*, vol. 8, Colebrooke, “On the Vedas,” excerpt from the *Sama Veda*; also in Colebrooke's *Miscellaneous Essays*, vol. 1, p. 88). Sacrifice here means resignation generally. The rest of nature awaits redemption from the human being, who is both priest and sacrifice.

Remarkably, the admirable and unfathomably deep Angelus Silesius expressed the same thought in a little verse titled “Man Brings Everything to God”:

“Human! All things love you; around you they crowd and press;<br>
All run toward you so that they may come to God.”

An even greater mystic, Meister Eckhart, whose marvelous writings have at last become available in Franz Pfeiffer's edition, says on p. 459, wholly in the present sense: “I confirm this with Christ, when he says: When I am lifted up from the earth, I will draw all things to me (John 12:32). Thus the good person should carry all things up to God, to their first origin. The masters confirm that all creatures are made for the sake of man. See in every creature how one serves another: the ox the grass, the fish the water, the bird the air, the animal the forest. In this way all creatures serve the good person: the good person carries one creature within another to God.” He means that a person may use animals in this life in return for redeeming them in and with himself. I even think that the difficult passage Romans 8:21–24 can be interpreted in this sense.

Buddhism too has expressions of this thought. For example, when the Buddha, still a bodhisattva, has his horse saddled for the last time, to flee his father's residence for the wilderness, he says to it: “For a long time you have been caught in life and death. Now you shall cease to bear and pull. Carry me away just this once, O Kanthaka, and when I have attained the law [become Buddha], I shall not forget you” (*Foe Koue Ki*, translated by Abel Rémusat, p. 233).

Asceticism then shows itself in voluntary, deliberate poverty. This does not arise merely *per accidens* (incidentally), through giving away possessions to relieve another's suffering; it is now an end in itself. It serves as continual mortification of the will, so that the satisfaction of wishes and life's sweetness will not stir the will afresh when self-knowledge has inspired revulsion against it. The person who has reached this point still feels, as an animate body and concrete appearance of will, a disposition to every kind of willing. Yet he deliberately suppresses it, forcing himself to do none of what he wants and, on the contrary, to do what he does not want, even if there is no further purpose than mortifying the will. Since he denies the will appearing in his own person, he does not resist when someone else does the same, that is, commits an injustice against him. Every suffering that comes to him from outside, by chance or another's malice, every loss, disgrace, or insult, is therefore welcome. He receives it joyfully as a chance to assure himself that he no longer affirms the will but gladly takes the side of every enemy of the appearance of will that is his own person. He therefore bears such disgrace and suffering with inexhaustible patience and gentleness, returns good for evil without display, and allows neither anger nor desire to reignite within him.

As he mortifies the will itself, he also mortifies its visible objectivity, the body. He feeds it sparingly, lest its lush flourishing revive and more strongly excite the will, of which it is merely expression and mirror. He turns to fasting, even to mortification and self-torment, so that continual deprivation and suffering may increasingly break and kill the will he recognizes and detests as the source of his own and the world's suffering existence. When death finally comes and dissolves this appearance of will, the will's essence has already long since died through free self-denial, except for the weak remnant appearing as animation of this body. Death is therefore welcomed joyfully as a longed-for redemption. With it, it is not merely appearance that ends, as it does for others; the essence itself is abolished, having had only a weak existence left in and through this appearance.[^1] Its last frail bond now breaks. For a person who ends in this way, the world too has ended.

What I have described here with a weak tongue and only in general terms is no philosophical tale I invented today. It was the enviable life of many saints and noble souls among Christians, still more among Hindus and Buddhists, and among other believers too. However different the dogmas impressed on their reason, the inner, immediate, intuitive cognition from which alone all virtue and holiness can arise expressed itself in the same way through their conduct. Here again we see the great distinction between intuitive and abstract cognition, so important throughout this work and so little noticed before. A wide gulf separates the two, which only philosophy can bridge in regard to knowledge of the world's essence. In intuition, or *in concreto* (in the concrete), everyone is aware of all philosophical truths; bringing them into abstract knowledge and reflection is the philosopher's task, and nothing more is asked or possible.

Perhaps, then, the inner essence of holiness, self-denial, mortification of one's own will, and asceticism is expressed here for the first time abstractly and without myth as *denial of the will to live*, occurring when complete knowledge of the will's own essence becomes a quietive of all willing. But all those saints and ascetics knew it immediately and expressed it through action. Sharing the same inner cognition, they used very different language according to the dogmas they had adopted. An Indian, a Christian, and a Lamaist saint must each give a very different account of his own conduct, but this is wholly indifferent to the matter. A saint may hold the most absurd superstition or, conversely, be a philosopher; either is equally possible. His conduct alone certifies him as a saint, for morally it springs not from abstract cognition but from immediate, intuitive cognition of the world and its essence. He interprets it through some dogma only to satisfy his reason. A saint no more needs to be a philosopher than a philosopher needs to be a saint, just as a perfectly beautiful person need not be a great sculptor, nor a great sculptor beautiful. In general, it is a strange demand that a moral philosopher recommend only the virtues he possesses himself. To repeat the whole essence of the world clearly and generally in abstract concepts, and to lay it before reason as a reflected image in concepts that endure and remain ready to hand: this, and nothing else, is philosophy. I recall the passage from Francis Bacon quoted in the first book.

Yet my description of denial of the will to live and of a noble soul's conduct, of a resigned saint voluntarily doing penance, is only abstract and general and therefore cold. Since the cognition from which denial of the will arises is intuitive, not abstract, its complete expression is found not in abstract concepts but solely in deeds and a life's conduct. To understand more fully what philosophy calls denial of the will to live, we must become acquainted with examples in experience and reality. We shall not find them in daily experience, of course: *nam omnia praeclara tam difficilia quam rara sunt* (“all excellent things are as difficult as they are rare”), as Spinoza says so well. Unless a particularly fortunate fate makes us eyewitnesses, we must turn to the lives of such people. Even the little of Indian literature known so far in translation shows it rich in accounts of the lives of saints and penitents, called *samanas*, *sannyasis*, and so on. The familiar, though not entirely commendable, *Mythologie des Indous par Mad. de Polier* (*Mythology of the Hindus by Madame de Polier*) contains many excellent examples, especially in chapter 13 of volume 2.

There is no lack of Christian examples either. Read the generally poorly written biographies of people called saintly souls, Pietists, Quietists, devout enthusiasts, and the like. Collections have been made at various times, such as Tersteegen's *Lives of Holy Souls*, Reiz's *History of the Reborn*, and in our day a collection by Kanne which, among much that is poor, includes some good material, particularly the life of Beata Sturmin. The life of Saint Francis of Assisi, a true personification of asceticism and model of all mendicant monks, belongs especially here. His younger contemporary Saint Bonaventure, also famous as a scholastic, wrote his life, recently republished as *Vita S. Francisci a S. Bonaventura concinnata* (*Life of Saint Francis compiled by Saint Bonaventure*, Soest, 1847). Shortly before that a careful, extensive biography using all the sources had appeared in France: Chavin de Mallan's *Histoire de S. François d'Assise* (*History of Saint Francis of Assisi*, 1845). As an Eastern parallel to these monastic writings we have Spence Hardy's very readable *Eastern Monachism: An Account of the Order of Mendicants Founded by Gotama Buddha* (1850). It shows us the same matter in different clothing and also how little it matters whether its religion is theistic or atheistic.

Above all, as a particular and very detailed factual illustration of my concepts, I recommend Madame Guyon's autobiography. It must delight every person of better character to know this noble and great soul, whose memory always fills me with reverence, and to do justice to the excellence of her disposition while overlooking the superstition of her reason. The book will always be held in low repute by ordinary minds, the majority, since each person can appreciate only what is at least somewhat akin to him and for which he has at least a weak disposition. This holds in ethics as in intellectual matters. One might even count the familiar French biography of Spinoza as an example, if one uses as its key the splendid opening of his otherwise very inadequate *De emendatione intellectus* (*On the Improvement of the Understanding*). I also recommend that passage as the most effective means I know of calming a storm of passions. Finally, even the great Goethe, for all his Greek spirit, did not consider it beneath him to show us this fairest side of humanity in poetry's clarifying mirror. In the “Confessions of a Beautiful Soul” he gave an idealized account of Fräulein Klettenberg's life, later supplying historical information in his autobiography. He also told the life of Saint Philip Neri twice.

World history must always be silent about people whose lives best and most fully illustrate this important point of our inquiry. Its subject is quite another matter, indeed the opposite: the affirmation and appearance of the will to live in countless individuals, not its denial and surrender. At the highest point of its objectivation the will's division against itself emerges there with complete clarity. History displays now an individual's superiority through cunning, now the strength of a crowd through sheer numbers, now the power of chance personified as fate, and always the futility and emptiness of the whole striving. We, however, are philosophers seeking the ethical significance of acts rather than following the thread of appearances in time. Taking that significance as our sole measure of importance, we should not be deterred by the permanent majority of commonness and banality from acknowledging that the world's greatest, most important, and most significant appearance is not the conqueror of the world but the one who overcomes the world. It is the quiet, unnoticed life of someone in whom a cognition dawns that leads him to surrender and deny the will to live, which fills everything and drives and strives in everything. Only here, in him alone, does the will's freedom appear, and his action becomes the direct opposite of ordinary action. For a philosopher, the lives of holy, self-denying people are consequently far more instructive and important in this respect than even Plutarch and Livy, though generally badly written and mingled with superstition and nonsense, because of the significance of their subject.

To know more closely and completely what we call denial of the will to live in the general, abstract terms of our exposition, it helps greatly to consider ethical instructions given in this spirit by people filled with it. They also show how old our view is, even if its purely philosophical expression is new. Christianity lies nearest to us. Its ethics is wholly in the spirit indicated and leads not only to the highest degrees of love of humanity but also to renunciation. The latter is already plainly present as a seed in the apostles' writings, though it developed fully and was expressly stated only later. The apostles prescribe love of one's neighbor equal to self-love, beneficence, the return of love and good deeds for hatred, patience, gentleness, endurance of all injuries without resistance, restraint in food to suppress desire, and, if one can, complete resistance to sexual desire. Here we already see the first stages of asceticism, or denial of the will proper. The latter expression means exactly what the Gospels call denying oneself and taking up the cross (Matthew 16:24–25; Mark 8:34–35; Luke 9:23–24, 14:26–27, 33). This tendency developed further and gave rise to penitents, anchorites, and monasticism. Its origin was pure and holy, but for that very reason wholly unsuited to most people, so that its later products could only be hypocrisy and abomination: *abusus optimi pessimus* (“the abuse of the best is the worst”).

As Christianity developed further, that ascetic seed came into full bloom in the writings of Christian saints and mystics. Alongside the purest love they preached complete resignation, voluntary and total poverty, true composure, perfect indifference to worldly things, dying to one's own will and rebirth in God, complete forgetting of one's own person, and immersion in the contemplation of God. A full account appears in Fénelon's *Explication des maximes des Saints sur la vie intérieure* (*Explanation of the Maxims of the Saints on the Inner Life*). Nowhere, perhaps, is the spirit of Christianity in this development expressed as fully and powerfully as in the German mystics, Meister Eckhart and the justly celebrated *German Theology*. In the preface he wrote for it, Luther says that apart from the Bible and Augustine he learned more from this book about God, Christ, and humanity than from any other. We received its authentic and uncorrupted text, however, only in Pfeiffer's Stuttgart edition of 1851. Its precepts and teaching are the fullest exposition, born of deep conviction, of what I have described as denial of the will to live. One should come to know it more closely there before pronouncing on it with Jewish-Protestant self-assurance. Tauler's *Following the Poor Life of Christ* and his *Medulla animae* (*Marrow of the Soul*) are written in the same excellent spirit, though not quite equal to it. In my view, the teaching of these genuine Christian mystics stands to that of the New Testament as distilled spirits stand to wine. Or what the New Testament lets us see through veil and mist stands before us in their works uncovered, clear and distinct. Finally, one might regard the New Testament as the first initiation and the mystics as the second: σμικρὰ καὶ μεγάλα μυστήρια (“the lesser and the greater mysteries”).

What we have called denial of the will to live is developed still further, expressed in more ways and pictured more vividly in ancient Sanskrit works than was possible in the Christian Church and the Western world. This ethical view of life could perhaps take a further and more definite form there chiefly because it was not constrained by an alien element, as Christianity was by Jewish doctrine. Christianity's sublime founder necessarily had to accommodate himself to and join with that doctrine, partly consciously and perhaps partly without knowing it. Christianity thus consists of two very different elements. I would call the purely ethical one especially, even exclusively, Christian, and distinguish it from the Jewish dogmatism it found already in place. It has often been feared, especially now, that this excellent and healing religion could fall into complete decay. If so, I would seek the cause only in its composition from two originally alien elements joined by the course of history. Their differing affinity for and reaction to the advancing spirit of the age could produce a separation and dissolution. Even then, however, its purely ethical part would remain intact, for it is indestructible.

In Hindu ethics, as far as we know its literature so far, we find this view expressed most powerfully and variously in the Vedas, Puranas, poems, myths, legends of saints, maxims, and rules of life.[^2] It prescribes love of one's neighbor with complete surrender of self-love; love reaching beyond the human race to everything living; beneficence even to the point of giving away one's hard-won daily earnings; boundless patience toward every offender; returning every evil, however grave, with good and love; willing and cheerful endurance of disgrace; abstention from animal food; complete chastity and renunciation of all sensual pleasure for anyone striving for holiness proper; surrender of all property; leaving every home and all relatives; and deep, complete solitude spent in silent contemplation, voluntary penance, and terrible, slow self-torment. The aim is complete mortification of the will, sometimes carried to voluntary death by starvation, facing crocodiles, throwing oneself from a sacred Himalayan peak, being buried alive, or flinging oneself beneath the wheels of the enormous chariot bearing idols amid singing, jubilation, and dancing by *bayaderes*. These precepts originated more than four thousand years ago. Though this people has declined in many respects, individuals still follow them now even to the furthest extremes.[^3] Something practiced for so long among so many millions despite requiring the gravest sacrifices cannot be an arbitrary fancy; it must be grounded in human nature.

More than this, the accord between the life of a Christian penitent or saint and that of an Indian is astonishing. Their dogmas, customs, and surroundings differ fundamentally, yet their striving and inner lives are the same, as are their precepts. Tauler, for instance, speaks of the complete poverty one should seek: to relinquish everything that could yield any worldly comfort or satisfaction, because it all gives the will fresh nourishment when the goal is its complete extinction. As an Indian counterpart, Fo's precepts finally instruct the sannyasi, who must own nothing and have no home, not even to lie under the same tree too often, lest he acquire a fondness or attachment to it. Christian mystics and teachers of Vedanta agree, too, that external works and religious practices become superfluous for someone who has reached perfection. Such agreement across such different times and peoples is factual evidence that it expresses not a perversity or madness of disposition, as shallow optimists like to claim, but a side of human nature that is essential and appears rarely only because of its excellence.


I have now named sources from which the appearances of denial of the will to live can be learned directly from life. This is in a sense the most important point of our whole inquiry. Yet I have presented it only very generally. It is better to refer readers to those who speak from immediate experience than to swell this book needlessly by repeating their accounts less effectively.

I will add only a little to characterize their state in general. We saw earlier that the evil person, through the vehemence of his willing, suffers constant, consuming inner torment, and that when every object of willing is exhausted he finally cools the fierce thirst of self-will at the sight of another's suffering. By contrast, someone in whom denial of the will to live has dawned is full of inner joy and true heavenly peace, however poor, joyless, and deprived his condition looks from outside. This is not the restless drive to live or exuberant joy that requires severe suffering before or after it, such as marks a pleasure-seeking life. It is an unshakable peace, deep calm, and inward serenity. When we see or imagine this condition, we cannot look at it without the greatest longing. We recognize it at once as the only right state, infinitely outweighing all others, and our better spirit calls us toward it with a great *sapere aude* (“dare to be wise”). We then feel that every fulfilment of desire wrested from the world resembles alms that keep a beggar alive today only so he can go hungry again tomorrow. Resignation, by contrast, resembles an inherited estate that forever relieves its owner of care.

Recall from the third book that much of aesthetic pleasure in beauty consists in our entering pure contemplation and being freed for a moment from all willing, desires, and cares. We seem to lose ourselves. We are no longer individuals cognizing for the sake of constant willing, correlates of individual things that become motives for us, but pure, eternal subjects of cognition free of will, correlates of the Idea. We know that these moments, in which we rise as though from the heavy air of earth, freed from the fierce urge of will, are the most blessed we experience. From this we can infer how blessed the life of someone must be whose will is calmed not momentarily, as in enjoyment of beauty, but forever, indeed wholly extinguished except for the last glowing spark that maintains the body and will go out with it. Such a person, having finally overcome his own nature after many bitter struggles, remains only as a purely knowing being, an unclouded mirror of the world. Nothing can frighten or move him now, for he has cut the thousand threads of willing that held us to the world and pull us to and fro in desire, fear, envy, and anger amid constant pain. Calmly and smilingly he looks back on the phantoms of this world that once stirred and tormented him. They now lie as indifferently before him as chess pieces after a game or, in the morning, discarded carnival costumes whose shapes teased and troubled us during the night. Life and its forms hover before him only as a passing appearance, like a light morning dream to someone half awake, through which reality already shows and which can no longer deceive. Like that dream they finally vanish without a violent transition.

These reflections help us understand the sense in which Madame Guyon often says near the end of her autobiography: “Everything is indifferent to me: I *can* want nothing more; often I do not know whether I exist or not.” To express how, when the will has died, the death of the body—only its appearance, whose disappearance therefore loses all meaning—can no longer be bitter and is instead most welcome, let me quote the words of this holy penitent, though they are not elegantly phrased: *Midi de la gloire; jour où il n'y a plus de nuit; vie qui ne craint plus la mort, dans la mort même: parceque la mort a vaincu la mort, et que celui qui a souffert la première mort, ne goutera plus la seconde mort* (“Midday of glory; day in which there is no more night; life which no longer fears death, even in death: for death has conquered death, and one who has suffered the first death shall no longer taste the second death”) (*Vie de Mad. de Guion*, vol. 2, p. 13).

We must not suppose, however, that once cognition has become a quietive and denial of the will to live has occurred, it can no longer waver and can be rested upon like an acquired possession. It must be won anew through constant struggle. The body is the will itself, only in the form of objectivity, or as appearance in the world as representation. As long as it lives, the entire will to live remains present as a possibility, always striving to become actual and blaze up again with all its heat. In the lives of saints the peace and blessedness described are therefore only the flower of continual conquest of the will; their soil is unending struggle with the will to live. No one can have lasting peace on earth. Their inner histories are full of struggles of the soul, temptations, and abandonment by grace—that mode of cognition which renders all motives ineffective, quiets every act of willing as a universal quietive, gives the deepest peace, and opens the gate of freedom.

We accordingly see people who have once reached denial of the will striving with all their strength to remain there. They force themselves into every kind of renunciation, lead a hard, penitential life, and seek out what they find unpleasant, all to subdue the will that continually rises again. Knowing already the value of redemption, they anxiously guard the salvation they have won. They feel scruples of conscience at every innocent pleasure or tiny stirring of vanity, which dies last even here: the most indestructible, energetic, and foolish of human inclinations. By *asceticism*, a word I have often used, I mean in the narrower sense this *deliberate* breaking of the will: refusing what is pleasant, seeking what is unpleasant, choosing a life of penance and self-mortification, to keep the will subdued.

People who have reached denial of the will practice such asceticism to remain in that condition. But suffering imposed by fate is a second path (*δεύτερος πλοῦς*, “second voyage”[^4]) to denial. Indeed, we may assume most people reach it only this way. Suffering *felt by oneself*, rather than merely recognized, most often brings complete resignation, often only near death. For only a few is cognition alone sufficient: seeing through the *principium individuationis*, it first produces the most perfect goodness of disposition and universal love of humanity, then makes them recognize all the world's suffering as their own, until denial of the will follows. Even for someone approaching this point, the tolerable state of his own person, the flattery of the moment, the lure of hope, and the ever available satisfaction of the will—pleasure—almost always obstruct denial and continually tempt him to affirm again. These lures have therefore been personified as devils. Generally the will must be broken by the greatest personal suffering before it denies itself.

We then see someone driven through every degree of increasing distress, resisting fiercely, to the brink of despair. Suddenly he turns inward, knows himself and the world, changes his whole being, and rises above himself and all suffering. As though purified and sanctified by it, he willingly renounces everything he had previously willed most fiercely, in unassailable calm, blessedness, and elevation, and gladly receives death. This is the silver gleam of denial of the will to live, or redemption, suddenly emerging from suffering's purifying flame. Even very evil people are sometimes purified through deepest pain to this degree: they become others, wholly transformed. Their previous misdeeds therefore no longer trouble their conscience, though they willingly atone for them with death and welcome the ending of the appearance of the will now alien and repulsive to them. In the story of Gretchen's sufferings in his immortal *Faust*, Goethe has given us a clearer and more vivid portrayal of denial of the will through profound misfortune and despair of rescue than any other I know in poetry. Gretchen perfectly exemplifies this second path, which leads to denial not, like the first, by cognition alone of a whole world's suffering willingly taken as one's own, but through immense personal pain actually felt. Many tragedies bring their fiercely willing heroes at the end to such complete resignation, where will to live and its appearance usually end together. Yet no work I know presents the essential transformation as clearly and without distractions as this passage of *Faust*.

In actual life we often see such a transformation among unfortunate people who must drain the greatest measure of suffering. All hope has been taken from them and, in full possession of their minds, they face a shameful, violent, often agonizing death on the scaffold. We should not assume their characters differ from most people's as much as their fate suggests; circumstances largely account for it. Nevertheless they are guilty and evil to a considerable degree. Many of them, once hope is wholly gone, change in the way described. They now show genuine goodness and purity of disposition and a real abhorrence of even the slightest evil or unkind act. They forgive their enemies, even those through whom they have suffered innocently—not merely in words or from hypocritical fear of judges in the underworld, but sincerely and wholeheartedly—and desire no revenge. At last even their suffering and death become dear to them, for denial of the will to live has occurred. They often refuse an offered rescue and die willingly, calmly, and blessedly.

In their excess of pain the ultimate secret of life has been revealed: the evil deed and the suffering, suffering and hatred, the tormented and the tormentor, however different they appear to cognition following the principle of sufficient reason, are one in themselves. They are appearances of the one will to live, which objectivates its conflict with itself through the *principium individuationis*. They have known both sides, evil doing and suffering, in full measure. At last recognizing their identity, they reject both together and deny the will to live. As I said, it makes no difference through which myths and dogmas they account to their reason for this immediate, intuitive cognition and their transformation.


*Matthias Claudius* was surely a witness to such a change of heart when he wrote the remarkable essay “Conversion Story of ***” in *Wandsbecker Bote* (part 1, p. 115). It ends: “A person's way of thinking can pass from one point on a circle to its opposite, and back again to the former point, if circumstances trace out the arc for him. Such changes are not in themselves anything great or interesting in a human being. But that *remarkable, comprehensive, transcendental change*, when the whole circle is torn irreparably apart and every law of psychology becomes vain and empty, when the coat of skins is taken off or at least turned inside out and it is as though scales fall from a person's eyes—that is something for which anyone at all conscious of the breath in his nostrils would leave father and mother, if he could hear and learn something certain about it.”

Neither closeness to death nor hopelessness is absolutely necessary for such purification by suffering. Without them, great misfortune and pain may force on a person knowledge of the will to live's conflict with itself and the emptiness of all striving. Thus people who led very eventful lives driven by passion—kings, heroes, adventurers—have often changed suddenly, taken to resignation and penance, and become hermits or monks. All genuine conversion stories belong here. One is that of Ramon Llull. A beautiful woman he had long courted finally summoned him to her room, and he expected all his wishes to be fulfilled. Instead she opened her bodice and showed him her breast, horribly eaten away by cancer. From that moment, as though he had seen into hell, he converted, left the court of the king of Majorca, and went into the wilderness to do penance.[^5] The conversion of the Abbé Rancé, briefly recounted in chapter 48 of the second volume, is very similar. Both were prompted by an abrupt passage from pleasure to life's horrors. This helps explain the striking fact that the most pleasure-loving, cheerful, sensual, and light-minded nation of Europe, the French, gave rise to by far the strictest monastic order, the Trappists. After its decline, Rancé restored it. Despite revolutions, changes in the Church, and the spread of unbelief, it has maintained its purity and fearsome austerity to this day.

Yet cognition of the kind described, concerning the character of this existence, can vanish along with the event that prompted it. The will to live and the former character then return. We see the passionate Benvenuto Cellini changed in this way once in prison and once during a serious illness, only to fall back into his old condition when the suffering ends. In general, denial of the will no more follows suffering with the necessity of an effect following a cause. The will remains free. This is the one point where its freedom enters appearance directly, hence the force of Claudius's amazement at the “transcendental change.” For any suffering one can conceive a will still more vehement and thus unconquered. Plato accordingly tells us in the *Phaedo* of people who continue feasting, drinking, and enjoying sex up to the moment of execution, affirming life into death. In Cardinal Beaufort, Shakespeare sets before us the terrible end of a villain who dies in despair: neither suffering nor death can break a will fierce enough for the utmost wickedness.[^6]

The fiercer the will, the more glaring the appearance of its conflict and thus the greater the suffering. A world that was the appearance of a much fiercer will to live than the present one would contain correspondingly greater suffering. It would be a *hell*.

All suffering has a potential sanctifying power insofar as it mortifies the will and calls for resignation. This explains why great misfortune and deep pain command a certain reverence in themselves. But the sufferer becomes wholly venerable to us only when he looks over his life as a chain of sufferings, or mourns a great incurable pain, without dwelling on the particular circumstances that plunged his own life into sorrow or on that single great misfortune. Up to that point his cognition still follows the principle of sufficient reason and clings to an individual appearance: he still wants life, just not under the conditions allotted him. He becomes truly venerable when his view rises from the particular to the universal, and he sees his own suffering merely as an example of the whole. Becoming a genius in ethical matters, he takes one case for thousands. Life as a whole, apprehended as essentially suffering, then brings him to resignation. This is why the princess in Goethe's *Torquato Tasso* is venerable when she speaks of how her own life and those of her loved ones have always been sad and joyless, while looking entirely to the universal.

We always imagine a very noble character with a tinge of quiet sorrow. This is nothing like constant annoyance at daily adversity, which would be an ignoble trait and give reason to fear an evil disposition. It is an awareness, born of cognition, of the emptiness of all goods and the suffering of all life, not merely one's own. Yet personal suffering, especially one great experience, may first awaken this cognition. A single unattainable wish brought Petrarch to the resigned sorrow over all life that speaks so movingly from his works. The Daphne he pursued had to escape his grasp and leave him the immortal laurel instead. When fate breaks the will to some degree through such a great, irrevocable refusal, hardly anything more is willed, and character becomes gentle, sorrowful, noble, and resigned. Finally, when grief has no particular object but spreads over life as a whole, it becomes a kind of inward turning, a withdrawal and gradual vanishing of the will. It gently but deeply undermines even the will's visible form, the body. The person feels his bonds loosening and softly anticipates death, which announces itself as dissolution of body and will together. A secret joy therefore accompanies this grief, the feeling I believe the most melancholy of peoples calls *the joy of grief*.

There is also the danger here of *sentimentality*, both in life and in poetic depictions of it. If one continually mourns and complains without rising to resignation and taking courage, one loses earth and heaven alike and is left with watery sentiment. Suffering becomes a venerable path to redemption only when it takes the form of pure cognition and thereby, as a *quietive of the will*, brings true resignation. In this respect, when we see anyone deeply unfortunate, we feel a certain respect akin to that compelled by virtue and nobility, and our own good fortune seems a reproach. We cannot help regarding every suffering, our own or another's, as at least a possible approach to virtue and holiness, and pleasures and worldly satisfactions as a movement away from them. This goes so far that someone enduring great bodily or mental suffering, or even doing exhausting physical labor by the sweat of his brow, visibly spent but patient and without complaint, strikes us, if we watch him intently, as a sick person undergoing a painful cure. He bears the pain willingly and even with satisfaction, knowing that the more he suffers, the more of the disease is destroyed and that his present pain measures his healing.

From everything said so far, denial of the will to live, called complete resignation or holiness, always arises from a quietive of the will: cognition of its inner conflict and essential emptiness as expressed in the suffering of all living things. The two paths differ in what prompts this cognition. On one, it is suffering merely and purely *known*, freely taken on by seeing through the *principium individuationis*. On the other, it is suffering immediately *felt oneself*. True salvation, redemption from life and suffering, is inconceivable without complete denial of the will. Until then each person is nothing other than that will itself. Its appearance is a vanishing existence, a striving always empty and frustrated, and the world full of suffering that we have described. All belong to it irrevocably and alike. For we found that life is always assured to the will to live; its only real form is the present, from which the will can never escape, however much birth and death rule in appearance. Indian myth expresses this by saying, “They are reborn.”

The great ethical difference among characters means that the evil person is infinitely far from cognition leading to denial of the will. All the torments that appear *possible* in life are thus *actual* for him in truth. Even his possibly happy present state is only an appearance mediated by the *principium individuationis*, Maya's illusion, the beggar's happy dream. The suffering he inflicts on others in the fierceness and fury of his will's urge measures the suffering whose personal experience would be unable to break his will and lead it finally to denial. Conversely, every true and pure love, indeed every freely chosen justice, arises from seeing through the *principium individuationis*. When that insight arrives in full force, it brings complete sanctification and redemption. Their appearance is the state of resignation described above, accompanied by unshakable peace and the highest joy in death.[^7]
