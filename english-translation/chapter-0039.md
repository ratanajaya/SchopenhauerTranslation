# §. 37.

Although, on the account given above, genius consists in the ability to know the Ideas of things independently of the principle of sufficient reason, rather than knowing individual things, which exist only in relations, and to stand before those Ideas as their correlate, no longer an individual but the pure subject of cognition, this ability must also dwell in everyone to some degree. Otherwise people would be no more capable of enjoying works of art than of producing them. They would have no receptivity at all to the beautiful or the sublime; indeed, these words could mean nothing to them.

We must therefore assume that everyone, unless there are people wholly incapable of aesthetic pleasure, has some power to recognize the Ideas in things and, in doing so, to set aside their own personality for a moment. Genius differs only in possessing this mode of cognition to a much higher degree and for longer stretches. That allows the person of genius to retain the presence of mind needed to reproduce what has been cognized in a deliberate work: the work of art. Through it, the artist communicates the Idea apprehended to others.

The Idea remains one and the same in this process. Hence aesthetic pleasure is essentially the same whether it comes through a work of art or directly through contemplation of nature and life. A work of art merely makes the cognition in which that pleasure consists easier. The Idea meets us more readily in art than in nature or actuality because the artist, having cognized the Idea rather than the actual thing, reproduces the Idea alone in the work, separating it from actuality and leaving out every distracting accident.

The artist lets us see the world through the artist's eyes. To have such eyes, to recognize what is essential in things and lies outside all relations, is the inborn gift of genius. To be able to lend us those eyes, however, is the acquired skill, the technique of art.

I have now outlined the inner nature of aesthetic cognition in its most general terms. The more detailed philosophical treatment of the beautiful and the sublime that follows will therefore discuss each in both nature and art, without separating the two. We will first consider what happens in a person moved by beauty or sublimity. Whether the experience comes directly from nature and life or through art makes an external difference, but no essential one.
