# §. 41.

The course of our discussion required us to insert the account of the sublime at a point where the account of the beautiful was only half finished: we had dealt only with its subjective side. For the sublime differs from the beautiful only through a special modification of that side. Every aesthetic contemplation presupposes and requires a state of pure, will-less cognition. That state may arise without resistance, almost of itself, when the object invites and draws us to it and the will simply disappears from consciousness. Or it may have to be won through a free and conscious elevation above the will, when the contemplated object itself stands in an adverse, hostile relation to the will—dwelling on that relation would abolish contemplation. This is the difference between the beautiful and the sublime.

In the object, the two are not essentially different. In either case, the object of aesthetic contemplation is not the individual thing but the Idea striving to reveal itself in it: the adequate objecthood of the will at a particular grade. The Idea's necessary correlate, equally withdrawn from the principle of sufficient reason, is the pure subject of cognition. The correlate of the individual thing, by contrast, is the knowing individual; both belong within the domain of that principle.

When we call an object beautiful, we say that it is an object of our aesthetic contemplation. This means two things. First, the sight of it makes us *objective*: while contemplating it we are conscious of ourselves no longer as individuals but as the pure, will-less subject of cognition. Second, we recognize in the object an Idea rather than an individual thing. This can happen only insofar as our contemplation is not governed by the principle of sufficient reason. We do not pursue the object's relations to anything outside it—relations that ultimately always connect with our willing—but rest with the object itself.

The Idea and the pure subject of cognition always enter consciousness together as necessary correlates. When they do, all difference of time immediately disappears, for both are wholly foreign to the principle of sufficient reason in all its forms and lie outside the relations it establishes. They are like the rainbow and the sun, which take no part in the ceaseless movement and succession of falling raindrops. If, for example, I look at a tree aesthetically, with an artist's eyes, and thus cognize its Idea rather than the tree itself, it at once becomes immaterial whether this is that tree or its ancestor that flourished a thousand years ago. It is equally immaterial whether the observer is this individual or any other who lives at any time or place. Along with the principle of sufficient reason, the individual thing and the knowing individual fall away. Nothing remains but the Idea and the pure subject of cognition, which together constitute the adequate objecthood of the will at this grade.

The Idea is removed not only from time but also from space. Strictly speaking, the Idea is not the spatial shape before me. It is that shape's expression, its pure significance, its innermost essence, which reveals itself to me and addresses me. This may be wholly the same even when the shape's spatial proportions differ greatly.

Now, on the one hand, every existing thing can be contemplated purely objectively and apart from all relations. On the other, in every thing the will appears at some grade of its objecthood, so that the thing expresses an Idea. Hence *every thing is beautiful*. Even the most insignificant thing permits pure, objective, will-less contemplation and thereby proves beautiful, as the Dutch still lifes mentioned earlier (§ 38) attest.

One thing is more beautiful than another insofar as it makes such purely objective contemplation easier, meets it halfway, or virtually compels it; we then call it very beautiful. This can happen, first, because as an individual thing it expresses the Idea of its species purely through the very clear, definite, and thoroughly significant relation of its parts. By combining in itself the full range of expressions possible to its species, it reveals that species' Idea perfectly. It thus greatly eases the viewer's passage from the individual thing to the Idea and, with it, to the state of pure contemplation. Or an object's special beauty may lie in the fact that the Idea speaking from it is itself a high grade of the will's objecthood, and therefore profoundly significant and expressive. This is why the human being is beautiful above all else and why revealing human nature is art's highest aim. Human form and expression are the most significant objects of the visual arts, just as human action is the most significant object of poetry.

Nevertheless, every thing has its own particular beauty. This is true not only of every organic being that presents itself as a unified individual, but also of everything inorganic and formless, indeed of every artifact. All these reveal the Ideas through which the will objectivates itself at its lowest grades. They sound, as it were, nature's deepest, fading bass notes. Gravity, rigidity, fluidity, light, and so forth are the Ideas expressed in rocks, buildings, and bodies of water. Fine gardening and architecture can do no more than help these things display their properties distinctly, variously, and fully. They give the things an opportunity to express themselves purely and thereby invite and facilitate aesthetic contemplation.

Poor buildings and places neglected by nature or spoiled by art do little or none of this. Yet even from them those universal, fundamental Ideas of nature cannot wholly disappear. They speak to a viewer who seeks them out, and even poor buildings and the like remain capable of aesthetic contemplation. The Ideas of their material's most general properties are still discernible. What makes contemplation difficult is the form artificially imposed on them, which offers no help and is instead an obstacle.

Artifacts, then, also serve to express Ideas. What speaks through them, however, is not an Idea of the artifact but an Idea of the material to which the artificial form was given. The language of the Scholastics states this neatly in two terms: the Idea expressed in an artifact is that of its `forma substantialis` (substantial form), not of its `forma accidentalis` (accidental form). The latter points to no Idea, but only to the human concept from which it arose. By “artifact” here I expressly do not mean a work of visual art. The Scholastics did in fact mean by `forma substantialis` what I call the grade of the will's objectivation in a thing. We shall return shortly, in considering fine architecture, to the expression of the Idea of the material.

On our view, therefore, we cannot agree with Plato when he claims (*Republic*, X, pp. 284–285, and *Parmenides*, p. 79, Bipont edition) that a table and chair express the Ideas of table and chair. We say instead that they express the Ideas already present in their material as such. According to Aristotle (*Metaphysics*, XI, chapter 3), however, Plato himself posited Ideas only of natural beings: `ὁ Πλάτων ἔφη, ὅτι εἴδη ἐστὶν ὁπόσα φύσει` (`Plato dixit, quod ideae eorum sunt, quae natura sunt`—Plato said that there are Ideas of things that exist by nature). Chapter 5 likewise says that, according to the Platonists, there are no Ideas of a house or a ring. At any rate, Plato's immediate pupils denied that there were Ideas of artifacts, as Alcinous reports (*Introduction to Platonic Philosophy*, chapter 9):

> `Ὁρίζονται δὲ τὴν ἰδέαν παράδειγμα τῶν κατὰ φύσιν αἰώνιον. Οὔτε γὰρ τοῖς πλείστοις τῶν ἀπὸ Πλάτωνος ἀρέσκει τῶν τεχνικῶν εἶναι ἰδέας, οἷον ἀσπίδος ἢ λύρας, οὔτε μὴν τῶν παρὰ φύσιν, οἷον πυρετοῦ καὶ χολέρας, οὔτε τῶν κατὰ μέρος, οἷον Σωκράτους καὶ Πλάτωνος, ἀλλ’ οὐδὲ τῶν εὐτελῶν τινος, οἷον ῥύπου καὶ κάρφους, οὔτε τῶν πρός τι, οἷον μείζονος καὶ ὑπερέχοντος· εἶναι γὰρ τὰς ἰδέας νοήσεις θεοῦ αἰωνίους τε καὶ αὐτοτελεῖς.`
>
> `Definiunt autem ideam exemplar aeternum eorum, quae secundum naturam existunt. Nam plurimis ex iis, qui Platonem secuti sunt, minime placuit, arte factorum ideas esse, ut clypei atque lyrae; neque rursus eorum, quae praeter naturam, ut febris et cholerae; neque particularium, ceu Socratis et Platonis; neque etiam rerum vilium, veluti sordium et festucae; neque relationum, ut majoris et excedentis: esse namque ideas intellectiones dei aeternas, ac seipsis perfectas`.
>
> Translation: They define an Idea as the eternal model of things that exist according to nature. Most followers of Plato did not accept Ideas of things made by art, such as a shield or lyre; nor of things contrary to nature, such as fever and cholera; nor of individuals, such as Socrates and Plato; nor even of lowly things, such as dirt and a straw; nor of relations, such as being greater or exceeding. For Ideas are God's eternal thoughts, complete in themselves.

This occasion allows me to mention another point on which our doctrine of Ideas differs greatly from Plato's. He teaches (*Republic*, X, p. 288) that the object fine art seeks to represent, the model for painting and poetry, is not the Idea but the individual thing. Everything we have argued so far asserts the very opposite. Plato's opinion is all the less likely to unsettle us here because it gave rise to one of that great thinker's most serious and generally acknowledged errors: his low opinion and rejection of art, especially poetry. He attaches his mistaken judgment about it directly to the passage just cited.
