# §. 65.

All our reflections so far on human action have prepared the way and greatly eased our final task: to make the real ethical significance of action, which everyday life designates and understands through the words *good* and *evil*, abstractly and philosophically clear, and to show its place in our central thought.

First, however, I want to bring the concepts *good* and *evil* back to their actual meanings. Philosophical writers today treat them, strangely enough, as simple concepts incapable of analysis. Otherwise one might remain under the hazy illusion that these words contain more than they really do, and that they alone already say all we need. I am no more disposed to hide behind the word *good* in ethics than I was earlier to hide behind *beautiful* or *true*. Nor will I add the suffix “-ness,” which nowadays lends a special σεμνότης (solemn dignity) and supposedly helps in several cases, then put on a solemn face and pretend that uttering the three words amounts to more than naming three very broad, abstract, and therefore not very rich concepts of widely differing origins and meanings. Who, after encountering today's writings, has not finally grown sick of these words, fine things though they originally indicate, on seeing a thousand times how someone utterly incapable of thought thinks it enough to utter them with mouth agape and the look of an inspired sheep in order to have spoken great wisdom?

I have already explained the concept *true* in my essay on the principle of sufficient reason, chapter 5, §29 ff. The whole of our third book has for the first time provided the proper explanation of *beautiful*. We can now state the meaning of *good* briefly. The concept is essentially relative: it denotes the *suitability of an object to a particular striving of the will*. Thus whatever suits the will in any of its expressions and fulfils its purpose is thought *good*, however different such things may otherwise be. We speak of good food, good roads, good weather, good weapons, a good omen, and so forth. In short, we call good whatever is as we happen to want it. What is good for one person can therefore be precisely the opposite for another. Good falls into two kinds: satisfaction of the will that is immediately present, and satisfaction attained only indirectly in the future. These are the pleasant and the useful. So long as we are speaking of beings without cognition, the opposite is expressed by the German *schlecht* (bad), more rarely and abstractly by *Übel* (ill): whatever does not suit the will's particular striving.

Like all other beings that can stand in relation to the will, human beings favorable, helpful, or friendly to a purpose we happen to will have been called *good*, in the same sense and always with the same relativity. This appears in the saying, “He is good to me, but not to you.” People whose character generally leads them not to obstruct others' strivings but to further them—who are consistently helpful, benevolent, friendly, and beneficent—are called *good people* because of the relation of their actions to the wills of others. In German, and for about a century in French, the opposite for beings with cognition, animals and people, is expressed by a different word from that used for things without cognition: *böse*, *méchant* (evil, wicked). In almost all other languages, this distinction is absent: κακός, *malus*, *cattivo*, and *bad* are applied alike to people and inanimate things opposed to the purposes of a particular individual will.

Reflection began wholly from the passive side of good and could only later pass to the active. It then examined the conduct of a person called *good* in relation to that person, not merely to others. In particular it had to explain both the purely objective respect such conduct apparently produced in others and the distinctive satisfaction with himself it produced in the good person, who even purchased it at the cost of other sacrifices. It had likewise to explain, on the opposite side, the inward pain accompanying an evil disposition, however many external advantages the person harboring it might gain. Ethical systems arose from this inquiry, both philosophical systems and those founded on religious doctrines. Both always seek somehow to connect happiness with virtue. Philosophical systems do so either by the principle of contradiction or by the principle of sufficient reason, making happiness either identical with virtue or its consequence, always sophistically. Religious systems assert other worlds beyond any that experience can disclose.[^1] On our account, by contrast, virtue's inner nature will prove to be a striving in quite the opposite direction from the pursuit of happiness, that is, well-being and life.

It follows that *good* as a concept is τῶν πρός τι, something relative to another thing. Every good is essentially relative because its nature exists only in relation to a desiring will. *Absolute good* is therefore a contradiction. “Highest good,” *summum bonum*, means the same thing: a final satisfaction of the will after which no new willing arises, an ultimate motive whose attainment yields an indestructible contentment of the will. Our account so far in this fourth book shows that this cannot be conceived. The will can no more cease willing anew after any satisfaction than time can come to an end or have a beginning. No fulfilment can satisfy its striving completely and forever. It is the barrel of the Danaids. There is no highest or absolute good for it, only a temporary one.

If, however, one wants to give an old expression that habit prevents us from retiring altogether an honorary office, as to an *emeritus*, one may figuratively call the will's complete self-abolition and denial, true freedom from willing, the absolute good or *summum bonum*. This alone stills and appeases the urge of will forever; alone gives a contentment that cannot again be disturbed; alone redeems from the world. We shall soon discuss it at the close of our whole inquiry. It may be viewed as the only radical cure of the disease for which all other goods are mere palliatives, mere anodynes. In this sense the Greek τέλος (“end”), as well as *finis bonorum* (“end of goods”), fits the matter even better. So much for the words *good* and *evil*; now to the matter itself.

When someone is always inclined to *commit injustice* as soon as an opportunity arises and no external power prevents him, we call him *evil*. According to our explanation of injustice, this means he not only affirms the will to live appearing in his own body but carries that affirmation so far that he denies the will appearing in other individuals. He demands their powers in the service of his will and seeks to destroy their existence when they oppose its strivings. Its ultimate source is the high degree of egoism explained above. Two things are immediately clear. *First*, such a person expresses an extraordinarily vehement will to live reaching far beyond affirmation of his own body. *Second*, his cognition is wholly devoted to the principle of sufficient reason and captive to the *principium individuationis* (principle of individuation). He stands by the complete difference that the latter establishes between his person and all others. He therefore seeks only his own well-being and is wholly indifferent to theirs. Their nature seems utterly foreign to him, separated by a wide gulf from his own; indeed, he sees them only as masks without any reality. These two traits are the basic elements of an evil character.

That great vehemence of willing is itself and immediately a constant source of suffering. First, every act of willing as such springs from lack and thus from suffering. (Recall from the third book that the momentary silence of all willing when we surrender to aesthetic contemplation as a pure, will-free subject of cognition, the correlate of the Idea, is itself a chief component of our pleasure in the beautiful.) Second, because things are causally connected, most desires must remain unfulfilled; the will is thwarted much more often than satisfied. Much and vehement willing therefore always brings much and vehement suffering. For suffering is nothing other than unfulfilled and obstructed willing. Even bodily pain when the body is injured or destroyed is possible as pain only because the body is the will itself become object.

Because much and vehement suffering is inseparable from much and vehement willing, even the faces of very evil people bear the stamp of inner suffering. Even if they have gained every outward good fortune, they always look unhappy unless in a moment of jubilation or pretending otherwise. From this inner torment, immediately essential to them, finally arises even an unselfish pleasure in others' suffering, rather than pleasure proceeding merely from egoism. This is *malice* proper, rising as far as *cruelty*. For it another's suffering is no longer a means to the ends of one's own will but an end in itself. The closer explanation of this phenomenon is as follows.

As an appearance of the will illuminated by the clearest cognition, a human being constantly measures the actual satisfaction of his will that he feels against a merely possible satisfaction presented by cognition. From this springs envy. Every deprivation is infinitely aggravated by another's enjoyment and alleviated by the knowledge that others suffer the same deprivation. Evils shared by all and inseparable from human life trouble us little, as do those belonging to the climate or the whole country. Remembering greater sufferings than our own eases our pain; seeing others suffer relieves our own. Suppose, then, a person filled with an extraordinarily vehement urge of will wants greedily to take everything in order to quench egoism's thirst. He must nevertheless discover that all satisfaction is only apparent: attainment never delivers what desire promised, the final stilling of that fierce urge. Fulfilment merely changes a wish's shape, so that it torments him in another form. Once all recognized wishes are exhausted, the urge of will itself remains without a recognized motive, appearing as the feeling of an appalling desolation and emptiness with incurable torment. At ordinary degrees of willing, these experiences are felt more mildly and produce only the ordinary level of gloomy mood. But in a person whose will appears as exceptional wickedness, they necessarily produce excessive inner torment, perpetual restlessness, and incurable pain. Unable to find relief directly, he seeks it indirectly by seeing others suffer, recognizing their suffering at the same time as an expression of his power. Their suffering becomes an end in itself, a sight in which he delights. Thus arises cruelty proper, bloodthirstiness, which history so often displays in Neros and Domitians, the African deys, Robespierre, and others.

The desire for revenge is already akin to malice. It repays evil with evil not with an eye to the future, as punishment does, but solely on account of what has happened in the past. It is thus disinterested, an end rather than a means, and delights in the offender's torment at one's own hand. What distinguishes revenge from pure malice and excuses it to some degree is an appearance of right. The very act that is now revenge would be punishment, and therefore right, if imposed under a law—according to a rule set in advance and known—in an association that has sanctioned it.

Alongside the suffering described above, which springs from the same root as malice—the very vehement will—and is therefore inseparable from it, there is another, quite different and distinctive torment. It is felt with every evil act, whether simple injustice arising from egoism or pure malice, and is called a *pang of conscience* or, when prolonged, *anguish of conscience*. Anyone keeping in mind what has been said in this fourth book, especially the truth explained at its beginning that life itself is always assured to the will to live as its mere image or mirror, and our account of eternal justice, will find that on those premises a pang of conscience can mean only what follows. Its content, abstractly expressed, has two parts, though they coincide completely and must be thought wholly together.

However thickly Maya's veil covers the evil person's understanding, however firmly he remains captive to the *principium individuationis*, he regards himself as absolutely different from every other person and separated from them by a wide gulf. He clings to this cognition with all his strength, since it alone suits and supports his egoism, and cognition is almost always bribed by the will. Yet deep within his consciousness a secret foreboding stirs that this order of things is only appearance and that matters are wholly otherwise in themselves. Time and space separate him from other individuals and their innumerable torments, including those they suffer at his hands, and present them as entirely foreign to him. Nevertheless, apart from representation and its forms, the one will to live appears in them all. Here it fails to recognize itself and turns its weapons against itself. In seeking enhanced well-being in one appearance, it lays the greatest suffering on another. He, the evil person, is this entire will. Thus he is not only the tormentor but also the tormented, from whose suffering he is separated and protected only by a deceptive dream shaped by space and time. That dream will vanish. In truth, he must pay for pleasure with torment; every suffering he recognizes as merely possible actually strikes him as the will to live. Possibility and actuality, nearness and distance in time and space, differ only for the individual's cognition through the *principium individuationis*, not in themselves. The transmigration of souls expresses this truth mythically, adapting it to the principle of sufficient reason and translating it into the form of appearance. Its purest expression, unmixed with anything else, is the dimly felt but desolate torment called anguish of conscience.

That anguish also arises from a *second* immediate cognition closely connected with the first: cognition of the strength with which the will to live affirms itself in the evil individual, extending far beyond his own individual appearance to complete denial of the same will appearing in others. The evil person's inward horror at his own deed, which he tries to hide even from himself, contains a foreboding that the *principium individuationis* and the difference it creates between him and others are void and merely apparent. It also contains knowledge of his own will's vehemence, the force with which he has seized and fastened himself to life. The dreadful side of that very life confronts him in the torment of those he has oppressed. Yet he is so firmly bound to it that precisely for the fuller affirmation of his own will he himself becomes the source of the most dreadful things. He recognizes himself as a concentrated appearance of the will to live. He feels how completely he has fallen prey to life, and with it to the countless sufferings essential to it. Life has endless time and space in which to remove the difference between possibility and actuality and turn all the torments he now merely *knows* into those he *feels*.

The millions of years of constant rebirth exist here only as a concept, just as the whole past and future exist only in concepts. Filled time, the form of the will's appearance, is only the present. Time is always new for the individual, who always finds himself newly arisen. Life is inseparable from the will to live, and its sole form is the now. Death—pardon the repeated image—is like the sun's setting. The sun is only apparently swallowed by night; in reality it burns without interruption as the source of all light, bringing new days to new worlds, always rising and always setting. Beginning and end pertain only to the individual through time, the form of his appearance as representation. Outside time are only the will, Kant's thing in itself, and its adequate objectivity, Plato's Idea. Suicide therefore offers no rescue: what each person *wills* in his innermost being, he must *be*; and what each person *is*, he *wills*.

So it is not merely his felt knowledge that the forms of representation separating individuals are apparent and void, but also knowledge of his own will and its degree, that gives conscience its sting. A life's course produces the image of the empirical character, whose original is the intelligible character. The evil person is terrified by that image, whether it has been drawn in large strokes so that the world shares his horror or in strokes so small that he alone sees it. Only he is directly concerned. The past, as mere appearance, would be indifferent and could not trouble conscience if character did not feel itself free from all time and unchangeable by time, so long as it does not deny itself. That is why deeds done long ago still weigh on conscience. The prayer “Lead me not into temptation” means “Do not let me see who I am.”

The evil person measures, by the force with which he affirms life as shown in the suffering he inflicts on others, how far he is from surrendering and denying that will, the only possible redemption from the world and its torment. He sees how completely he belongs to the world and how firmly he is bound to it. The *known* suffering of others could not move him; he falls prey to life and to *felt* suffering. Whether the latter will ever break and overcome the force of his will remains open.

This account of the meaning and inner nature of *evil* is the content of *anguish of conscience* as mere feeling, *not* as clear, abstract cognition. It will become clearer and more complete through an equally thorough consideration of the *good* as a property of the human will and, finally, of complete resignation and holiness, which emerge when the good reaches its highest degree. Opposites always illuminate one another; as Spinoza said so well, day reveals both itself and night.

[^1]: Let me note in passing that what gives every positive religious doctrine its great power, the hold by which it takes firm possession of minds, is its ethical side. Yet this acts not immediately as such but because it is firmly linked and woven together with the mythical dogma peculiar to that religion and seems explicable only by that dogma. The ethical significance of actions cannot be explained by the principle of sufficient reason, while every myth follows that principle. Nevertheless, believers regard the ethical significance of action and their myth as utterly inseparable, even identical, and therefore regard every attack on the myth as an attack on right and virtue. Among monotheistic peoples this has gone so far that atheism or godlessness has become synonymous with the absence of all morality. Priests welcome these confusions of concepts. Only through them could that terrible monster, fanaticism, arise and rule not merely certain exceptionally perverse and evil individuals but whole peoples. Finally, to humanity's honor, only once in its history did it take bodily form in this West as the Inquisition. According to the latest, at last authentic reports, in Madrid alone—and there were many other such clerical murder pits in Spain—it caused 300,000 people over three centuries to die painfully at the stake over matters of faith. Every zealot should be reminded of this as soon as he raises his voice.
