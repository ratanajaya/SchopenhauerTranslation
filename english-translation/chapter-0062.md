# §. 60.

We have completed two discussions that had to intervene: freedom of the will in itself alongside the necessity of its appearance, and the will's lot in the world that mirrors its essence, knowledge of which leads it to affirm or deny itself. We can now bring affirmation and denial themselves, earlier stated and explained only in general terms, into sharper focus by presenting the modes of action in which alone they find expression and considering their inner meaning.

*Affirmation of the will* is simply willing that persists undisturbed by any cognition, as it generally fills human life. The human body is already the will's objecthood as it appears at this grade and in this individual. Willing as it unfolds in time is, so to speak, a paraphrase of the body, an explanation of the meaning of the whole and its parts. It is another presentation of the same thing in itself of which the body is also an appearance. Hence we can say “affirmation of the body” in place of “affirmation of the will.”

The basic theme of all the varied acts of will is satisfaction of needs inseparable from the healthy body's existence. They are already expressed in it and can be traced to preservation of the individual and propagation of the species. Indirectly, however, these give a vast variety of motives power over the will, producing a vast variety of acts. Each is only a sample or instance of the will appearing here in general. What kind of instance it is, what shape the motive has and gives it, is inessential. What matters is that willing occurs at all and how vehement it is. The will can become visible only through motives, as the eye can manifest its power of sight only in light. Motive in general stands before the will like a Proteus of many shapes. It always promises complete satisfaction, the quenching of the will's thirst. As soon as it is attained, it appears again in another form and moves the will anew, according to the will's degree of vehemence and its relation to cognition. These instances and examples disclose the empirical character.

From the moment consciousness begins, a person finds himself willing. His cognition generally remains in constant relation to his will. He first seeks complete knowledge of the objects of his willing, then of the means to them. Now he knows what to do and usually seeks no other knowledge. He acts and presses ahead, sustained and kept active by consciousness of continually working toward his desired goal. His thinking concerns the choice of means. Such is the life of almost everyone. They will, know what they will, and strive for it, with enough success to protect them from despair and enough failure to protect them from boredom and its consequences. This produces a certain cheerfulness, or at least composure, which wealth and poverty do not fundamentally change. Rich and poor alike enjoy not what they have—for, as shown, its effect is only negative—but what they hope to gain through their activity. They press on with great seriousness and an air of importance, just as children do in play.

It is always an exception when such a life course is interrupted by cognition independent of service to the will and directed toward the world's essence as a whole. This may bring the aesthetic call to contemplation or the ethical call to renunciation. Need drives most people through life without allowing them to reflect. Often, by contrast, the will flares up to a degree far beyond affirmation of one's own body, manifesting itself in violent emotions and powerful passions. The individual then not only affirms his own existence but denies and seeks to cancel that of others when it stands in his way.

Maintaining the body through its own powers is such a low degree of the will's affirmation that, if affirmation voluntarily went no further, we could suppose the will appearing in that body ended when it died. Satisfaction of the sexual drive already goes beyond affirming one's own existence, which fills only a short time. It affirms life beyond the individual's death and into an indefinite future. Nature, always true and consistent and here even candid, openly presents the inner meaning of procreation. Our consciousness and the vehemence of the drive teach us that this act expresses the most decisive *affirmation of the will to live*, purely and without any further element, such as denying other individuals. In time and in the causal series—in nature—a new life appears as the act's consequence. The offspring stands before the parent, different in appearance but identical with the parent in itself, or according to the Idea. Through this act the generations of living beings are connected into a whole and perpetuate themselves as such.

For the parent, procreation is only the expression or symptom of his decisive affirmation of the will to live. For the offspring, it is not the ground of the will appearing in that offspring, since the will in itself knows neither ground nor consequent. Like every cause, it is merely the occasional cause of this will's appearing at this time and place. As thing in itself, the will of parent and offspring is not different; only appearance, not the thing in itself, is subject to the *principium individuationis* (principle of individuation). By affirming beyond one's own body as far as a new body, one also affirms anew suffering and death as belonging to life's appearance. The possibility of redemption opened by the highest cognitive capacity is declared fruitless for this occasion. Here lies the deep source of shame about the act of procreation.

Christian doctrine presents this view mythically in its dogma that we all share in Adam's fall, which plainly means the satisfaction of sexual desire, and through it are liable to suffering and death. Here the doctrine goes beyond consideration according to the principle of sufficient reason and recognizes the Idea of humanity. Generation, the bond joining all individuals, restores the Idea's unity from its fragmentation into countless individuals. Accordingly it regards each individual, on one side, as identical with Adam, representative of life's affirmation, and thus fallen into sin—original sin—suffering, and death. On the other side, its cognition of the Idea shows each individual as identical with the Redeemer, representative of denial of the will to live, and thus participating in his self-sacrifice, redeemed by his merit, and saved from the bonds of sin and death—that is, the world (Romans 5:12–21).

The Greek story of Persephone presents another mythic image of our view. Her return from the underworld was still possible until she tasted its fruit, but eating the pomegranate surrendered her to it completely. It shows sexual satisfaction as affirming the will to live beyond the individual's life: only through it does one fall wholly under life's power, as though signing oneself over anew. This meaning emerges especially clearly in Goethe's incomparable treatment of the myth, when the invisible chorus of the Fates suddenly breaks in just after she eats the pomegranate:

> “You are ours!<br>
> You should have returned without eating;<br>
> One bite of the apple makes you ours!”

It is noteworthy that *Clement of Alexandria* (*Stromata*, III, chapter 15) uses the same image and wording: `Οἱ μὲν εὐνουχίσαντες ἑαυτοὺς ἀπὸ πάσης ἁμαρτίας, διὰ τὴν βασιλείαν τῶν οὐρανῶν, μακάριοι οὗτοί εἰσιν, οἱ τοῦ κόσμου νηστεύοντες` (`Qui se castrarunt ad omni peccato, propter regnum coelorum, ii sunt beati, a *mundo jejunantes*`—Blessed are those who have made themselves eunuchs from all sin for the kingdom of heaven, fasting from the world).

The sexual drive also proves itself life's strongest and most decisive affirmation because for the natural human being, as for an animal, it is life's final purpose and highest goal. Self-preservation is the first pursuit. Once that is secured, one seeks only to propagate the species. As a merely natural being, one can strive for nothing further. Nature too, whose inner essence is the will to live itself, urges human beings and animals toward reproduction with all her force. Afterward her purpose in the individual is achieved and she is quite indifferent to its destruction. As the will to live, she cares only to preserve the species; the individual is nothing to her.

Because the inner essence of nature, the will to live, speaks most powerfully in the sexual drive, the ancient poets and philosophers Hesiod and Parmenides said, with deep significance, that *Eros* was the first being, the creator and principle from which all things arose (see Aristotle, *Metaphysics*, I, 4). Pherecydes said: `Εἰς ἔρωτα μεταβεβλῆσθαι τὸν Δία, μέλλοντα δημιουργεῖν` (`Jovem, cum mundum fabricare vellet, in cupidinem sese transformasse`—When Zeus intended to create the world, he transformed himself into desire; Proclus on Plato's *Timaeus*, III). G. F. *Schoemann* has recently treated this subject at length in *De cupidine cosmogonico* (On Cosmogonic Desire), 1852. The Indians' Maya, whose work and web are the whole world of illusion, is likewise paraphrased by `amor` (love).

More than any other external part of the body, the genitals are subject to the will alone and not at all to cognition. Indeed, the will shows itself here almost as independent of cognition as it does in the organs that serve vegetative life and reproduction in response to mere stimuli. In them it acts blindly, as in nature without cognition. Procreation is simply reproduction transferred to a new individual: reproduction raised to a second power, just as death is excretion raised to a second power. The genitals are therefore the true *focus* of the will and thus the opposite pole from the brain, representative of cognition, the other side of the world as representation. They are the life-preserving principle that guarantees life without end in time. In this capacity they were revered as the phallus among the Greeks and the lingam among Hindus, symbols of the will's affirmation. Cognition, by contrast, makes possible the cancellation of willing, redemption through freedom, and the overcoming and annihilation of the world.

At the beginning of Book Four we considered at length how the will to live, in its affirmation, should view death. Death does not touch it, for it belongs to life itself, and its opposite, generation, balances it completely, securing and guaranteeing life for the will to live through all time despite the individual's death. The Indians expressed this by giving the lingam to Shiva, god of death, as an attribute. We also showed how someone standing with full reflection at the standpoint of decisive affirmation of life faces death without fear. Nothing more needs to be said of it here. Most people stand at this standpoint without clear reflection and continually affirm life. The world mirrors this affirmation in countless individuals, endless time and space, and endless suffering, between generation and death without end.

Yet no complaint can be raised about this from any side. The will stages the great tragedy and comedy at its own expense and is itself the spectator. The world is as it is because the will whose appearance it is is as it is, because it wills so. Suffering is justified in that the will affirms itself even in this appearance; that affirmation is justified and balanced because the will bears the suffering. Here we already glimpse *eternal justice* in the whole. Later we shall recognize it more closely and clearly in individual cases. First, however, we must discuss temporal or human justice.[^1]
