# §. 49.

All our reflections on art so far rest on one truth: the object of art, which the artist aims to present and whose cognition must therefore precede the work as its seed and origin, is an *Idea* in Plato's sense, and nothing else. It is not the individual thing, the object of ordinary apprehension, nor is it the concept, the object of rational thought and science. Idea and concept do have something in common: each, as a unity, stands for a multitude of actual things. Yet their great difference should be sufficiently clear from what was said about the concept in Book One and the Idea in this book. I do not claim that Plato himself always grasped the distinction purely; many of his examples of Ideas and discussions of them apply only to concepts. We can leave that aside and follow our own path, glad whenever we tread the track of a great and noble mind, but pursuing our own goal rather than his footsteps.

The *concept* is abstract and discursive, wholly indeterminate within its sphere and determined only at its boundary. Anyone with reason can reach and grasp it. It can be communicated directly in words and exhausted by a definition. The *Idea*, by contrast—perhaps definable as the concept's adequate representative—is intuitive throughout. Although it stands for an infinite number of individual things, it is fully determinate. An individual *as such* never cognizes it. It is accessible only to someone who has risen above all willing and individuality to become the pure subject of cognition: to genius, then, and to someone raised into a state of genius by an enhancement of pure cognitive power, usually occasioned by a work of genius.

The Idea can therefore be communicated only conditionally, not unconditionally. An Idea apprehended and repeated in an artwork speaks to each person only in proportion to that person's intellectual worth. For this reason the finest works of every art, the noblest products of genius, remain closed books to the dull majority of people, inaccessible across a wide gulf, just as the company of princes is inaccessible to the common crowd. Even the shallowest people accept reputedly great works on authority, so as not to betray their own weakness. Yet in private they are always ready to condemn them once they think they can do so without exposing themselves. Then their long-suppressed hatred of everything great and beautiful—things that never spoke to them and thereby humiliated them—and of their creators breaks out with pleasure.

In general, one must have worth of one's own to acknowledge another's worth freely and willingly. This is the basis of the need for modesty in anyone with merit, and of the disproportionately loud praise given to this virtue. Alone among its sister virtues, modesty is always added to any praise of a distinguished person, in order to appease and still the anger of those without worth. What is modesty, after all, but feigned humility by which one begs those who have no merit to forgive one's qualities and achievements in a world teeming with base envy? Someone who claims no merit because he really has none is not modest, but merely honest.

The *Idea* is a unity broken into multiplicity by the temporal and spatial forms of our intuitive apprehension. The *concept*, conversely, is a unity restored from multiplicity by the abstraction of our reason. The concept can be called `unitas post rem` (unity after the thing), the Idea `unitas ante rem` (unity before the thing). We can express the difference through a comparison. The *concept* is like a dead container: what one has put into it lies there side by side, and one can take out through analytic judgments no more than one put in through synthetic reflection. The *Idea*, by contrast, develops representations in those who have grasped it that are new in relation to the concept bearing the same name. It is like a living, developing organism with the power of generation, producing what was not packed away inside it.

It follows that the concept, however useful in life and however serviceable, necessary, and fruitful for science, is forever barren in art. The apprehended Idea is the true and sole source of every genuine work of art. In its vigorous originality it can be drawn only from life itself, from nature and the world, and only by genuine genius or by someone inspired for the moment to a state of genius. Genuine works, bearing immortal life within them, arise only from such immediate conception. Precisely because the Idea is and remains intuitive, the artist is not conscious of the work's intention and goal *in abstracto* (in the abstract). What hovers before the artist is an Idea, not a concept. Hence artists cannot give an account of what they do; they work, as people say, from mere feeling, unconsciously, even instinctively.

Imitators and mannerists, by contrast—`imitatores, servum pecus` (imitators, a servile herd)—begin in art with the concept. They notice what pleases and has an effect in genuine works, make it explicit, grasp it abstractly as a concept, and then imitate it, openly or covertly, with calculated intent. Like parasitic plants, they draw their nourishment from others' works; like polyps, they take on the colour of their food. To extend the comparison, they are like machines that chop and mix whatever is fed into them very finely but can never digest it. The foreign ingredients can always be recovered and separated from the mixture. Genius alone resembles the organic body that assimilates, transforms, and produces. Genius is indeed educated and formed by predecessors and their works, but is fertilized only by life and the world themselves, directly through intuitive impressions. Even the highest education therefore never harms originality.

All imitators and mannerists grasp the essence of exemplary works by others in concepts; concepts, however, can never give a work inner life. An age—that is, the dull multitude in each period—knows only concepts and clings to them. It therefore greets mannered works with quick, loud applause. Within a few years these same works become intolerable, because the spirit of the age—the prevailing concepts in which alone they were rooted—has changed. Only genuine works, drawn directly from nature and life, remain, like them, forever young and always vigorous. They belong to no age, but to humanity. For that very reason their own age, to which they disdained to adapt, received them coolly. They indirectly and silently exposed its passing errors and were therefore recognized late and reluctantly. Yet they cannot grow old. Even in the most distant future they speak freshly and anew.

Nor do they remain liable to being overlooked or misunderstood. They stand crowned and ratified by the approval of the few capable judges who appear singly and sparsely over the centuries,[^1] recording their judgments. The slowly growing sum of these judgments establishes the authority of the only tribunal meant when one appeals to posterity. These successive individuals alone constitute it. The mass of posterity will always remain as misguided and dull as the mass of one's contemporaries has always been. Read the complaints great minds have made about their contemporaries in every century: they always sound as if written today, because the human race remains the same. In every age and every art, manner takes the place of spirit, which is always the possession of a few individuals. Manner, though, is the old, cast-off garment of the most recent recognized manifestation of spirit. Accordingly, as a rule, the approval of posterity is won only at the cost of contemporary approval, and vice versa.[^2]

[^1]: `Apparent rari, nantes in gurgite vasto` (A few appear, swimming in the vast whirlpool).
[^2]: See chapter 34 of the second volume.
