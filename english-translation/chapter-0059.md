# §. 57.

At every grade illuminated by cognition, the will appears to itself as an individual. The human individual finds himself finite in infinite space and time, a magnitude vanishing beside them, cast into them. Because they have no bounds, the *when* and *where* of his existence are always relative, never absolute: his place and duration are finite portions of the infinite and boundless. His real existence is only in the present. Its unimpeded flight into the past is a constant passage into death, a continual dying. His past life, apart from its possible consequences for the present and the evidence of his will imprinted in it, is already entirely done with, dead, and nothing. It should therefore be rationally indifferent to him whether that past contained torments or pleasures. Yet the present continually becomes past in his hands, while the future is wholly uncertain and always short. Viewed from its formal side alone, his existence is a ceaseless fall of the present into the dead past, a continual dying.

Viewed physically, too, just as walking is a fall constantly interrupted, the life of our body is a dying constantly interrupted, death repeatedly postponed. Even the activity of our mind is boredom constantly deferred. Every breath wards off death as it continually presses upon us. We fight it every second and at longer intervals with every meal, sleep, warming, and so forth. It must win at last, for we were given over to it at birth; it only plays with its prey for a while before devouring it. Meanwhile we prolong our lives with great concern and much care for as long as possible, like blowing a soap bubble as large and lasting as we can, though we know with certainty that it will burst.

Even in nature without cognition we saw its inner essence as constant striving without goal or rest. This becomes clearer still in animals and human beings. Willing and striving are their entire essence, exactly like an unquenchable thirst. The basis of all willing is need, lack, and therefore pain, to which a being is subject originally and by its very nature. If, instead, it lacks objects of willing because they are too readily satisfied and taken away again, dreadful emptiness and boredom seize it: its own being and existence become an intolerable burden. Life therefore swings like a pendulum between pain and boredom, which are indeed its ultimate constituents. A curious expression of this truth is that, after human beings placed all sufferings and torments in hell, nothing was left for heaven but boredom.

The ceaseless striving that constitutes every appearance of the will acquires its first and most general basis at the higher grades of objectivation when the will appears to itself as a living body under the iron command to nourish it. The command has its force precisely because that body is nothing other than the objectivated will to live itself. As the most complete objectivation of this will, the human being is also the neediest of all beings. He is willing and needing through and through, a concentration of a thousand needs. He stands on earth with these needs, left to himself, uncertain of everything except his need and distress. Caring for his existence under such heavy demands, renewed every day, generally fills his whole life. A second demand immediately follows it: propagation of the species. At the same time dangers of every kind threaten him on all sides, and evading them requires constant vigilance. He proceeds with careful step and anxious glance, for a thousand accidents and a thousand enemies lie in wait. So he went in the wilderness, and so he goes in civilized life. There is no security for him:

> `Qualibus in tenebris vitae, quantisque periclis`<br>
> `Degitur hocc' aevi, quodcunque est!`<br>
> `Lucr., II, 15.`
>
> Translation: In what darkness of life and amid what great dangers is this span of life, whatever it may be, spent! Lucretius, II, 15.

Most lives are only a constant struggle for existence itself, with the certainty of losing it at last. What keeps people going through this arduous struggle is less love of life than fear of death, which stands inevitably in the background and can approach at any moment. Life itself is a sea full of reefs and whirlpools, which a person avoids with the greatest care, though he knows that even if every effort and skill enables him to thread his way through them, each step brings him closer to the greatest shipwreck: total, unavoidable, incurable death. Indeed, he steers straight toward it. Death is the final goal of the arduous voyage and worse for him than all the reefs he avoided.

It is striking, however, that life's sufferings and torments can grow so severe that death, fleeing from which occupies the whole of life, becomes desirable and one rushes toward it voluntarily. Conversely, as soon as need and suffering give a person respite, boredom is so near that diversion becomes necessary. The striving for existence keeps every living being occupied and moving. Once existence is secure, however, they do not know what to do with it. They are then moved to get rid of the burden of existence, to make it unfelt, to “kill time”—that is, to escape boredom.

Thus almost everyone sheltered from need and care, having finally cast off every other burden, becomes a burden to himself. Every hour passed is then counted as a gain: a subtraction from the very life he previously spent all his powers trying to preserve as long as possible. Boredom is far from a slight evil; it eventually paints genuine despair on the face. It drives beings who love one another as little as human beings do to seek one another out, and so becomes a source of sociability. Public precautions are taken against it everywhere, as against other widespread calamities, if only for reasons of state. Like its opposite extreme, famine, it can drive people to the greatest excesses. The people need `panem et Circenses` (bread and circuses). The strict Philadelphia penitentiary system makes boredom alone, through solitude and inactivity, an instrument of punishment. It is so terrible that it has already driven prisoners to suicide. As need is the people's constant scourge, boredom is the scourge of the upper class. In middle-class life Sunday represents boredom and the six weekdays represent need.

Every human life flows between wanting and attaining. Desire by its nature is pain; attainment quickly breeds satiety. The goal was only apparent, and possession takes away its charm. Desire and need appear again in a new form. If they do not, desolation, emptiness, and boredom follow, and the struggle against them is as tormenting as the struggle against need. Desire and satisfaction following one another at intervals neither too short nor too long reduce the suffering each brings to its smallest measure and make the happiest possible life course.

What one might otherwise call the most beautiful part and purest joys of life deserve those names precisely because they raise us out of actual existence and turn us into spectators without personal stake: pure cognition, foreign to all willing, enjoyment of beauty, and genuine pleasure in art. Yet these require rare aptitudes and are granted to very few people, and even to them only as a passing dream. Moreover, greater intellectual power makes these few receptive to much greater suffering than less perceptive people could ever feel and leaves them alone among beings markedly unlike themselves. This balances the account. Purely intellectual pleasures are inaccessible to the vast majority; they are almost wholly incapable of the joy of pure cognition and remain entirely dependent on willing.

If anything is to engage them, to be *interesting* to them, it must stir their *will* in some way, even by a remote and merely possible relation to it; the very meaning of “interest” implies this. The will can never be wholly absent, since their existence lies far more in willing than knowing. Action and reaction are their only element. This trait shows itself naively in small, everyday matters. At sights they visit, for example, they write their names, reacting upon a place that did not act upon them. They find it hard merely to observe a strange, rare animal; they must provoke, tease, or play with it to feel action and reaction. The need for stimulation of the will appears especially in the invention and persistence of card-playing, a precise expression of humanity's pitiable side.

Whatever nature or fortune may have done, whoever one may be and whatever one may possess, the pain essential to life cannot be cast off:

> `Πηλεΐδης δ’ ᾤμωξεν, ἰδὼν εἰς οὐρανὸν εὐρύν.`<br>
> (`Pelides autem ejulavit, intuitus in coelum latum.`)
>
> Translation: The son of Peleus cried out, looking into the wide sky.

And again:

> `Ζηνὸς μὲν πάϊς ἦα Κρονίονος, αὐτὰρ ὀιζὺν Εἶχον ἀπειρεσίην.`<br>
> (`Jovis quidem filius eram Saturnii; verum aerumnam Habebam infinitam.`)
>
> Translation: I was the son of Zeus, son of Cronus, yet my suffering was without end.

Ceaseless efforts to banish suffering accomplish only a change of its form. Originally it is want, need, and anxiety about preserving life. Should one succeed, with great difficulty, in displacing pain in this form, it immediately returns in a thousand others, varying with age and circumstances: sexual drive, passionate love, jealousy, envy, hatred, fear, ambition, greed, illness, and so on. If it can find entry in no other form, it arrives in the sad grey clothing of tedium and boredom, against which various remedies are tried. If one succeeds in driving boredom away, one will hardly do so without readmitting pain in one of its earlier forms, beginning the dance again. Every human life is tossed between pain and boredom.

This is a disheartening reflection, but I should draw attention to a side of it from which one can draw comfort and perhaps even Stoic indifference to one's present trouble. Much of our impatience with suffering arises because we regard it as accidental, produced by a chain of causes that could easily have been different. We do not usually grieve over evils that are immediately necessary and entirely general, such as old age, death, and many daily inconveniences. What gives a particular suffering its sting is thinking about the chance circumstances that brought it upon us.

Suppose we recognize that pain as such is essential and unavoidable in life, and only the particular form it takes depends on chance. Our present suffering occupies a place that another would immediately fill if this one were absent; the present suffering merely excludes that other one. Fate can thus do little to us in what is essential. If this reflection became a living conviction, it might bring considerable Stoic calm and greatly reduce anxious concern for our welfare. In fact, such powerful rule of reason over directly felt suffering may rarely, if ever, occur.

Reflection on the inevitability of pain, on one suffering driving out another and a new one being summoned when an earlier one departs, might even lead to a paradoxical but not absurd hypothesis. Perhaps each individual has by nature a fixed measure of essential pain that cannot remain empty or be overfilled, however its form changes. On this view suffering and well-being are determined not by external things but by this measure, this disposition. Physical condition may increase or decrease it somewhat at different times, yet overall it remains the same. It is what we call temperament or, more exactly, the degree to which someone is, as Plato says in Book One of the *Republic*, `εὔκολος` or `δύσκολος` (of an easy or difficult disposition).

Familiar experience supports this hypothesis: great suffering makes every lesser trouble unfelt, whereas in its absence even minor inconveniences torment and upset us. Experience also shows that when a great misfortune actually arrives, one we shuddered even to imagine, our overall mood remains fairly much as before once we have endured the first pain. Conversely, attaining a happiness long desired does not make us noticeably better or more comfortable overall and for long. Only the moment of such a change moves us unusually strongly, with deep grief or loud jubilation. Both soon fade, because they rested on illusion. They arise not from present pleasure or pain itself but from the opening of a new future anticipated within it. Only by borrowing pain or joy from the future could they become so abnormally intense; that is why they cannot last.

On the hypothesis that a large part of suffering and well-being, like cognition, is determined subjectively and *a priori* (prior to experience), further evidence can be offered. Cheerfulness and gloom plainly do not depend on outer circumstances, wealth, or rank: we find at least as many cheerful faces among the poor as among the rich. The motives for suicide vary so greatly that we can name no misfortune sure to cause it with any high probability in people of every character, and few so small that equally small ones have not already caused it. Our degree of cheerfulness or sadness does not remain the same at every time. On this view we would attribute its variations not to changing external circumstances but to changing inner states and physical condition. A real, though temporary, increase in cheerfulness, even to joy, usually appears without any external cause.

We often see our pain arise from a definite external relation and feel visibly pressed and saddened only by it. We then believe that removing it would bring the greatest contentment. This is an illusion. On our hypothesis each person's overall measure of suffering and well-being is subjectively fixed at any given time. The external cause of sadness acts on that measure as a blistering plaster does on the body, drawing together harmful fluids otherwise dispersed. The pain rooted in our being during this period, which we cannot cast off, would be spread among a hundred points without that specific external cause. It would appear as a hundred small annoyances and anxious fancies about things we now overlook because the chief trouble has filled our capacity for pain and concentrated on one point the suffering otherwise scattered.

This fits another observation. When an oppressive major worry is finally lifted by a happy outcome, another immediately takes its place. All its material was there before, but it could not enter consciousness as a worry while there was no room for it. It stood unnoticed as a dim mist at consciousness's furthest horizon. Now that room has opened, the ready material advances and takes the throne of the day's ruling concern—`πρυτανεύουσα` (presiding). Though the material of the new worry may be much lighter than the old, it swells to an apparently equal size and completely fills that throne.

Excessive joy and very violent pain occur only in the same person. Each conditions the other, and both require great liveliness of mind. As we have just seen, neither is produced by what is purely present; both arise from anticipation of the future. Pain is essential to life, and its degree is also determined by the subject's nature. Sudden changes, being external, cannot really change that degree. Extreme jubilation or anguish therefore always rests on an error or delusion. Insight could prevent both these excesses of mind.

Every excessive jubilation—`exultatio, insolens laetitia` (exultation, immoderate joy)—rests on the illusion that one has found in life what cannot be found there: lasting satisfaction of desires and cares that torment us and continually renew themselves. We must inevitably be disabused of each such illusion and pay for its departure with pain as bitter as the joy its arrival brought. It is like a height from which we can descend only by falling; we should therefore avoid it. Sudden excessive pain is just the fall from such a height, the disappearance of an illusion, and depends on it. If we could bring ourselves always to view things clearly as a whole and in their connections, and steadfastly avoid lending them the colour we wish them to have, we could prevent both extremes. Stoic ethics chiefly sought to free the mind from such illusions and their consequences and give it unshakable equanimity instead. Horace is filled with this insight in the familiar ode:

> `Aequam memento rebus in arduis`<br>
> `Servare mentem, non secus in bonis`<br>
> `Ab insolenti temperatam`<br>
> `Laetitia.`
>
> Translation: Remember to keep an even mind in adversity, and in prosperity too to restrain it from extravagant joy.

Most often, however, we shut ourselves off from the bitter medicine of recognizing that suffering is essential to life. It does not flow into us from outside: everyone carries its inexhaustible source within. We instead continually seek some particular external cause for the pain that never leaves us, as if seeking a pretext. A free person fashions an idol to give himself a master. We tirelessly move from wish to wish. However much each attained satisfaction promised, it fails to satisfy and soon appears as a humiliating mistake. Yet we do not see that we are drawing water with the Danaids' bottomless vessel. We hurry on to new wishes:

> `Sed, dum abest quod avemus, id exsuperare videtur`<br>
> `Caetera; post aliquid, quum contigit illud, avemus;`<br>
> `Et sitis aequa tenet vitai semper hiantes. (Lucr. III, 1095.)`
>
> Translation: While what we desire is absent, it seems to surpass everything else; when we have obtained it, we desire something further, and the same thirst always holds us with mouths agape. Lucretius, III, 1095.

This continues without end. Or, more rarely and requiring some strength of character, we meet a wish that cannot be fulfilled yet cannot be abandoned. Then we have, as it were, what we sought: something we can blame at every moment as the source of our suffering instead of blaming our own being. We become estranged from our fate but reconciled to our existence, as the knowledge recedes that suffering is essential to existence itself and true satisfaction impossible. This latter course produces a somewhat melancholy mood: one continually bears a single great pain and thereby comes to regard lesser pains and pleasures with indifference. It is a more dignified appearance than the much more common endless chase after one deceptive image after another.
