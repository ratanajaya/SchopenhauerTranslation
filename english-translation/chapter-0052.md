# §. 50.

The purpose of all art is to communicate an apprehended Idea. Through its passage through the artist's mind, where it appears isolated and cleared of everything foreign to it, the Idea becomes comprehensible even to someone less receptive and without creative power. If beginning with a concept is objectionable in art, we cannot approve of an artwork deliberately and avowedly designed to express a concept. That is what happens in *allegory*.

An allegory is an artwork that means something other than what it depicts. Yet what is intuitive, and thus the Idea, speaks for itself directly and completely. It does not need to be indicated by the mediation of something else. What is indicated and represented by something entirely different because it cannot itself be brought before intuition is always a concept. An allegory therefore always signifies a concept. It guides the viewer's mind away from the intuitive representation depicted toward an entirely different representation, abstract and non-intuitive, lying wholly outside the artwork. The painting or statue is thus supposed to do what writing does much more perfectly.

Here the presentation of the Idea, which can be apprehended only intuitively and which we have declared the purpose of art, is not the aim. Nor does the intended aim require any great perfection in the artwork. It is enough to recognize what the thing is supposed to be. Once that is known, the aim is achieved and the mind is directed to an abstract concept of an entirely different kind. Allegories in visual art are therefore nothing but hieroglyphs. Any artistic value they may also possess as intuitive presentations belongs to them on another account, not as allegories. Correggio's *Night*, Annibale Carracci's *Genius of Fame*, and Poussin's *Hours* are beautiful paintings; that is quite separate from their being allegories. As allegories they do no more than an inscription, and perhaps less.

We are reminded here of the distinction made above between a picture's real and nominal significance. Its nominal significance is the allegory as such: for example, the Genius of Fame. Its real significance is what is actually depicted: here, a handsome winged youth surrounded by handsome boys. This expresses an Idea. Yet its real significance acts upon us only while we forget the nominal, allegorical one. Once we think of the latter, we leave intuition and occupy our minds with an abstract concept. The passage from Idea to concept is always a descent. Indeed, the nominal significance, the allegorical intention, often impairs the real significance or intuitive truth. Consider the unnatural lighting in Correggio's *Night*: beautifully executed though it is, it is motivated only allegorically and is impossible in reality.

Thus any artistic value an allegorical painting has is separate from, and independent of, what it accomplishes as allegory. Such a work serves two ends at once: expressing a concept and expressing an Idea. Only the latter can be an artistic end. The former is foreign to art: a playful diversion in which a picture doubles as an inscription or hieroglyph, devised for people whom the true essence of art can never touch. It is like an artwork that is also a useful implement, such as a statue that doubles as a candelabrum or caryatid, or a bas-relief that also serves as Achilles' shield. True lovers of art will approve of neither arrangement.

An allegorical picture may, admittedly, have a powerful effect on the mind precisely as allegory. Under the same circumstances, however, an inscription would do likewise. Suppose a wish for fame has taken permanent, firm root in someone, who perhaps regards fame as a rightful possession withheld only until he can produce the documents establishing his claim. Standing before the Genius of Fame with its laurel wreaths might stir his whole mind and call his powers into action. But the same would happen if he suddenly saw the word “Fame” written large and clear on a wall. Or suppose someone has proclaimed a truth important either as a practical principle or as a scientific insight, yet no one believes him. An allegorical picture of Time lifting the veil to disclose naked Truth will affect him powerfully; so will the motto `Le temps découvre la vérité` (Time reveals the truth). What actually acts upon him is always the abstract thought, not what he sees.

Allegory in visual art is thus a mistaken effort in the service of an end wholly foreign to art. It becomes intolerable when it proceeds so far that forced, far-fetched interpretations lapse into absurdity. Examples include a tortoise meant to suggest a woman's seclusion; Nemesis looking down into the bosom of her dress to suggest that she sees even what is hidden; and Bellori's explanation that Annibale Carracci dressed Pleasure in yellow to suggest that its delights soon wither and turn yellow as straw does.

If there is no link at all between what is depicted and the concept indicated by it through subsumption under that concept or association of Ideas—if sign and signified are connected wholly by convention, through a positive and accidental stipulation—I call this kind of allegory a *symbol*. Thus the rose symbolizes secrecy, the laurel fame, the palm victory, the shell pilgrimage, and the cross the Christian religion. Direct indications through mere colours belong here too, such as yellow for falsity and blue for fidelity. Such symbols may often be useful in life, but their value is foreign to art. They should be regarded simply as hieroglyphs or even as a kind of Chinese script. They belong in the same class as coats of arms, the branch indicating an inn, the key by which one recognizes a chamberlain, or the leather by which one recognizes a miner.

When particular historical or mythical persons, or personified concepts, are identifiable through symbols fixed once and for all, these should properly be called *emblems*: the animals of the Evangelists, Minerva's owl, Paris's apple, Hope's anchor, and so forth. By “emblems,” however, people usually mean simple symbolic pictures explained by a motto and intended to illustrate a moral truth. Large collections of these exist by J. Camerarius, Alciatus, and others. They lead toward poetic allegory, to which I shall turn below. Greek sculpture addresses intuition and is therefore *aesthetic*; Indian sculpture addresses the concept and is thus merely *symbolic*.

This judgment of allegory follows closely from our account of art's inner nature, but is directly opposed to Winckelmann's view. Far from declaring allegory foreign to art's purpose and often obstructive, he defends it everywhere. He even places art's highest aim in the “presentation of general concepts and things that cannot be sensed” (*Works*, vol. 1, p. 55 ff.). Everyone may choose between these views. Winckelmann's ideas on this and similar questions concerning the metaphysics of beauty have, however, made one truth very clear to me: someone may have the greatest receptivity to beauty in art and the soundest judgment of it without being able to give an abstract, properly philosophical account of the nature of beauty and art. In the same way, someone may be very noble and virtuous, with a finely sensitive conscience that decides individual cases as accurately as a gold balance, yet be unable to fathom the ethical significance of actions philosophically and present it *in abstracto* (in the abstract).

Allegory has a quite different relation to *poetry* from its relation to visual art. Though objectionable in the latter, it is entirely permissible and useful in the former. In visual art it leads from what is given intuitively, the true subject of all art, to abstract thoughts. In poetry the relation is reversed: what words directly give us is the concept, and the immediate aim is always to lead from it to the intuitive, which the hearer's imagination must present. If visual art directs us from what is immediately given to something else, that other thing must be a concept, for only the abstract cannot be given directly there. Yet a concept must never be an artwork's origin or its communication the artwork's end. In poetry, by contrast, the concept is the material immediately given. It may quite properly be left behind in order to evoke something wholly different in intuition, where the goal is reached.

In a poem's context a concept or abstract thought may be indispensable yet incapable of immediate intuitive presentation. It is then often brought before intuition by an example falling under it. This happens already in every figurative expression, and in every metaphor, simile, parable, and allegory, which differ only in the length and fullness of their presentation. Comparisons and allegories therefore have an excellent effect in literary art. How beautifully Cervantes says of sleep, to express its release from all mental and bodily suffering, that “it is a cloak covering the whole person.” How beautifully Kleist gives allegorical expression to the thought that philosophers and researchers enlighten humanity in the line:

> “Those whose nightly lamp lights up the whole globe.”

How powerfully and vividly Homer characterizes ruin-bringing Ate: “Her feet are delicate, for she does not tread on the hard ground, but walks only upon the heads of human beings” (*Iliad*, XIX, 91). How strongly Menenius Agrippa's fable of the stomach and the limbs affected the Roman people who had seceded. How beautifully Plato's previously mentioned allegory of the cave, at the opening of Book Seven of the *Republic*, expresses a highly abstract philosophical doctrine. The story of Persephone, who falls under the power of the underworld because she tastes a pomegranate there, should likewise be regarded as a profound philosophical allegory. This becomes especially evident in Goethe's treatment of the story, beyond all praise, inserted as an episode in *The Triumph of Sensibility*.

I know three extended allegorical works. One is open and avowed: Baltasar Gracián's incomparable *Criticón*, a great, rich web of linked and highly ingenious allegories. They give moral truths a cheerful guise and thereby make them vividly intuitive, while the richness of his invention fills us with wonder. Two others are concealed allegories: *Don Quixote* and Gulliver in Lilliput. The former allegorizes the life of anyone who, unlike other people, seeks not merely personal welfare but an objective, ideal end that takes possession of thought and will; naturally such a person appears strange in this world. With Gulliver, we need only take everything physical in a spiritual sense to see what the `satirical rogue` (satirical rascal), as Hamlet would call him, means by it.

In poetic allegory the concept is always what is given, and its aim is to make that concept intuitive through an image. A painting may therefore sometimes express or support it; even then, the painting is regarded not as a work of visual art but as a signifying hieroglyph. It claims no value as painting, only as poetry. Such is Lavater's fine allegorical vignette, which must strengthen the heart of every noble defender of truth. A hand holding a light is stung by a wasp while gnats burn themselves in the flame above it. Beneath is the motto:

> “Though the gnat may scorch its wing,<br>
> And shatter its skull and all its little brain,<br>
> Light is still light;<br>
> And though the fiercest wasp may sting me,<br>
> I will not let it go.”

Here too belongs a gravestone bearing an extinguished, smoking candle and the inscription:

> “When it goes out, it will be plain<br>
> Whether it was tallow or wax.”

Finally, there is an old German family tree on which the last descendant of a far-reaching line expressed his decision to end his life in complete abstinence and chastity and thus let his family die out. He had himself depicted at the root of the many-branched tree, cutting it off above him with scissors. This class generally includes the symbolic pictures mentioned earlier and usually called emblems; they might also be called short painted fables with their moral stated. Allegories of this kind always belong to poetry, not painting, and are justified on that account. Their visual execution remains secondary; all it need do is make the subject recognizable.

In poetry as in visual art, allegory passes over into symbol when the intuitive thing presented and the abstraction it signifies have only an arbitrary connection. Since every symbol rests at bottom on convention, it has, among other drawbacks, this one: its meaning is forgotten over time and it then falls wholly silent. Who could guess, without already knowing, why the fish symbolizes Christianity? Only a Champollion: it is a phonetic hieroglyph through and through. As a poetic allegory the *Revelation of John* now stands roughly as the reliefs inscribed `magnus Deus sol Mithra` (the great god Sun Mithra) do: they still invite interpretation.[^1]
