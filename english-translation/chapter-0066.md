# §. 64.

Let us now pass from our philosophical, rather than mythical, account of eternal justice to the related questions of the ethical significance of action and of conscience, which is a merely felt knowledge of that justice. First, however, I want to call attention to two peculiarities of human nature. They may help show how everyone has at least a dim feeling of the essence of eternal justice, and of the unity and identity of the will in all its appearances on which that justice rests.

Quite apart from the state's purpose in punishing, which we have established as the basis of its right to punish, it satisfies not only the injured party, generally animated by a desire for revenge, but also a wholly disinterested spectator to see someone who has caused another pain suffer just as much pain in return. To me this expresses nothing other than an awareness of eternal justice. An unpurified understanding, however, immediately misunderstands and distorts that awareness. Captive to the *principium individuationis* (principle of individuation), it confuses the concepts and demands of appearance what belongs only to the thing in itself. It does not see that the offender and the injured person are one in themselves, that one being, failing to recognize itself in its own appearance, bears both torment and guilt. Instead it demands to see torment again in the very individual who bears the guilt.

Thus most people would also demand that a person of exceptional wickedness, whose degree of malice might exist in many others but not combined with his other qualities, should pay for everything he has done. Suppose that exceptional mental powers make him far superior to others and enable him, as a world conqueror for example, to inflict unspeakable suffering on millions. People would insist that at some time and place he atone for all those sufferings with an equal measure of pain. They do not see that in themselves the tormentor and the tormented are one: the same will through which his victims exist and live appears in him and finds in him the clearest revelation of its nature. It suffers in the conqueror no less than in the oppressed, indeed to a greater degree in him insofar as his consciousness is clearer and more distinct and his will more vehement. Christian ethics already shows that a deeper cognition, no longer captive to the *principium individuationis*, from which every virtue and nobility of character proceeds, no longer harbors this demand for retribution. It absolutely forbids returning evil for evil and locates eternal justice in the realm of the thing in itself, distinct from appearance: “Vengeance is mine; I will repay, says the Lord” (Romans 12:19).

A much more striking, though much rarer, trait of human nature also expresses the desire to draw eternal justice into the realm of experience, that is, of individuation. At the same time it points to a felt awareness that, as I said above, the will to live stages the great tragedy and comedy at its own expense, and that the same one will lives in all appearances. Sometimes we see a person so deeply outraged by a grave injustice he has suffered, or perhaps only witnessed, that he deliberately and without hope of escape stakes his own life on avenging himself against its perpetrator. He may seek out a powerful oppressor for years, finally murder him, and then die on the scaffold as he foresaw. Often he does not even try to avoid that end, for his life has retained value for him only as a means to revenge. Such cases are found particularly among the Spaniards.[^1]

If we look closely at the spirit behind this desire for retribution, we find it very different from ordinary revenge, which seeks to lessen one's own suffering by seeing the person who caused it suffer. Its aim deserves to be called punishment rather than revenge, for it really intends a future effect through an example. Here, however, there is no selfish purpose, either for the avenging individual, who perishes in the act, or for a society seeking safety through laws. This punishment is carried out by an individual, not by the state or in fulfilment of a law. Indeed, it always responds to an act the state would not or could not punish, and the state disapproves of the punishment.

It seems to me that the indignation driving such a person so far beyond all bounds of self-love springs from a profound awareness that he himself is the entire will to live appearing in all beings through all times. The remotest future therefore belongs to him just as the present does, and cannot be indifferent to him. He still affirms this will, but demands that no such monstrous injustice ever again appear in the spectacle expressing his being. By an act of revenge against which there is no protective wall, since fear of death does not deter the avenger, he wants to frighten every future offender. Though still affirming itself, the will to live is no longer attached here to its particular appearance, the individual. It embraces the Idea of the human being and wishes to keep its appearance pure of so monstrous and outrageous an injustice. This is a rare, significant, even sublime trait of character. The individual sacrifices himself in striving to become the arm of eternal justice, whose real nature he still misunderstands.

[^1]: The Spanish bishop who, during the last war, poisoned himself and the French generals dining at his table belongs here, as do several other incidents of that war. Examples can also be found in Montaigne, book 2, chapter 12.
