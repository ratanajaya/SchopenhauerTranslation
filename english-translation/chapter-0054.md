# §. 52.

We have now considered all the fine arts at the level of generality suited to our standpoint. We began with architecture, whose purpose as a fine art is to clarify the will's objectivation at the lowest grade of visibility, where it appears as the dim, cognitionless, law-governed striving of mass and yet already reveals division and conflict within itself, namely between gravity and rigidity. We ended with tragedy, which at the highest grade of the will's objectivation shows us that same conflict within the will in terrible magnitude and clarity. Yet one fine art has remained outside our account and had to remain so, since there was no suitable place for it in the systematic course of our presentation: *music*.

Music stands quite apart from all the others. In it we do not recognize a copy or repetition of any Idea of the beings in the world. Yet it is so great and splendid an art, acts so powerfully on the innermost human being, and is understood there so completely and deeply as a universal language whose clarity even surpasses that of the intuitive world itself, that we must surely seek in it more than an `exercitium arithmeticae occultum nescientis se numerare animi` (a hidden exercise in arithmetic by a mind unaware that it is counting).[^1] Leibniz described it thus, and was quite right insofar as he considered only its immediate outer significance, its shell. Were there nothing more to music, however, the satisfaction it offers would resemble the satisfaction of correctly solving an arithmetic problem. It could not be the intimate joy of hearing the deepest part of our own being given voice.

From our standpoint, which attends to aesthetic effect, we must therefore assign music a far more serious and profound significance concerning the innermost nature of the world and ourselves. The numerical relations into which music can be resolved are not what it signifies, but are themselves merely signs. By analogy with the other arts, all of which present a relation of copy to model, we may infer that music must stand in some such relation to the world. Its effect upon us is generally of the same kind as theirs, but stronger, swifter, more necessary, and more certain. Its relation to the world as a copy must also be exceedingly intimate, infinitely true, and accurate. Everyone understands music immediately, and it displays a certain unfailing precision: its form can be reduced to definite rules expressible in numbers, and it cannot depart from them without ceasing altogether to be music. Yet the point of comparison between music and the world, the respect in which music imitates or repeats it, lies deeply hidden. People have practised music in every age without being able to account for this relation. Content to understand it immediately, they give up trying to grasp their immediate understanding in abstract terms.

I gave myself over entirely to the impression of music in its many forms, then returned to reflection and to the course of thought set out in this book. I gained an insight into music's inner nature and the way it stands to the world in the relation of a copy, which analogy requires us to assume. This insight is entirely sufficient to me and satisfies my inquiry. It will perhaps be just as convincing to someone who has followed me so far and shares my view of the world. Yet I recognize that it is essentially impossible to prove. It posits a relation between music, as a representation, and something that can never essentially be a representation, and regards music as the copy of a model that can never itself be presented directly.

At the end of this third book, devoted chiefly to the arts, I can therefore do no more than set out the explanation of this wonderful art of sound that satisfies me. Whether readers assent to or reject it must depend partly on the effect music has on them, and partly on the effect of the whole, unified thought communicated in this book. Moreover, to assent to this account of music's significance with genuine conviction, one must often listen to music while reflecting steadily on it. This in turn requires thorough familiarity with the whole thought I have presented.

The adequate objectivation of the will consists in the Platonic Ideas. The aim of all the other arts is to prompt cognition of those Ideas through depictions of individual things—for artworks themselves are still individual things. This is possible only through a corresponding change in the knowing subject. All those arts therefore objectivate the will only indirectly, through the Ideas. Our world is nothing but the appearance of the Ideas in multiplicity through their entry into the *principium individuationis* (principle of individuation), the form of cognition possible for the individual as such. Music, since it bypasses the Ideas, is also wholly independent of the world of appearance. It simply ignores that world and could in a sense exist even if the world did not—a claim we cannot make of the other arts.

Music is as *immediate* an objectivation and image of the whole *will* as the world itself is, indeed as the Ideas are, whose multiplied appearance constitutes the world of individual things. Music is therefore by no means, like the other arts, an image of the Ideas. It is an *image of the will itself*, of which the Ideas too are objecthood. This is precisely why its effect is so much more powerful and penetrating than that of the other arts. They speak only of shadows; music speaks of the essence. Still, the same will objectivates itself in the Ideas and in music, though in wholly different ways. There must therefore be no direct likeness, but a parallel or analogy, between music and the Ideas, whose appearance in multiplicity and imperfection is the visible world. Demonstrating this analogy will help make the explanation intelligible despite its difficult subject.

In the deepest tones of harmony, in the fundamental bass, I recognize the lowest grades of the will's objectivation: inorganic nature, the mass of the planet. All the higher tones, which move more easily and die away more quickly, are understood to arise from the subsidiary vibrations of the low fundamental tone. When it sounds, they sound faintly along with it. A rule of harmony allows only those higher tones above a bass note that already sound with it of themselves as its `sons harmoniques` (harmonics) through these subsidiary vibrations. This is analogous to the way all the bodies and organized forms of nature must be regarded as arising through gradual development from the planet's mass. It is their bearer and their source; the higher tones stand in the same relation to the fundamental bass.

There is a lower limit beyond which no tone can be heard. Correspondingly, no matter can be perceived without form and quality—that is, without the expression of some further inexplicable force in which an Idea is expressed. More generally, no matter can be wholly without will. Just as a certain pitch is inseparable from a tone as such, a certain grade of the will's expression is inseparable from matter. The fundamental bass thus plays the part in harmony that inorganic nature, the rawest mass upon which everything rests and from which everything rises and develops, plays in the world.

In all the accompanying parts that produce harmony between the bass and the leading voice that sings the melody, I recognize the whole sequence of Ideas in which the will objectivates itself. The voices closer to the bass represent lower grades, still inorganic bodies but already manifesting themselves in many ways; the higher ones represent the worlds of plants and animals. The definite intervals of the scale run parallel to the definite grades of the will's objectivation, the definite species in nature. Deviations from the intervals' arithmetical correctness, produced by some temperament or by the choice of key, are analogous to the individual's deviation from the type of the species. Indeed, impure discordant notes that form no definite interval can be compared with monstrous births between two animal species, or between human being and animal.

All these bass and accompanying voices that make up *harmony* lack the coherence of progression possessed only by the upper voice singing the melody. Only that voice moves quickly and easily through modulations and runs; the others move more slowly and lack a coherent course of their own. The low bass, representing the rawest mass, moves most ponderously. It rises and falls only in large steps—thirds, fourths, fifths—never by a *single* tone, unless it is a bass transposed by double counterpoint. Slow movement is physically essential to it: a quick run or trill in the low register cannot even be imagined. The higher accompanying voices, parallel to the animal world, move faster, but still without melodic coherence or a meaningful course.

The disjointed movement and fixed rules governing all the accompanying voices are analogous to the fact that, throughout the non-rational world from crystal to the most perfect animal, no being has a truly coherent consciousness that would make its life a meaningful whole. None passes through a sequence of mental developments or perfects itself by cultivation. Everything stands uniformly at every moment just as it is according to its kind, determined by fixed law.

Finally, in the *melody*, the high, singing principal voice that leads the whole and progresses with unbounded freedom from beginning to end in the uninterrupted, meaningful connection of *one* thought, forming a whole, I recognize the highest grade of the will's objectivation: the reflective life and striving of the human being. The human being alone, endowed with reason, looks continually backward and forward over the course of actual life and innumerable possibilities, and thus leads a reflective life coherent as a whole. Correspondingly, the *melody* alone has a meaningful, purposeful connection from beginning to end.

Melody therefore tells the story of the will illuminated by reflection, whose imprint in reality is the series of its deeds. But it says more. It tells the will's most secret story, paints every stirring, every striving, every movement of the will—all that reason gathers under the broad, negative concept of feeling and cannot further admit into its abstractions. For this reason music has always been called the language of feeling and passion, just as words are the language of reason. Plato already describes it as `ἡ τῶν μελῶν κίνησις μεμιμημένη, ἐν τοῖς παθήμασιν ὅταν ψυχὴ γίνηται` (`melodiarum motus, animi affectus imitans`—the movement of melodies imitating affections of the soul; *Laws*, VII). Aristotle likewise asks: `διὰ τί οἱ ῥυθμοὶ καὶ τὰ μέλη, φωνὴ οὖσα, ἤθεσιν ἔοικε` (`cur numeri musici et modi, qui voces sunt, moribus similes sese exhibent?`—why do rhythms and melodies, though sounds, resemble dispositions?; *Problems*, 19).

The essence of a human being is that the will strives, is satisfied, and then strives anew, without end. Happiness and well-being consist only in a rapid passage from desire to satisfaction and from that satisfaction to a new desire. The failure of satisfaction is suffering; the failure of a new desire is empty longing, *languor* (listlessness), boredom. Correspondingly, melody's essence is a constant departure and wandering away from the key note by a thousand paths. It moves not only to harmonious degrees, the third and dominant, but to every tone, even the dissonant seventh and augmented intervals. Yet it always eventually returns to the key note. On all these paths melody expresses the will's many-shaped striving; by finally finding a harmonious degree, and still more by finding the key note, it also expresses satisfaction.

Inventing a melody and in it uncovering all the deepest secrets of human willing and feeling is the work of genius. Here more plainly than anywhere else genius works apart from reflection and conscious design; one could call it inspiration. Here, as everywhere in art, the concept is barren. The composer reveals the world's innermost nature and speaks its deepest wisdom in a language his reason does not understand, like a magnetic sleepwalker disclosing things of which she has no conception when awake. In a composer, therefore, the human being is more separate and distinct from the artist than in any other artist. Even when we explain this wonderful art, concepts show their poverty and limits. I shall nevertheless try to carry our analogy through.

A rapid passage from desire to satisfaction and from there to a new desire is happiness and well-being. Accordingly, quick melodies without large departures are cheerful. Slow melodies that encounter painful dissonances and work their way back to the key note only after many measures are sad, analogous to delayed and difficult satisfaction. The delay of a new stirring of the will, *languor*, could be expressed only by holding the key note, which would soon become unbearable. Very monotonous, empty melodies already come close to this. The short, readily understood phrases of quick dance music seem to speak only of easily attained, ordinary happiness. The *maestoso* (majestic manner), by contrast, with large phrases, long passages, and wide departures, signifies a greater and nobler striving toward a distant goal and its eventual attainment. It speaks of the suffering of a great, noble striving that disdains all petty happiness.

How marvellous is the effect of *minor* and *major*! How astonishing that the change of a semitone, the entrance of the minor third in place of the major, immediately and unfailingly impresses an anxious, painful feeling on us, from which the major mode releases us just as immediately. An *adagio* (slow movement) in minor expresses the highest pain and becomes the most moving lament. Dance music in minor seems to signify missing a petty happiness one would do better to scorn, or reaching a lowly goal through hardship and drudgery. The inexhaustibility of possible melodies corresponds to nature's inexhaustible diversity of individuals, faces, and life courses. A passage into an entirely different key breaks the connection with what went before and resembles death: the individual ends there, but the will that appeared in that individual lives on as before, appearing in others whose consciousness has no connection with the first.

In tracing these analogies, we must never forget that music is related to them only indirectly, never directly. It expresses not appearance but solely the inner essence, the in-itself of every appearance: the will itself. It therefore expresses not this or that particular joy, sorrow, pain, horror, jubilation, cheerfulness, or peace of mind, but *joy*, *sorrow*, *pain*, *horror*, *jubilation*, *cheerfulness*, and *peace of mind themselves*: in a sense *in abstracto* (in the abstract), their essence without any accessory details, including the motives for them. Yet we understand them perfectly in this distilled form.

Our imagination is therefore readily stirred by music and tries to give shape to this invisible yet vividly stirring spirit world that speaks so directly to us, to clothe it in flesh and bone and embody it in an analogous example. This is the origin of song with words and ultimately of opera. Opera's text should therefore never leave its subordinate place to become the principal thing and reduce music to a mere means of its expression. That would be a great mistake and a serious reversal. Music everywhere expresses only the quintessence of life and its events, never the events themselves; their differences therefore do not always affect music. This generality unique to music, joined to utmost precision, gives it its high value as a panacea for all our sufferings. When music tries to cling too closely to words or shape itself after events, it tries to speak a language that is not its own. No one avoided this error more completely than *Rossini*: his music speaks its *own* language so distinctly and purely that it needs no words, and has its full effect when played by instruments alone.

We may therefore regard the world of appearance, or nature, and music as two different expressions of the same thing. That thing alone mediates their analogy, and knowledge of it is required to understand the analogy. Seen as an expression of the world, music is a language of the highest generality. It stands to the generality of concepts much as concepts stand to individual things. Yet music's generality is not the empty generality of abstraction. It is quite different, joined throughout to clear definiteness. In this respect it resembles geometrical figures and numbers: universal forms of all possible objects of experience, applicable to all *a priori* (prior to experience), yet intuitive rather than abstract and determinate throughout.

All possible strivings, excitations, and expressions of the will—all those events within the human being that reason throws together under the broad, negative concept of feeling—can be expressed through the infinitely many possible melodies. But they are always expressed in the generality of mere form, without the material; according to their in-itself, not their appearance: their innermost soul, as it were, without a body. This intimate relation between music and the true essence of all things explains why fitting music accompanying any scene, action, event, or setting seems to disclose its most secret meaning and offers the truest and clearest commentary on it. It also explains why someone who gives himself wholly to the impression of a symphony seems to see all possible events of life and the world passing before him, yet, on reflection, can point to no likeness between the play of tones and the things it called to mind.

As I have said, music differs from all other arts because it is not an image of appearance—or, more precisely, of the will's adequate objecthood—but directly an image of the will itself. It thus presents the metaphysical counterpart to everything physical in the world, the thing in itself to every appearance. We could as well call the world embodied music as embodied will. This explains why music at once makes every painting and every scene in real life and the world appear more significant, especially when its melody is analogous to the inner spirit of the appearance before us.

This relation makes it possible to set a poem to music as a song, an intuitive depiction as a pantomime, or both together as an opera. Such particular images of human life set beneath the universal language of music are never joined or fitted to it by complete necessity. They stand to it as an arbitrarily selected example stands to a general concept: they present in the definiteness of reality what music says in the generality of mere form. Melodies, in a sense, are abstractions from reality, like general concepts. The world of individual things supplies intuition, the particular and individual case, to both the generality of concepts and that of melodies. Yet in one respect these generalities are opposites. Concepts contain only the forms first abstracted from intuition, the stripped outer shell of things: they are abstractions in the strict sense. Music gives the innermost kernel prior to all shaping, the heart of things.

We could express this relation aptly in Scholastic terms: concepts are `universalia post rem` (universals after the thing), music gives `universalia ante rem` (universals before the thing), and reality gives `universalia in re` (universals in the thing). Other examples of the universal expressed by a melody attached to a poem could fit its general sense equally well. Thus one composition suits many stanzas, and this is also why the *vaudeville* is possible. As noted, any relation between a musical composition and an intuitive depiction is possible because both are quite different expressions of the world's same inner essence. When such a relation actually exists in a particular case—when the composer has succeeded in expressing, in music's universal language, the stirrings of the will at the core of an event—the song's melody or the opera's music is expressive.

The analogy the composer discovers between the two must arise from immediate, unconscious cognition of the world's essence. It must not be imitation mediated by concepts and carried out with deliberate intention. Otherwise the music does not express the inner essence, the will itself, but inadequately imitates only its appearance. This is what all music that literally copies things does: Haydn's *The Seasons*, for example, and many passages in his *Creation*, where appearances of the intuitive world are directly imitated, as well as all battle pieces. Such music is to be rejected altogether.

Music's inexpressibly intimate quality, by which it passes us like a paradise wholly familiar yet forever distant, entirely comprehensible yet inexplicable, rests on its reproducing every stirring of our innermost being while leaving out reality altogether and remaining far from its torment. Its essential seriousness, which excludes the ridiculous from music's own immediate domain, has the same explanation. Its object is not representation, with respect to which deception and the ridiculous alone are possible; it is directly the will, essentially the most serious thing of all, since everything depends on it. Even repeat signs and the *da capo* (from the beginning) testify to how rich in content and significance its language is. They would be intolerable in works made of words, but in music they are apt and welcome, for we must hear it twice to grasp it fully.

Throughout this account I have tried to show that music, in a language of the highest generality yet with the greatest definiteness and truth, expresses in homogeneous material—mere tones—the world's inner essence, its in-itself, which we conceive as will according to its clearest manifestation. According to my view and aim, philosophy is nothing but a complete and correct repetition and articulation of the world's essence in very general concepts, for only these permit a survey comprehensive and applicable everywhere. Anyone who has followed me and entered into my way of thinking will therefore find it less paradoxical if I say this: if we could give a perfectly correct, complete explanation of music down to its details—a full repetition in concepts of what it expresses—that would at once be an adequate repetition and explanation of the world in concepts, or would correspond exactly to one. It would be the true philosophy.

Accordingly, we may parody Leibniz's remark quoted above, quite right at a lower standpoint, in the light of our higher view of music: `Musica est exercitium metaphysices occultum nescientis se philosophari animi` (Music is a hidden exercise in metaphysics by a mind unaware that it is philosophizing). For *scire*, to know, means everywhere to have translated something into abstract concepts.

Leibniz's original remark has, however, been confirmed in many ways. Apart from its aesthetic or inner significance, viewed merely from outside and empirically, music is a means of grasping large numbers and complex numerical relations immediately and *in concreto* (in the concrete), though ordinarily we can grasp them only indirectly, through concepts. Combining these two quite different yet correct views of music, we can therefore conceive how a philosophy of numbers such as that of Pythagoras, or the Chinese *I Ching*, might be possible. In this sense we can interpret the Pythagorean saying cited by Sextus Empiricus (*Against the Mathematicians*, VII): `τῷ ἀριθμῷ δὲ τὰ πάντ’ ἐπέοικεν` (`numero cuncta assimilantur`—all things are likened to number).

If we then bring this view to our account of harmony and melody, we shall find a purely moral philosophy without an explanation of nature, such as Socrates wished to introduce, analogous to a melody without harmony, which Rousseau wanted exclusively. Conversely, physics and metaphysics without ethics correspond to harmony without melody.

Allow me to append a few further observations on the analogy between music and the world of appearance. In the preceding book we found that the human being, as the highest grade of the will's objectivation, could not appear alone and detached. Humanity presupposed the lower grades, and these in turn the grades below them. Similarly, music, which like the world objectivates the will directly, is complete only in full harmony. For its whole effect, the high leading voice of the melody needs the accompaniment of all the other voices down to the lowest bass, which is to be regarded as their source. Melody itself enters harmony as an integral part, just as harmony enters melody. Only thus, in the complete combination of voices, does music express what it aims to express. Likewise the one will, outside time, finds its complete objectivation only in the full union of all grades that reveal its essence at countless degrees of increasing clarity.

Another analogy is especially remarkable. In the preceding book we saw that, despite the adaptation of all appearances of the will to one another as species, which gives rise to teleological reflection, an irreconcilable conflict remains between those appearances as individuals. Visible at every grade, it makes the world a perpetual battleground among appearances of one and the same will, revealing the will's inner contradiction. Something corresponding to this is found even in music. A perfectly pure harmonic system of tones is impossible not only physically but even arithmetically. The numbers expressing tones have irreducible irrationalities. No scale can be calculated in which every fifth relates to the key note as 2 to 3, every major third as 4 to 5, every minor third as 5 to 6, and so on. If the tones are correct relative to the key note, they are no longer correct relative to one another; the fifth, for example, must also serve as the minor third of the third. The tones of a scale are like actors who must play different roles at different times. Perfectly correct music cannot even be conceived, let alone performed. Every possible music therefore departs from perfect purity. It can only conceal its essential dissonances by distributing them among all the tones—that is, by temperament. See Chladni's *Acoustics*, § 30, and his *Short Survey of the Theory of Sound and Tone*, p. 12.[^2]

I could add more about the way music is perceived: solely in and through time, with space completely excluded and without the influence of cognition of causality, that is, of the understanding. Tones make their aesthetic impression already as effects; unlike in intuition, we do not first trace them back to their cause. I shall not prolong this discussion further, however. Perhaps I have already been too expansive in this third book for some readers or entered too far into details. My purpose required it, and it will seem less objectionable if one recalls the rarely appreciated importance and high value of art.

In our view the entire visible world is only the will's objectivation, its mirror, accompanying it toward self-knowledge and, as we shall soon see, toward the possibility of redemption. At the same time, the world as representation, viewed apart from willing and allowed alone to fill consciousness, is life's most delightful and only innocent side. We must thus regard art as a higher intensification and more perfect development of all this. Art essentially achieves, but in a more concentrated, accomplished, deliberate, and reflective way, what the visible world itself achieves. In the fullest sense it may therefore be called the flower of life. If the whole world as representation is merely the will's visibility, art clarifies that visibility. It is the *camera obscura* (dark chamber), showing objects more purely and allowing us to survey them better: the play within the play, the stage upon the stage in *Hamlet*.

Enjoyment of all beauty, the comfort art provides, and the artist's enthusiasm that makes him forget life's hardships all rest on one fact. So does the genius's one advantage over others, which alone compensates for suffering increased in proportion to clarity of consciousness and for barren solitude among people unlike himself. As we shall see later, the in-itself of life—the will, existence itself—is continual suffering, partly miserable and partly terrible. Yet life regarded purely as representation, or repeated in art, is free of torment and offers a meaningful spectacle. This purely knowable side of the world, and its repetition in any art, is the artist's element.

The artist is captivated by contemplating the spectacle of the will's objectivation. He stays with it and never tires of contemplating and presenting it again, even while he himself bears the cost of staging that spectacle: he himself is the will that objectivates itself in this way and remains in constant suffering. Pure, true, deep cognition of the world's essence becomes an end in itself for him, and with it he remains. It does not, therefore, become for him a quietive of the will, as we shall see in the following book with the saint who has attained resignation. It releases him from life only for moments, not forever. For now it is no way out of life, only a comfort within it—until the strength thus increased, finally weary of the play, turns to the serious matter. Raphael's St Cecilia may be taken as a symbol of this transition. We too shall now turn to that serious matter in the following book.
