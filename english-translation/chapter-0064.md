We have already seen that the first and simplest affirmation of the will to live is the affirmation of one's own body: the will presents itself in acts in time only so far as the body already presents the same will spatially in its form and purposiveness. This affirmation appears as preservation of the body through the use of its own powers. Satisfaction of the sexual drive is immediately connected to it and belongs to it insofar as the genitals belong to the body. *Voluntary* renunciation of that satisfaction, based on no *motive*, is therefore already denial of the will to live: the will freely cancels itself when cognition has arisen that acts as a *quietive*. Denial of one's own body thus already appears as the will's contradiction of its own appearance. The body still objectivates the will to reproduce in the genitals, yet reproduction is not willed. Precisely because such renunciation denies or cancels the will to live, it is a difficult and painful conquest of oneself. We shall return to it below.

The will presents this *self-affirmation* of each body in countless individuals alongside one another. Through the egoism common to them all, the will in one individual readily passes beyond that affirmation to *deny* the will appearing in another. The first individual's will breaks through the boundary of another's affirmation either by destroying or injuring that person's body, or by forcing its powers to serve his own will instead of the will appearing in that body. He withdraws the powers of another body from the will to which they belong and increases the power serving *his* will beyond his own body's powers. Thus he affirms his own will beyond his own body by denying the will appearing in another body.

This intrusion across the boundary of another's affirmation of the will has always been clearly recognized and named *injustice*. Both parties recognize what has happened at once, though as feeling rather than in the clear abstraction used here. The person suffering injustice feels the invasion of the sphere in which his own body affirms itself, and its denial by another individual, as an immediate mental pain quite separate from physical pain inflicted by the deed or distress over the loss. The person committing injustice likewise has an immediate dim feeling, not an abstract thought, that in himself he is the same will that appears in the other's body. In one appearance it affirms itself so vehemently that it crosses the boundary of its own body and its powers, denying that same will in another appearance. As will in itself, therefore, its vehemence makes it struggle against and tear at itself. This feeling is called a pang of conscience or, more exactly in this case, the feeling of having committed *injustice*.

*Injustice*, whose concept we have analysed in the most general abstraction, finds its most complete, proper, and palpable expression *in concreto* (in the concrete) in cannibalism. That is its clearest visible type, a dreadful image of the will's greatest conflict with itself at its highest grade of objectivation, the human being. Murder follows next. A pang of conscience—whose meaning we have just stated abstractly and dryly—follows its commission at once with terrible clarity, inflicting a wound on peace of mind that cannot heal in a lifetime. Our horror of a murder committed, like our recoil from committing one, corresponds to the boundless attachment to life that pervades every living being as an appearance of the will to live. Later we shall analyse in more detail the feeling that accompanies injustice and evil, the anguish of conscience, and bring it into conceptual clarity. Intentional mutilation or mere injury of another body, even a blow, is of the same essential kind as murder and differs only in degree. Injustice also takes the form of subjugating another individual and forcing that person into slavery. Finally, it appears in an attack on another's property. Insofar as the property is regarded as the fruit of that person's labour, this is essentially of the same kind as enslavement, bearing the relation to it that a mere injury bears to murder.

On our account of injustice, *property* that cannot be taken from someone *without injustice* can only be what has been worked upon by that person's powers. To take it away is to withdraw the powers of his body from the will objectivated there and make them serve the will objectivated in another body. Only in this way can someone committing injustice invade another's sphere of willing by attacking an inanimate thing quite distinct from the other's body: the powers and labour of that body have, as it were, grown together with the thing and become identified with it. It follows that every genuine *moral* right of property is originally grounded solely in labour. This was generally accepted before Kant and is beautifully and plainly stated in the oldest of law books: “Sages who know ancient times declare that a cultivated field belongs to the person who uprooted the wood, cleared it, and ploughed it, just as an antelope belongs to the first hunter who mortally wounded it” (*Laws of Manu*, IX, 44).

I can explain Kant's entire doctrine of right, an odd tangle of mutually supporting errors, only by the weakness of his old age. This applies also to his attempt to ground property right in first occupation. How could the mere declaration of my will to exclude others from using a thing immediately give me the *right* to exclude them? The declaration plainly needs a ground of right; Kant assumes that it is one. How could a person act unjustly in himself, morally, by disregarding a claim to sole possession grounded on nothing but someone else's declaration? How could his conscience be troubled by it? It is obvious that there can be no *rightful act of taking possession* as such. There can only be a rightful *appropriation* or acquisition through the use of powers originally one's own.

Where someone else's effort has worked upon, improved, protected from harm, or preserved a thing, however slight that effort—even if only picking a wild fruit or lifting it from the ground—someone who takes the thing plainly deprives that person of the result of the power expended upon it. The taker makes the other's body serve *his* will instead of its own, affirming his will beyond its appearance in his own body by denying the other's. That is injustice.[^1] By contrast, merely enjoying a thing without any effort to work upon or protect it from destruction gives no right to it, any more than declaring one's will to possess it exclusively does. If a family has hunted alone in an area for a century without improving it at all, it cannot exclude a newcomer who now wishes to hunt there without committing moral injustice.

The so-called right of preoccupation, under which one demands, as a further reward for having enjoyed a thing, an exclusive right to go on enjoying it, has no moral foundation. A newcomer could answer someone relying on that right with much better reason: “Precisely because you have enjoyed it so long, it is right that others enjoy it now.” No exclusive possession of a thing wholly incapable of being worked upon, improved, or protected from harm has a moral foundation, unless all others voluntarily surrender their claims, perhaps in reward for other services. That already presupposes a community governed by agreement: the state. Moral property right as derived above, by its nature, gives the owner as unrestricted a power over a thing as over his own body. He can therefore transfer his property by exchange or gift, and another then possesses it with the same moral right.

In general, *injustice is committed* either through *force* or through *cunning*. They are the same in what matters morally. In murder it makes no difference whether I use a dagger or poison, and the same holds by analogy for bodily injury. Every other case of injustice can be traced to forcing another person to serve my will instead of his own, to act according to my will rather than his. With force I achieve this by physical causality. With cunning I use motivation, causality passing through cognition: I offer the other person *apparent motives* by which, believing he follows *his* will, he in fact follows *mine*. Since motives exist in cognition, I can do this only by falsifying his cognition. This is the *lie*.

A lie always aims to influence someone else's will, not merely that person's cognition in and for itself. It influences cognition only as a means, insofar as cognition determines the will. My lie itself proceeds from my will and needs a motive. Only another's willing and acting can be such a motive, not that person's cognition in itself, which can never influence *my* will or move it toward an end except indirectly through willing and action. This applies not only to lies told for plain self-interest, but to lies told from pure malice, which seeks pleasure in the painful consequences of the error it causes. Even mere boasting aims to gain easier or greater influence over others' willing and acting by raising their regard and opinion of the boaster. Simply withholding a truth, refusing to say anything, is not in itself unjust. But every assertion of a falsehood is. Someone who refuses to show a lost traveller the right way does him no injustice; someone who points him the wrong way does.

It follows that every *lie*, like every act of violence, is *injustice* as such. Its purpose is to extend the rule of my will over other individuals and thus affirm my will by denying theirs, just as violence does. The most complete lie is a *broken contract*, in which all the features described are wholly and distinctly present. When I enter a contract, the other party's promised performance is directly and avowedly the motive for my performance now. Promises are exchanged deliberately and formally. By assumption each party controls the truth of his statement. If the other breaks the contract, he has deceived me. By putting merely apparent motives into my cognition, he has directed my will to his ends and extended the rule of his will over another person. He has therefore committed a complete injustice. This is the basis of contracts' moral lawfulness and validity.

Injustice through *force* is less *disgraceful* for its perpetrator than injustice through *cunning*. Force testifies to physical strength, which impresses humanity under all circumstances. Cunning takes a roundabout path and thus betrays weakness, lowering its user both physically and morally. Moreover, lying and deceit succeed only when the deceiver simultaneously expresses disgust and contempt for them to gain trust. His victory rests on being credited with honesty he does not possess. The deep revulsion everywhere felt toward guile, disloyalty, and betrayal arises because trust and honesty form the bond that unites again from outside the will fragmented among individuals. They thereby restrain the consequences of the egoism produced by that fragmentation. Disloyalty and betrayal tear this last external bond and give egoism's consequences free rein.

In our account the content of the concept *injustice* is an individual's action extending affirmation of the will appearing in his own body until it denies the will appearing in other bodies. Through general examples we have shown where injustice begins and marked its degrees from highest to lower by a few principal concepts. Thus *injustice* is the original, positive concept; *right* or justice, its opposite, is derived and negative. We should attend to concepts, not words. No one would ever have spoken of right if there were no injustice. The concept of right contains simply the negation of injustice. It includes every act that does not cross the boundary described above by denying another's will in order to affirm one's own more strongly. In a purely *moral* determination, this boundary divides all possible actions into just and unjust.

As soon as an act does not invade and deny another's sphere of affirmation in the manner described, it is not an injustice. Refusing help to someone in urgent need, or calmly watching another starve while one has plenty, is therefore cruel and devilish but not unjust. We can, however, say with complete certainty that anyone capable of such lovelessness and hardness will commit every injustice when his desires require it and no force restrains him.

The concept of *right*, as negation of injustice, finds its chief application and doubtless first arose when an attempted injustice is warded off by force. The defence cannot itself be injustice and is therefore right, although the force used in it, considered alone and apart, would be unjust. Its motive justifies it and makes it rightful. If someone affirms his will so far that he enters the sphere of affirmation essential to me as a person and thereby denies it, my resistance only denies that denial. On my part it is no more than affirmation of the will that appears essentially and originally in my body and is already expressed *implicitly* by its mere appearance. It is therefore not unjust but *rightful*.

I then have a *right* to negate the other's denial with the force needed to cancel it. This may extend to killing the other individual. An invading external force can be resisted with a counterforce somewhat stronger than itself without any injustice, and therefore rightly. Everything I do remains within the sphere of affirmation of the will essential to my person and already expressed by it. That sphere is the site of the struggle; I do not enter the other's sphere. My act is the denial of a denial, thus affirmation rather than denial itself. If another will denies mine as it appears in my body and my use of its powers to preserve it, without denying any other will that respects the same boundary, I may *without injustice* compel the other to cease that denial. To this extent I have a *right to use force*.

Whenever I have a complete right to use *force* against another, I may, according to the circumstances, counter the other's force with *cunning* instead, without injustice. I therefore have a genuine *right to lie exactly as far as I have a right to compel*. Someone who assures a highway robber searching him that he has nothing else is acting quite rightly, as is someone who lures a nocturnal intruder into a cellar by a lie and locks him in. A captive carried away by robbers, such as Barbary corsairs, has a right to kill them for freedom, either by open force or by stealth. A promise extorted by immediate physical violence is therefore wholly nonbinding: the person coerced could rightfully escape the coercers by killing them, still more by deceiving them.

Someone who cannot recover stolen property by force commits no injustice by getting it back through cunning. Indeed, if someone gambles away money stolen from me, I have the right to use false dice against him, since everything I win from him already belongs to me. Anyone denying this would have still more reason to deny the lawfulness of stratagems in war, which amount to lies in action and confirm Queen Christina of Sweden's saying: “Human words are worth nothing; their deeds can scarcely be trusted.” Thus the boundary of right runs very close to that of injustice. It is unnecessary to show that all this agrees with what I said above about the injustice of lying and violence. It may also clarify the curious theories about lies told from necessity.[^2]

Right and injustice are therefore purely *moral* determinations. They are valid for considering human action as such and its *inner meaning in itself*. This announces itself directly in consciousness. On one side, committing injustice is accompanied by inner pain, a felt awareness in the wrongdoer of his will's excessive affirmation, which has reached the point of denying the will's appearance in another. He also feels that although as appearance he differs from the injured person, in himself he is identical with that person. The full explanation of this inner meaning of anguish of conscience belongs later. On the other side, the person suffering injustice is painfully aware that another has denied his will as expressed already in his body and its natural needs, which nature directs him to satisfy through that body's powers. He is also aware that he could resist that denial in any way without injustice if he had the power.

This purely moral meaning is the only meaning right and injustice have for a human being *as a human being*, not as a citizen. It would therefore remain even in a state of nature without positive law. It is the basis and content of what has been called *natural right*, though “moral right” would be better. Its validity extends not to suffering or outward actuality but only to doing and to the self-knowledge of one's individual will that follows from doing, called *conscience*. In a state of nature it cannot always assert itself outwardly against others or prevent force from ruling in place of right. There it depends only on a person not to *commit* injustice in each case. Whether he will *suffer* injustice depends on his contingent external strength.

Right and injustice are therefore valid even in the state of nature and are not conventions, but there they are only *moral* concepts for each person's self-knowledge of his will. They mark a fixed point on the scale of the widely differing degrees to which the will to live affirms itself in human individuals, like the freezing point on a thermometer. At this point affirmation of one's own will turns into denial of another's; committing injustice shows how vehement one's will is and how captive one's cognition remains in the *principium individuationis* (principle of individuation), the form of cognition wholly in the will's service.

Someone who sets aside or denies a purely moral consideration of action and considers only its outward effect and results can, with *Hobbes*, declare right and injustice conventional, arbitrary determinations that do not exist outside positive law. External experience can never teach such a person what does not belong to external experience. Hobbes strikingly characterizes his entirely empirical way of thinking in his *De principiis Geometrarum* (On the Principles of the Geometers): he denies pure mathematics outright and stubbornly maintains that a point has extension and a line breadth. We cannot show him a point without extension or a line without breadth, and can no more teach him the *a priori* nature of mathematics than that of right, since he has shut himself off from all non-empirical cognition.

Pure *doctrine of right* is thus a chapter of *morality*. It concerns directly only *doing*, not *suffering*. Only doing expresses the will, and morality considers the will alone. Suffering is merely an event. Morality can consider it only indirectly, by showing that something done solely to avoid suffering an injustice is not itself an injustice. This chapter of morality would determine precisely how far an individual may affirm the will already objectivated in his body without denying the same will appearing in someone else. It would then identify the acts that cross that boundary, are therefore unjust, and may accordingly be resisted without injustice. One's own *doing* would always remain the subject of inquiry.

In external experience, however, what appears as an event is *suffering injustice*. In it, more plainly than anywhere else, we see the will to live in conflict with itself. This conflict arises from the multiplicity of individuals and their egoism, both conditioned by the *principium individuationis*, the form the world as representation takes for an individual's cognition. We have already seen that this conflict among individuals is an ever flowing source of a very large part of the suffering essential to human life.

Reason, which all these individuals share, lets them grasp not only particular cases, as animals do, but the whole in its abstract connections. It soon taught them to recognize the source of that suffering and to seek a way to diminish or, if possible, abolish it through a common sacrifice outweighed by the common benefit. Although an individual egoist enjoys committing an injustice on an occasion, that act necessarily has a counterpart: another individual suffers the injustice, and this is a great pain to him. When reason considers the whole, it steps for a moment outside its own individual's one-sided standpoint and attachment to him. It sees that one person's enjoyment of committing injustice is outweighed each time by the relatively greater pain of the one who suffers it. Reason sees further that if everything is left to chance, each person must fear suffering injustice much more often than he enjoys committing it. Thus, to reduce the suffering spread among everyone and distribute it as evenly as possible, the best and only means is to spare everyone the pain of suffering injustice by having everyone renounce the pleasure obtainable through committing it.

This means, readily devised and gradually perfected by egoism proceeding methodically through reason and abandoning its one-sided standpoint, is the *social contract*, or *law*. Plato already presents its origin as I do here in the *Republic*. In fact, this is the only essential origin, dictated by the nature of the matter. Nor can the state in any country ever have had another, for this manner of origin and this purpose alone make it a state. It makes no difference whether the condition preceding it among a particular people was a crowd of independent savages, or anarchy, or a crowd of slaves ruled at the strongest person's whim, or despotism. Neither condition was a state. The state first arises through that common agreement, and is more or less perfect according as the agreement is more or less mixed with anarchy or despotism. Republics tend toward anarchy, monarchies toward despotism, and the constitutional monarchy invented as a middle course toward rule by factions. To found a perfect state one would have to begin by creating beings whose nature allowed them always to sacrifice their own welfare for the public good. Until then, something can be achieved by having *one* family whose welfare is inseparable from the country's, so that at least in the principal matters it cannot advance one without the other. This is the source of the strength and advantage of hereditary monarchy.

Morality, then, is concerned exclusively with *doing* right or wrong, and can mark the precise boundary of action for someone resolved to commit no injustice. Conversely, political theory, the theory of legislation, concerns only *suffering* injustice. It would take no interest in *committing* injustice except for its necessarily corresponding instance of someone suffering it, the enemy it aims to oppose. Indeed, if one could conceive an unjust act connected with no suffering of injustice on anyone else's part, the state would consistently have no reason to forbid it. Moreover, because in *morality* the will and disposition are the object of consideration and the only reality, morality counts a firm resolve to commit an injustice, restrained and rendered ineffective only by external power, as equal to an injustice actually committed; it judges the person so resolved unjust. The state, by contrast, takes no interest whatever in will and disposition merely as such. It is concerned only with the *act*, whether attempted or completed, because of its counterpart, the *suffering* of another. For the state the act, the occurrence, is the only reality; disposition and intention are investigated only insofar as they explain the meaning of the act. Thus the state will not forbid anyone to entertain constant thoughts of murder and poison against another, provided it knows for certain that fear of the sword and the breaking wheel will always prevent those thoughts from taking effect. The state has no foolish scheme to extirpate the inclination to commit injustice, or an evil disposition. It merely places beside every possible motive for committing an injustice a stronger motive to refrain from it: the inevitable punishment. Accordingly the criminal code is as complete a register as possible of counter-motives to all presumably possible criminal acts, both set out *in abstracto* (in the abstract), for application *in concreto* (in a concrete case) when needed.

For this purpose political theory, or legislation, borrows from morality the chapter on the doctrine of right, which determines the precise boundary between right and injustice along with their inner meaning. It does so solely to use the other side of that doctrine. Every boundary morality says we must not cross if we do not wish to *commit* injustice is viewed instead as a boundary whose crossing by another we need not tolerate if we do not wish to *suffer* injustice, and from which we have a *right* to repel others. Laws therefore fortify those boundaries as seen from the potentially passive side. As the historian has wittily been called a prophet turned backward, so the jurist is a moralist turned backward. Jurisprudence in the proper sense, the doctrine of the *rights* one may assert, is morality turned backward in the chapter where morality teaches the rights one may not violate. The concept of injustice and of its negation, right, is originally *moral*; it becomes *juridical* when the starting point shifts from the active to the passive side. This reversal, together with Kant's doctrine of right, which very wrongly derives the establishment of the state as a moral duty from his categorical imperative, has lately caused the strange error that the state is an institution for promoting morality, arises from striving after morality, and is consequently directed against egoism. As if the inner disposition, to which alone morality or immorality belongs, the eternally free will, could be modified externally and changed by external action! Still more perverse is the doctrine that the state is the condition of freedom in the moral sense, and thus of morality, when freedom lies beyond appearance, still more beyond human institutions.

The state, as I have said, is so little directed against egoism as such that it springs precisely from egoism that understands its interests, acts methodically, and moves from a one-sided to a general standpoint. It sums the shared egoism of everyone and exists only to serve it. It is established on the correct assumption that pure morality, or doing right for moral reasons, cannot be expected; otherwise the state itself would be superfluous. Its purpose is therefore not to oppose egoism but to secure well-being against the harmful consequences that arise reciprocally among a multitude of egoistic individuals. Thus *Aristotle* already says (*De Rep.*, III): Τέλος μὲν οὖν πόλεως τὸ εὖ ζῆν· τοῦτο δέ ἐστιν τὸ ζῆν εὐδαιμόνως καὶ καλῶς. (*Finis civitatis est bene vivere, hoc autem est beate et pulchre vivere*; “The end of the city is living well, that is, living happily and nobly.”) *Hobbes* too explained this origin and purpose of the state correctly and excellently. The old maxim of all political order, *salus publica prima lex esto* (“let the public welfare be the first law”), expresses the same point.

If the state achieved its purpose perfectly, it would produce the same outward appearance as a universal, perfectly just disposition. Yet the inner nature and origin of the two appearances would be opposite. In the latter case no one would *want to commit* injustice; in the former, no one would *want to suffer* it, and the proper means to that end would have been used perfectly. The same line can be drawn from opposite directions, and a muzzled predator is as harmless as an herbivore. The state cannot go further than this point. It cannot produce an appearance like that arising from universal mutual benevolence and love. For we have just found that, by its nature, it would not prohibit an unjust act without any corresponding suffering of injustice by someone else; it prohibits every unjust act only because such a thing is impossible. Conversely, in keeping with its aim of general welfare, it would gladly ensure that everyone *received* benevolence and all kinds of charitable acts. But these necessarily have a counterpart in someone's *performing* them. Every citizen would want the passive role, no one the active role, and there would be no ground for imposing the latter on one person rather than another. Consequently only the negative, namely *right*, can be enforced, not the positive duties called duties of love or imperfect duties.

Legislation, then, borrows the pure doctrine of right, the moral doctrine of the nature and limits of right and injustice. It applies that doctrine from the opposite side for purposes foreign to morality, and accordingly establishes positive legislation and the means of upholding it, that is, the state. Positive legislation is thus the purely moral doctrine of right applied in reverse. Such an application may take account of the special conditions and circumstances of a particular people. But the resulting legislation is properly *positive right*, and the state a *lawful* association, a *state* in the proper sense and a morally permissible institution, only if the positive legislation is essentially determined throughout by the pure doctrine of right, so that every provision can be grounded in that doctrine. Otherwise it establishes *positive injustice*: it is itself publicly acknowledged and enforced injustice. Every despotism is of this sort, as are the constitutions of most Muslim realms and even parts of many other constitutions, such as serfdom and forced labor. Pure doctrine of right, or natural right—better, moral right—underlies every lawful positive legislation, though always through this reversal, just as pure mathematics underlies every branch of applied mathematics.

The most important points of pure doctrine of right, which philosophy must provide to legislation for that purpose, are these: (1) explanation of the inner and proper meaning and origin of the concepts of injustice and right, and their application and place in morality; (2) derivation of the right of property; (3) derivation of the moral validity of contracts, since this is the moral foundation of the social contract; (4) explanation of the state's origin and purpose, the relation of this purpose to morality, and the transfer of the moral doctrine of right to legislation by reversing it in accordance with this relation; and (5) derivation of the right to punish. The rest of the doctrine of right merely applies these principles, defining more precisely the boundaries of right and injustice in every possible situation of life, grouped and divided under certain headings. Textbooks of pure right largely agree in these particular doctrines. They differ widely in their principles, since these are always bound to some philosophical system. Having discussed the first four chief points briefly and generally, but definitely and clearly, in accordance with ours, we must speak similarly of the right to punish.

Kant makes the fundamentally false claim that no fully valid right of property exists outside the state. According to our derivation above, property exists even in the state of nature, with a full natural, that is, moral right. It cannot be violated without injustice, and may be defended to the utmost without injustice. Outside the state, however, there is certainly no *right to punish*. Every such right is founded solely on a positive law that has assigned a punishment *before* the offence, its threat being intended as a counter-motive stronger than any possible motive for committing that offence. This positive law must be regarded as sanctioned and acknowledged by all citizens. It thus rests on a common contract whose fulfilment binds the members of the state in all circumstances: on one side to carry out the punishment and on the other to submit to it. Submission may therefore rightly be compelled. Thus the immediate *purpose of punishment* in a particular case is *fulfilment of the law as a contract*. But the sole purpose of the *law* is *deterrence* from violating another's rights. People have united in a state, renounced committing injustice, and accepted the burdens of maintaining the state so that each may be protected from suffering injustice.

Law and its execution, punishment, are therefore essentially directed toward the *future*, not the *past*. This distinguishes *punishment* from *revenge*, which is motivated solely by what has happened, by the past as such. Inflicting pain in retaliation for injustice without any future aim is revenge. It can serve no purpose except to console oneself for one's own suffering by seeing another suffer at one's hand. This is malice and cruelty and cannot be justified ethically. An injustice someone has done to me does not entitle me to do him an injustice. Returning evil for evil without any further aim can be justified neither morally nor on any other rational ground; the *jus talionis* (“law of retaliation”) as an independent, ultimate principle of punishment is meaningless. Kant's theory of punishment as sheer retribution for the sake of retribution is therefore wholly groundless and perverse. It still haunts the works of many jurists under grand phrases that amount to empty words: that punishment atones for the crime, neutralizes it, cancels it, and the like. Yet no one is authorized to set himself up as a purely moral judge and avenger, to visit another's misdeeds upon him by causing him pain and imposing penance. That would be a most presumptuous claim. Hence the biblical saying: “Vengeance is mine, says the Lord, and I will repay.”

Human beings do have the right to provide for society's safety. They can do so only by prohibiting all acts called “criminal,” with threatened punishments serving as counter-motives to prevent them. A threat can be effective only if punishment is carried out when such a case nevertheless occurs. That the purpose of punishment—or more precisely of penal law—is deterrence from crime is so generally acknowledged and self-evident that it is even expressed in the very old English form of indictment still used by the Crown's advocate in criminal cases: *if this be proved, you, the said N. N., ought to be punished with pains of law, to deter others from the like crimes, in all time coming*.[^3][^4] A future purpose distinguishes punishment from revenge. Punishment has that purpose only when carried out *in fulfilment of a law*. By announcing itself through this fulfilment as inevitable in every future case, it preserves the law's deterrent force, its very purpose.

Here a Kantian would certainly object that, on this view, the punished criminal is used “merely as a *means*.” But the maxim repeated tirelessly by all Kantians, that one must always treat a human being only as an end and never as a means, sounds impressive and is therefore particularly useful to those who like a formula that relieves them of further thought. In the light, however, it is extremely vague and indefinite, achieves its purpose only indirectly, and requires special explanation, definition, and modification for every application. Taken generally, it says too little, is inadequate, and is also open to question. A murderer who has incurred the death penalty under the law must now indeed, and quite rightly, be used as a mere *means*. He has disturbed public safety, the principal purpose of the state; if the law remains unfulfilled, he has abolished it. His life and person must now be the *means* of fulfilling the law and thereby restoring public safety. He is rightly made such a means in fulfilment of the social contract that he too entered as a citizen. Under it he pledged his life, liberty, and property to secure those of all, in order to enjoy security for his own. That pledge has now been forfeited.

The theory of punishment presented here, immediately clear to sound reason, is admittedly not, in its essentials, a new thought. New errors had nearly displaced it, which made a clear account necessary. It is substantially contained in what Pufendorf says on the subject in *De officio hominis et civis* (*On the Duty of Man and Citizen*), book 2, chapter 13. Hobbes agrees with it in *Leviathan*, chapters 15 and 28. In our day, Feuerbach has famously defended it. Indeed, it is already present in the pronouncements of ancient philosophers. Plato sets it out clearly in the *Protagoras* (p. 114, Bipont edition), in the *Gorgias* (p. 168), and finally in book eleven of the *Laws* (p. 165). Seneca expresses Plato's view and the whole theory of punishment perfectly in a few words: *Nemo prudens punit, quia peccatum est; sed ne peccetur* (*De Ira*, I, 16; “No wise person punishes because a wrong has been done, but so that a wrong will not be done”).

We have thus come to know the state as the means by which egoism equipped with reason seeks to escape its own harmful consequences when they turn against it, so that each now promotes the welfare of all because he sees his own welfare included in it. If the state fully attained its goal, it might ultimately bring about something approaching a land of plenty, since it can use the combined powers of its members to make the rest of nature more and more serviceable and remove all kinds of evils. Yet it remains very far from this goal. Even if it reached it, countless evils essential to life would still preserve suffering; if all the others were removed, boredom would immediately occupy every place they left. Nor can the state ever wholly abolish conflict among individuals: it teases in small matters where it is forbidden in great ones. Finally Eris, successfully expelled from within, turns outward. Banished by the state from conflict among individuals, she returns from outside as war among nations and demands at once, on a large scale, the bloody sacrifices that prudent arrangements had denied her one by one, as though collecting an accumulated debt. Even if wisdom founded on millennia of experience finally overcame and eliminated all of this, the result would in the end be a genuine overpopulation of the entire planet, whose terrible evils only a bold imagination can now picture.[^5]
