# §. 71.

As I finish this outline of ethics, and with it the whole development of the single thought I set out to communicate, I have no wish to hide an objection to this last part. Rather, I will show that it lies in the nature of the matter and cannot possibly be removed. Our inquiry has ended by bringing before us, in complete holiness, the denial and surrender of all willing and thereby redemption from a world whose whole existence appeared to us as suffering. Yet precisely this now seems to be a passage into empty *nothingness*.

First, the concept of *nothingness* is essentially relative. It refers only to some particular thing that it negates. Some, especially Kant, have ascribed this property only to the *nihil privativum* (relative nothing), represented by a minus in opposition to a plus, which could become plus from the opposite standpoint. They set against it the *nihil negativum* (absolute nothing), which would be nothing in every relation, giving as an example a logical contradiction that cancels itself. On closer examination, however, no absolute nothing, no *nihil negativum* in the strict sense, is even conceivable. Viewed from a higher standpoint or placed under a wider concept, every such nothing is again only a *nihil privativum*. A nothing is nothing only in a relation to something else and therefore presupposes that other thing. Even a logical contradiction is only relatively nothing. It is not a thought of reason, but neither is it absolute nothing. It is a combination of words and an example of what cannot be thought, needed in logic to demonstrate the laws of thought. When we seek such an example for that purpose, we grasp the nonsense as the positive thing sought and pass over sense as the negative. Every *nihil negativum*, or absolute nothing, thus appears as a mere *nihil privativum*, or relative nothing, when subordinated to a higher concept. It can exchange signs with what it negates, so that the other becomes the negation and it becomes the positive. This agrees with Plato's result in the difficult dialectical inquiry into nothing in the *Sophist* (pp. 277–287, Bipont edition; *Sophist* 258e): Τὴν τοῦ ἑτέρου φύσιν ἀποδείξαντες οὖσαν τε, καὶ κατακεκερματισμένην ἐπὶ πάντα τὰ ὄντα πρὸς ἄλληλα, τὸ πρὸς τὸ ὂν ἕκαστον μόριον αὐτῆς ἀντιτιθέμενον, ἐτολμήσαμεν εἰπεῖν, ὡς αὐτὸ τοῦτό ἐστιν ὄντως τὸ μὴ ὄν. (*Cum enim ostenderemus, alterius ipsius naturam esse, perque omnia entia divisam atque dispersam invicem; tunc partem ejus oppositam ei, quod cujusque ens est, esse ipsum revera non ens asseruimus.* “Having shown that the nature of the other exists and is divided and dispersed among all beings in relation to one another, we ventured to say that the part opposed to each being is truly non-being.”)

What is generally taken as positive, what we call *being* and whose negation the concept *nothingness* expresses in its broadest sense, is the world as representation. I have shown it to be the objectivity of the will, its mirror. This will and this world are also ourselves. Representation as such belongs to the world as one of its sides. Its form is space and time, so everything that exists from this standpoint must exist somewhere and sometime. Denial, abolition, and reversal of the will are also the abolition and disappearance of the world, its mirror. When we no longer see the will in that mirror, we ask in vain where it has gone. We lament that it has vanished into nothing, because it no longer has any where or when.

If the opposite standpoint were possible for us, it would reverse the signs, showing what exists for us as nothing and that nothing as what exists. But so long as we are ourselves the will to live, we can know and name the latter only negatively. The old saying of Empedocles, that like is known only by like, deprives us here of all cognition. Conversely, it ultimately grounds the possibility of everything we actually know: the world as representation, or the will's objectivity. For the world is the will's self-knowledge.

If one still insisted on somehow obtaining positive knowledge of what philosophy can express only negatively as denial of the will, we could only point to the state experienced by those who have reached complete denial. It has been called ecstasy, rapture, illumination, union with God, and so on. Yet it cannot properly be called cognition, since it no longer has the form of subject and object. Besides, it is accessible only through one's own experience, which cannot be communicated further.

We, however, must remain wholly at the standpoint of philosophy and be content here with negative cognition, satisfied to have reached the last boundary of positive cognition. We have known the world's essence in itself as will and all its appearances as the will's objectivity, tracing it from the unknowing urge of obscure natural forces to the most fully conscious human action. We do not shrink from the consequence: with the will's free denial and surrender, all those appearances are abolished. The ceaseless pressure and activity without goal or rest on every level of objectivity, in and through which the world exists, is abolished. So is the multiplicity of its successive forms; with the will, its whole appearance is abolished. Finally the general forms of that appearance, time and space, and its most fundamental form, subject and object, are abolished too. No will: no representation, no world.

Only nothingness certainly remains before us. But what resists this dissolution into nothingness is our nature, and that is precisely the will to live. We are that will, as our world is. Our strong aversion to nothingness is simply another expression of how strongly we will life, and that we are nothing but this will and know nothing but it. Yet if we turn from our own poverty and captivity to those who overcame the world, in whom the will, reaching full self-knowledge, found itself in everything and then freely denied itself, and who now only await the disappearance of its last trace along with the body it animates, we see something else. In place of restless pressure and striving, the continual movement from desire to fear and joy to pain, the hope never satisfied and never dying that makes up the willing person's dream of life, there is the peace beyond all reason, the complete calm of a sea without wind in the mind, deep repose, unshakable confidence and serenity. Their mere reflection on a face, as Raphael and Correggio portrayed it, is a complete and certain gospel: cognition alone remains; the will has disappeared. We look on this state with deep and painful longing, and in contrast the misery and hopelessness of our own stands revealed. Nevertheless, this is the only contemplation that can console us enduringly when we have recognized incurable suffering and endless misery as essential to the world, the will's appearance, yet see that world dissolve when the will is abolished and are left before empty nothingness.

By contemplating the lives and conduct of saints—whom we are seldom fortunate enough to meet in our own experience, but whose recorded histories and works of art bearing the stamp of inward truth set before our eyes—we can thus dispel the dark impression of the nothingness hovering as the final goal beyond all virtue and holiness, which we fear like children fear the dark. We do this instead of evading it, as the Indians do through myths and words without meaning such as absorption into *Brahman* or the Buddhists' *Nirvana*. We acknowledge freely: what remains after the complete abolition of the will is indeed nothing for everyone still full of will. But conversely, for those in whom the will has turned and denied itself, this world of ours, so very real, with all its suns and Milky Ways, is—nothing.[^1]

[^1]: This is also the Buddhists' *Prajñāpāramitā*, “beyond all cognition”: the point where subject and object no longer exist. (See J. J. Schmidt, *On the Mahayana and Prajñāpāramitā*.)
