# §. 63.

We have come to know *temporal justice*, which has its seat in the state, as retributive or punitive. We saw that it becomes justice only by regard for the *future*. Without this, every punishment and repayment of an offence would remain unjustified: merely adding a second evil to one already committed, without sense or meaning. But *eternal justice*, which we mentioned earlier, is quite different. It governs the world, not the state; it depends on no human institutions, is subject to neither chance nor deception, and is neither uncertain, wavering, nor mistaken, but infallible, fixed, and sure. The concept of retribution already includes time. Eternal justice therefore cannot be retributive: unlike temporal justice, it can grant no delay or respite, and cannot require time to balance an evil deed with an evil consequence. Here punishment must be so connected with the offence that the two are one.

(“Do you think that crimes fly on wings to the heavenly dwelling, are entered there in Jupiter's records, and that Jupiter reads them and pronounces judgment? The whole expanse of heaven could not contain the deeds of mortals, nor could Jupiter read and punish them all. Yet justice is here, close to us, if we choose to look.”)

Anyone who has grasped the thought developed so far will soon find it perfectly clear that such eternal justice really does lie in the world's essence.

The world, in all the multiplicity of its parts and forms, is the appearance, the objectivity, of the one will to live. Existence itself, and its character in the whole as in every part, comes solely from the will. It is free and all-powerful. In each thing the will appears exactly as it determines itself in itself and outside time. The world is only the mirror of this willing. All the finitude, suffering, and torment it contains belong to the expression of what the will wills; they exist because it wills in this way. Thus every being bears existence in general, and then the existence of its species and its particular individuality, just as they are, in circumstances and a world just as they are, ruled by chance and error, temporal, transient, and always suffering, with the strictest right. In everything that happens to it, or even can happen to it, it always receives its due. For the will is its own, and as the will is, so is the world. Only the world itself can bear responsibility for its existence and character. Who else could have made it out of himself? If one wants to know what human beings are worth as a whole and in general, viewed morally, one should look at their fate as a whole and in general. It is deprivation, misery, distress, torment, and death. Eternal justice rules: if, taken as a whole, they were not worthless, their fate as a whole would not be so wretched. In this sense we can say: the world itself is the Last Judgment. If all the world's misery could be placed in one pan of a balance and all the world's guilt in the other, the scales would surely balance.

Of course, the world does not appear in this way to cognition that has arisen to serve the will and belongs to an individual as such. To the inquirer the world is ultimately revealed as the objectivity of the one and only will to live that he himself is. But, as the Indians say, the veil of Maya clouds the uncultivated individual's sight. Instead of the thing in itself, he sees only appearance in time and space, according to the *principium individuationis* (principle of individuation), and in the other forms of the principle of sufficient reason. Within this limited form of cognition he does not see the essence of things, which is one; he sees its appearances as separate, divided, innumerable, very different, and even opposed. Pleasure seems one thing to him and torment quite another; one person is a tormentor and murderer, another a sufferer and victim; evil deeds are one thing and suffering another. He sees one person living in joy, abundance, and pleasure while another dies painfully of deprivation and cold at his door. Then he asks where retribution is. He himself, in the intense urge of will that is his origin and essence, seizes life's pleasures and enjoyments and clings tightly to them. He does not know that by this very act of willing he also seizes and embraces all the pains and torments of life, at whose sight he shudders. He sees suffering and evil deeds in the world, but is far from recognizing them as different sides of the appearance of the one will to live. He takes them to be very different, even wholly opposed, and often tries to escape suffering in his own person through an evil deed, by causing another to suffer. He remains captive to the *principium individuationis*, deceived by Maya's veil.

On a raging sea, boundless in every direction, where roaring mountains of water rise and fall, a sailor sits in a little boat and trusts his frail craft. Just so the individual sits calmly amid a world of torments, supported by and trusting the *principium individuationis*, the way the individual knows things as appearance. The boundless world, full of suffering everywhere in an infinite past and an infinite future, is alien to him, even a fairy tale. Only his fleeting person, his extensionless present, and his momentary comfort are real to him; he does everything to preserve them, so long as better cognition does not open his eyes. Until then, only in the deepest recess of his consciousness does a very dim foreboding remain: all that suffering may not really be so foreign to him, and may be connected with him in a way from which the *principium individuationis* cannot protect him. From this foreboding comes the ineradicable *dread* common to all human beings, perhaps even to the more intelligent animals. It suddenly seizes them when some chance occurrence makes them doubt the *principium individuationis*, when the principle of sufficient reason seems to admit an exception in one of its forms: when a change seems to occur without a cause, for example, or a dead person seems to return, or the past or future somehow becomes present, or the distant near. Their immense terror arises because they suddenly doubt the forms of cognition belonging to appearance, the forms that alone keep their own individuality separate from the rest of the world. Yet this separation belongs only to appearance, not to the thing in itself. Eternal justice rests on this very point.

Indeed, all temporal happiness and all prudence move across undermined ground. They protect the person from misfortune and provide enjoyment, but the person is mere appearance. His difference from other individuals, and his exemption from the suffering they bear, rest on the form of appearance, the *principium individuationis*. According to the true essence of things, everyone must regard all the world's sufferings as his own, and indeed all possible suffering as actual for himself, so long as he is a firm will to live, affirming life with all his strength. For cognition that sees through the *principium individuationis*, a happy life in time, whether granted by chance or won by prudence, amid the sufferings of countless others is merely a beggar's dream that he is a king. He must wake to discover that only a fleeting illusion separated him from the suffering of his life.

Eternal justice escapes the view captive to cognition that follows the principle of sufficient reason and the *principium individuationis*. This view finds justice wholly absent unless it saves it with fictions. It sees the wicked person live in pleasure after every kind of misdeed and cruelty, then depart this world untroubled. It sees the oppressed drag out a life of suffering to its end without an avenger or one who will repay. But eternal justice can be grasped only by someone who rises above cognition that advances along the thread of the principle of sufficient reason and remains bound to individual things. He knows the Ideas, sees through the *principium individuationis*, and realizes that the thing in itself does not bear the forms of appearance. The same cognition alone enables him to understand the true nature of virtue, which will soon become clear in connection with our present inquiry, though its practice by no means requires this cognition *in abstracto* (in the abstract).

Someone who reaches that cognition sees clearly that, because the will is the in-itself of all appearance, the torment inflicted on others and that experienced oneself, the evil deed and the suffering, always strike one and the same being. Their appearances stand as quite different individuals and may even be separated by remote times and places. He understands that the difference between the person who inflicts suffering and the person who must endure it is only phenomenal; it does not touch the thing in itself. This is the will living in both. Deceived by cognition bound to its service, it fails to recognize itself: seeking greater well-being in *one* of its appearances, it causes great suffering in *another*. In its vehement urge it sinks its teeth into its own flesh, unaware that it always injures only itself. Through individuation it thus reveals the conflict it carries within itself. The tormentor and the tormented are one. The former errs in thinking himself exempt from torment; the latter, in thinking himself exempt from guilt. If both had their eyes opened, the one who inflicted suffering would recognize that he lives in everything suffering torment anywhere in the wide world, and, if endowed with reason, ponders in vain why it was called into existence for such immense suffering without seeing any guilt for it. The tormented person would realize that every evil ever done in the world flows from the will that also constitutes *his* essence and appears in *him*. Through this appearance and its affirmation he has taken upon himself all the sufferings arising from such a will, and rightly endures them so long as he is that will.

The poet Calderón speaks from an intimation of this knowledge in *Life Is a Dream*:

*Pues el delito mayor*<br>
*Del hombre es haber nacido.*

(“For the greatest crime of man is to have been born.”)

How could it not be a crime when an eternal law attaches death to it? In those lines Calderón has merely expressed the Christian dogma of original sin.

Living knowledge of eternal justice—of the balance beam that inseparably joins *malum culpae* (the evil of guilt) with *malo poenae* (the evil of punishment)—requires a complete ascent beyond individuality and the principle that makes it possible. Like the related clear, pure knowledge of the essence of all virtue that we shall discuss next, it will therefore always remain inaccessible to most people. The wise ancestors of the Indian people expressed it directly, as far as concepts and language and their still figurative, rhapsodic manner allowed, in discourses permitted only to the three twice-born castes, that is, in their esoteric teaching. In the popular religion, or exoteric teaching, by contrast, they communicated it only through myth. We find the direct presentation in those discourses, the fruit of the highest human cognition and wisdom, whose core has at last come to us in the Upanishads as the greatest gift of this century. It is expressed in several ways, above all when all the beings of the world, living and lifeless, are shown to the pupil one after another, and over each is spoken the phrase that has become the *Mahavakya*, the great saying: *Tatoumes*, more correctly *tat twam asi*—“This is you.”[^1]

For the people, that great truth was translated, so far as their limitations allowed them to grasp it, into the mode of cognition following the principle of sufficient reason. This mode by its very nature cannot receive the truth purely and in itself; indeed, it directly contradicts it. In myth, however, it received a substitute adequate to guide conduct: a figurative presentation makes the ethical significance of conduct intelligible even in the mode of cognition governed by the principle of sufficient reason, to which that significance is inherently foreign. This is the purpose of all doctrines of faith, which all clothe in myth a truth inaccessible to an untrained human understanding. In Kant's language this myth could even be called a postulate of practical reason. Considered in this way, it has the great advantage of containing no elements beyond those lying visibly before us in reality; all its concepts can therefore be supplied with intuitions. I mean the myth of the transmigration of souls.

It teaches that all the sufferings one inflicts on other beings in this life must be atoned for in a later life in this very world by enduring precisely those sufferings oneself. It goes so far as to teach that even someone who kills an animal will, sometime in infinite time, be born as that very animal and die the same death. It teaches that evil conduct leads to a future life in this world as a suffering and despised being: rebirth in a lower caste, or as a woman, an animal, a pariah or Chandala, a leper, a crocodile, and so forth. The myth illustrates every torment it threatens by beings who suffer in the actual world without knowing why they deserve their suffering. It needs no other hell. As reward, by contrast, it promises rebirth in better and nobler forms, as a Brahmin, a sage, or a saint. The highest reward awaits the noblest acts and complete resignation. It is also promised to a woman who voluntarily dies on her husband's pyre in seven successive lives, and to a person whose pure mouth has never spoken a single lie. The myth can express this reward in the language of this world only negatively, by its frequent promise that one will no longer be born again: *non adsumes iterum existentiam apparentem* (“you will not again assume an apparent existence”). Or as the Buddhists, who recognize neither the Vedas nor castes, put it: “You will attain Nirvana,” a state in which four things do not exist: birth, old age, sickness, and death.

No myth has ever followed, or ever will follow, a philosophical truth accessible to so few as closely as this ancient doctrine of the noblest and oldest people. Despite the ways in which that people has now declined, the doctrine still prevails there as a general popular belief and decisively influences life today as it did four thousand years ago. Pythagoras and Plato already admired that unsurpassable mythical presentation, brought it from India or Egypt, revered and used it, and perhaps even believed it to some degree we cannot determine. Yet we now send English *clergymen* and Moravian linen weavers to the Brahmins, out of pity to teach them better and tell them that they were made from nothing and should gratefully rejoice in it. But we fare like someone firing a ball at a cliff. Our religions will never take root in India: the primeval wisdom of humanity will not be displaced by events in Galilee. Indian wisdom, on the contrary, is flowing back to Europe and will bring a fundamental change to our knowledge and thought.
