# §. 56.

The whole visible world stands as the expression and image of the will's freedom and omnipotence. It is the will's appearance and develops according to the laws inherent in the form of cognition. That freedom can now express itself anew where the will, in its most complete appearance, has attained fully adequate knowledge of its own essence. At the summit of reflection and self-consciousness, it may still will what it formerly willed blindly and without self-knowledge. Cognition then always remains a *motive* for it, in the whole as in particular cases. Or, conversely, this cognition becomes a *quietive* that stills and cancels all willing.

This is the affirmation and denial of the will to live outlined earlier in general terms. As general, not individual, expressions of will concerning a person's way of life, they do not modify the development of character piecemeal or find expression in isolated actions. The maxim the will has freely adopted upon receiving this knowledge is expressed livingly either in the ever stronger emergence of the person's whole previous mode of conduct or in its cancellation. The reflections on freedom, necessity, and character that intervened have prepared the way for a clearer account of all this, the main subject of the final book. We shall prepare it further by postponing it once more and turning first to life itself, whose willing or refusal is the great question. We shall ask in general what affirmation actually brings to the will, which is everywhere the inner essence of life; how far and in what way life satisfies it, or can satisfy it; in short, what we must take to be the will's general and essential condition in this world that is its own in every respect.

First I ask readers to recall the reflection with which we ended Book Two. It arose from a question about the will's goal and purpose. Instead of an answer, we saw that at every grade of its appearance, from the lowest to the highest, the will lacks any final goal or purpose. It always strives because striving is its sole essence. No attained goal ends it. It can never be finally satisfied; it can be checked only by an obstacle and otherwise proceeds without end.

We saw this in gravity, the simplest natural appearance. It never ceases striving or pressing toward a centre without extension, whose attainment would destroy it and matter, even if the whole universe had already gathered into one mass. We see it in other simple natural appearances too. A solid strives to become fluid, whether by melting or dissolving; only then are its chemical forces released. Rigidity is the prison in which cold holds them. Fluid strives to become vapour and passes into it as soon as it is freed from all pressure. No body is without affinity, which is to say without striving, without craving and desire, as Jakob Böhme would put it. Electricity propagates its inner division of itself without limit, even if the earth's mass absorbs the effect. As long as the galvanic pile lasts, galvanism too is a purposeless act of division and reconciliation, ceaselessly renewed.

A plant's existence is likewise restless striving that is never satisfied: a ceaseless drive through ever higher forms until its endpoint, the seed, becomes the starting point again. This repeats endlessly, with no goal, final satisfaction, or resting place anywhere. We shall also recall from Book Two that the many natural forces and organic forms everywhere contend for the matter in which they seek to appear. Each possesses only what it has wrested from another. Thus a continual struggle of life and death is sustained. From it arises, above all, the resistance that everywhere obstructs the striving constituting each thing's innermost essence. Striving presses in vain yet cannot give up its nature. It suffers its way forward until that appearance perishes, when others eagerly seize its place and matter.

We have long recognized that this striving, the core and in-itself of every thing, is the very same as what we call *will* in ourselves, where it appears most clearly in the light of fullest consciousness. When an obstacle comes between it and a temporary goal, we call this obstruction *suffering*. Attaining the goal we call *satisfaction*, well-being, or happiness. We can apply these names to the weaker in degree but identical in essence appearances of the cognitionless world. We then see them caught in continual suffering and without lasting happiness. All striving arises from lack, from dissatisfaction with one's state, and is therefore suffering until satisfied. Yet no satisfaction endures; each is merely the starting point for new striving. Everywhere we see striving obstructed in many ways and therefore struggling, so it remains suffering throughout. With no final goal to striving, there is no limit or end to suffering.

What careful, strenuous attention alone discovers in nature without cognition meets us clearly in nature that cognizes, in animal life, whose constant suffering is easy to show. Without pausing at this intermediate grade, let us turn to where all this emerges most distinctly under the brightest light of cognition: human life. As the will's appearance becomes more complete, suffering too becomes more evident. Plants have no sensibility and thus no pain. The lowest animals, such as infusoria and radiates, undergo only a very slight degree of suffering. Even insects have a limited capacity for sensation and suffering. Only with the fully formed nervous system of vertebrates does it reach a high degree, and it rises further as intelligence develops. In proportion as cognition becomes clear and consciousness intensifies, suffering increases. It therefore reaches its highest degree in the human being, and there increases again with clearer cognition and greater intelligence: the person of genius suffers most.

In this sense—referring to the degree of cognition generally, not merely abstract knowledge—I understand and use the saying from Ecclesiastes: `Qui auget scientiam, auget et dolorem` (Who increases knowledge increases sorrow). The close relation between the degree of consciousness and the degree of suffering was beautifully made visible in a drawing by that philosophical painter, or painting philosopher, *Tischbein*. The upper half shows women whose children are being taken from them. In several groups and postures they express the depths of maternal pain, anguish, and despair in varied ways. The lower half shows sheep from whom their lambs are being taken, arranged and grouped in exactly the same way. Each human head and posture above has an animal counterpart below. We see plainly how the pain possible in dim animal consciousness compares with the terrible suffering made possible only by distinct cognition and clear consciousness.

We shall therefore consider the will's inner and essential fate in *human existence*. Everyone will readily find the same thing, expressed more weakly at different degrees, in animal life. Suffering animals, too, provide ample conviction that *all life is essentially suffering*.
