Turning now from the visual arts to *poetry*, after our reflections on art in general, we can have no doubt that poetry too aims to reveal the Ideas, the grades of the will's objectivation, and to communicate them to the listener with the clarity and vividness with which the poet's mind apprehended them. Ideas are essentially intuitive. Words in poetry immediately communicate only abstract concepts, but the clear intention is to let the listener intuit the Ideas of life in the representatives of those concepts. This can happen only with the aid of the listener's own imagination.

To set imagination in motion for this purpose, the abstract concepts that form the immediate material of poetry, as of the driest prose, must be arranged so that their spheres intersect. None can then remain in its abstract generality; an intuitive representative takes its place before the imagination, and the poet's words go on modifying this representative according to the poet's intention. A chemist produces solid precipitates by combining perfectly clear and transparent liquids. In much the same way, the poet knows how to precipitate the concrete and individual, the intuitive representation, from the abstract, transparent generality of concepts through the way they are combined. Only intuitively is the Idea cognized, and cognition of the Idea is the purpose of every art. Mastery of poetry, as of chemistry, enables one to obtain precisely the precipitate intended each time.

The many epithets in poetry serve this end. They increasingly restrict the generality of a concept until intuition is reached. Homer attaches an adjective to almost every noun. Its concept intersects and at once considerably narrows the sphere of the noun's concept, bringing it that much closer to intuition. For example:

> `Ἐν δ’ ἔπεσ’ Ὠκεανῷ λαμπρὸν φάος ἠελίοιο,`<br>
> `Ἕλκον νύκτα μέλαιναν ἐπὶ ζείδωρον ἄρουραν`<br>
> (`Occidit vero in Oceanum splendidum lumen solis,`<br>
> `Trahens noctem nigram super almam terram`.)
>
> Translation: The splendid light of the sun sank into Ocean, drawing black night over the life-giving earth.

And these lines precipitate the whole delight of the southern climate before the imagination from a few concepts:

> “A gentle wind blows from the blue sky;<br>
> The myrtle stands still, the laurel tall.”

Rhythm and rhyme are very special aids to poetry. I know no other explanation for their incredibly powerful effect than that our powers of representation, essentially bound to time, have the peculiarity of inwardly following every regularly recurring sound and, as it were, joining in. Rhythm and rhyme thus partly bind our attention, making us follow the recital more willingly. They also create in us a blind assent to what is said, prior to every judgment, giving it a certain emphatic power to convince independent of any reasons.

Because the material poetry uses to communicate Ideas—concepts—is general, poetry's domain is very large. It can present all nature, Ideas at every grade, proceeding now by description, now by narration, and now by direct dramatic presentation, according to the Idea to be communicated. In portraying the lower grades of the will's objecthood, the visual arts usually surpass poetry, since nature without cognition, and even merely animal nature, reveals almost its whole essence in a single well-grasped moment. Human beings, however, express themselves not merely through form and facial expression but through a chain of actions and the thoughts and emotions accompanying them. They are therefore poetry's principal subject. No other art equals it here, for poetry can unfold its subject through a progression that is denied to the visual arts.[^text]

Poetry's great subject is thus the revelation of the Idea that forms the highest grade of the will's objecthood: the presentation of human beings in the connected series of their strivings and actions. Experience and history also teach us about human beings, but more often about *people* than about *the human being*. They give us empirical information about how people behave toward one another, from which rules for our own conduct may be drawn, more often than they offer deep insight into humanity's inner nature. Such insight is not excluded from them, however. Whenever the essence of humanity itself is disclosed to us in history or our own experience, we or the historian have already apprehended these with an artist's eyes, poetically: according to the Idea rather than the appearance, the inner essence rather than the relations. Personal experience is indispensable for understanding both poetry and history; it is, so to speak, the dictionary of the language they speak.

History stands to poetry as portrait painting stands to history painting. The former gives what is true of the individual; the latter, what is true in general. One has the truth of appearance and can attest it from appearance; the other has the truth of the Idea, which is found in no single appearance yet speaks from them all. The poet deliberately selects and presents significant characters in significant situations. The historian takes both as they come. Indeed, the historian must regard and select events and persons not for their inner, genuine significance expressing the Idea, but for their outer, apparent, relative importance in connections and consequences. Nothing may be considered in itself, by its essential character and expression. Everything must be viewed relationally, in the chain of events, through its influence on what follows, especially on the historian's own age.

Thus the historian must not omit a king's action that is insignificant, even commonplace, in itself: it has consequences and influence. Conversely, the actions of individuals highly significant in themselves, and even exceptional individuals, go unmentioned if they have no consequences or influence. Historical inquiry follows the principle of sufficient reason and grasps appearance, whose form that principle is. The poet apprehends the Idea, the essence of humanity apart from every relation and every time: the adequate objecthood of the thing in itself at its highest grade.

Even in the historian's necessary manner of looking, the inner essence, the significance of appearances, the kernel within all those shells, can never be wholly lost. At least someone who seeks it can still find and recognize it. Nevertheless, what is significant in itself rather than through relation—the actual unfolding of the Idea—will be found far more accurately and clearly in poetry than in history. Paradoxical as it sounds, poetry therefore deserves far more attribution of proper, genuine, inner truth. The historian must follow an individual event exactly as it occurred, developing in time through the many entangled chains of grounds and consequences. Yet the historian cannot possibly possess all the data, have witnessed everything, or have investigated everything. At every moment the original of the portrait is lost or replaced by a false one. This happens so often that I think it reasonable to suppose there is more falsehood than truth in all history.

The poet, by contrast, has apprehended the Idea of humanity from the particular side to be presented. It is the essence of the poet's own self that becomes objective to him in it. His cognition is, as explained above concerning sculpture, half *a priori* (prior to experience). His model stands before his mind, firm, distinct, and brightly lit, and cannot desert him. In the mirror of his mind he therefore shows us the Idea purely and clearly, and his depiction is true to life down to the particulars.[^2][^3]

The great ancient historians are therefore poets in their details where the data fail them—for example, in their heroes' speeches. Indeed, their whole handling of their material approaches epic poetry. This gives their accounts unity and lets them retain inner truth even when outer truth was unavailable or actually distorted. We compared history with portrait painting and poetry with history painting. The ancient historians also followed Winckelmann's maxim that the portrait should be the ideal of the individual: they present the particular in such a way that the side of the Idea of humanity expressed in it comes forward. Modern historians, with few exceptions, give us mostly “a rubbish bin and junk room, and at most a major affair of state.”

Anyone who wishes to know humanity according to its inner essence, identical through all appearances and developments—according to its Idea—will find a far more faithful and distinct image in the works of the great, immortal poets than historians can ever offer. Even the best historians are far from being the greatest poets, and their hands are not free. We can illuminate the relation by a comparison. A historian who works solely from data resembles a person with no knowledge of mathematics who measures the relations between accidentally discovered figures. The resulting empirical measurements bear all the errors of the drawn figure. The poet resembles a mathematician who constructs those relations *a priori* in pure intuition and states them as they are in the Idea the drawing is meant to make sensible, not as they happen to be in the drawing. Hence Schiller says:

> “What has never happened anywhere, at any time—<br>
> That alone never grows old.”

For knowledge of humanity's essence, I must even grant biographies, particularly autobiographies, more value than history proper, at least as history is usually practised. In biographies the data can be gathered more accurately and completely. In history proper, moreover, it is not so much people as peoples and armies that act. The individuals who still appear stand far away amid a great setting and retinue, veiled in stiff official dress or heavy, inflexible armour. It is truly hard to discern human movement through all of that. By contrast, a faithful account of an individual's life within a narrow sphere shows the ways people act in all their nuances and forms: the excellence, virtue, even holiness of a few; the perversity, wretchedness, and malice of most; the depravity of some.

From the only standpoint considered here—the inner significance of what appears—it makes no difference whether the objects around which actions turn are relatively trivial or important, farms or kingdoms. In themselves all these things have no significance. They acquire it only because and insofar as the will is moved by them. A motive has significance solely through its relation to the will; its relations as a thing to other things do not enter into consideration. A circle one inch in diameter and one forty million miles across have exactly the same geometrical properties. Likewise, events and history in a village and in a kingdom are essentially the same. We can study and come to know humanity from either.

It is also wrong to suppose that autobiographies are full of lies and pretence. Lying, though possible everywhere, may be harder there than anywhere else. Pretence is easiest in mere conversation. Paradoxical as it sounds, it is already harder in a letter: left alone with himself, the writer looks inward rather than outward. What is foreign and distant is hard for him to bring near, and he has no measure before him of the impression made on the recipient. The recipient, meanwhile, calmly reads the letter in a mood foreign to the writer's, perhaps several times and at different times, and easily discovers the hidden intention. A book is likewise the easiest way to come to know its author as a human being, since all these conditions act still more strongly and persistently. Pretence is so difficult in an autobiography that perhaps not a single one is, on the whole, less truthful than any other written history.

Someone recording his life surveys it as a whole and on a large scale. Details grow small, what is near recedes, what is distant draws near again, and considerations of self-interest shrink. He has willingly sat down to confess to himself. The spirit of falsehood does not take hold of him so easily here. Every person also has an inclination toward truth, which must be overcome for any lie and which takes an unusually strong position here.

Another comparison makes the relation between biography and national history intuitive. History shows us humanity as a view from a high mountain shows us nature: we see much at once, broad tracts and great masses, but nothing distinctly or in its full, true essence. The life of an individual, by contrast, shows us the human being as we come to know nature by walking among its trees, plants, rocks, and waters. In landscape painting the artist lets us look upon nature through his eyes and thus greatly eases our cognition of its Ideas and the state of pure, will-less cognition required for it. Poetry has a similar advantage over both history and biography in presenting the Ideas we seek in them. Genius holds up a clarifying mirror in which all that is essential and significant is gathered and placed in the clearest light, while the accidental and extraneous is removed.[^4]

The poet can present the Idea of humanity in two ways. The person presented may also be the one presenting: this occurs in lyric poetry, in the song proper, where the poet vividly contemplates and describes only his own state. This genre therefore necessarily has a certain subjectivity by virtue of its subject. Or the person presented may be wholly distinct from the one presenting, as in every other genre. There the presenter hides to a greater or lesser degree behind what is presented, ultimately disappearing altogether. In a ballad the presenter still expresses his own state to some extent through the tone and bearing of the whole. Though much more objective than the song, it retains something subjective. This recedes further in the idyll, much further in the novel, almost wholly in the epic proper, and down to its last trace in drama—the most objective and, in more than one respect, the most perfect as well as the most difficult kind of poetry.

For the same reason the lyric genre is the easiest. Although art generally belongs only to rare, genuine genius, even someone not especially outstanding can produce a beautiful song if a strong external stimulus genuinely inspires and heightens his mental powers. It requires only vivid contemplation of one's own state in an excited moment. Many individual songs by otherwise unknown people demonstrate this, especially German folk songs, of which *Des Knaben Wunderhorn* offers an excellent collection, and countless popular love songs and other songs in every language. Grasping a moment's mood and embodying it in a song is the whole accomplishment of this poetic genre.

Yet in the lyric poetry of genuine poets, the inner life of all humanity is reflected. Whatever millions of people past, present, and future have felt and will feel in the same, constantly recurring situations finds its fitting expression there. Since those situations recur continually, they endure like humanity itself and always stir the same feelings. The lyric works of genuine poets therefore remain true, effective, and fresh for thousands of years. Indeed, the poet is the universal human being. Everything that has ever stirred a human heart, everything human nature brings forth in any situation, everything that dwells and broods in a human breast anywhere is the poet's theme and material—as is all the rest of nature. The poet can sing sensual pleasure as readily as mysticism, can be Anacreon or Angelus Silesius, write tragedy or comedy, and depict a sublime or a common disposition, according to inclination and calling. No one may therefore prescribe that the poet be noble and sublime, moral, pious, Christian, or anything else, much less reproach him for being one thing and not another. He is humanity's mirror, bringing to its consciousness what it feels and what drives it.

Let us consider the nature of the song proper more closely. Take excellent and pure examples, rather than works already approaching another genre, such as the ballad, elegy, hymn, or epigram. We shall find that the song's distinctive nature in the strict sense is as follows. The subject of willing—one's own willing—fills the singer's consciousness, sometimes as willing released and satisfied (joy), perhaps still more often as willing obstructed (sorrow), but always as emotion, passion, or a stirred state of mind. Yet alongside this, and at the same time, the sight of surrounding nature makes the singer conscious of himself as the subject of pure, will-less cognition. Its unshakable, blissful calm now contrasts with the urge of willing, always confined and always still in need. What the song as a whole expresses, and what constitutes the lyric state, is the feeling of this contrast and interplay.

In that state pure cognition seems to approach us to redeem us from willing and its urgency. We follow, but only for moments. Willing—the memory of our personal ends—repeatedly tears us away from calm contemplation. Yet the next beautiful sight in our surroundings, offering pure, will-less cognition, draws us from willing once more. Thus in the song and the lyric mood, willing (personal interest in ends) and pure intuition of the surroundings mingle wonderfully. Relations between them are sought and imagined. The subjective mood, the affection of the will, colours the surroundings we see; these in turn reflect their colour back upon that mood. A genuine song is the imprint of this whole mixed and divided state of mind.

Examples can make this abstract analysis of a state remote from all abstraction understandable. One may take any of Goethe's immortal songs. For this purpose I particularly recommend “The Shepherd's Lament,” “Welcome and Farewell,” “To the Moon,” “On the Lake,” and “Autumn Feeling.” The songs proper in *Des Knaben Wunderhorn* are also excellent examples, especially the one beginning “O Bremen, now I must leave you.” I know a comic parody by Voss that captures the lyric character exactly. He describes the feelings of a drunken roofer falling from a tower. As he falls, he makes an observation wholly foreign to his situation and therefore belonging to will-free cognition: the tower clock shows half past eleven.

Anyone who shares this view of the lyric state will also agree that it is intuitive, poetic cognition of the proposition I set out in my essay on the principle of sufficient reason and have mentioned in this work: the identity of the subject of cognition with the subject of willing may be called the miracle *par excellence* (`κατ’ ἐξοχήν`—above all others). The song's poetic effect ultimately rests on the truth of that proposition. In the course of life these two subjects—or, in popular language, head and heart—separate more and more. We increasingly distinguish subjective feeling from objective cognition. In a child the two are still wholly fused. The child scarcely distinguishes himself from his surroundings, but blends into them. In a young person every perception first produces feeling and mood, and even mingles with them, as *Byron* beautifully expresses it:

> `I live not in myself, but I become`<br>
> `Portion of that around me; and to me`<br>
> `High mountains are a feeling`[^5]
>
> Translation: I do not live in myself alone; I become part of my surroundings, and high mountains are something I feel.

This is why the young person is so attached to the intuitive outer aspect of things, and why youth is suited only to lyric poetry, whereas maturity is suited to drama. We can imagine an old person at most as an epic poet, like Ossian or Homer, for narration belongs to the character of old age.

In the more objective kinds of poetry, especially novel, epic, and drama, revelation of the Idea of humanity is achieved chiefly by two means: the accurate, deeply grasped presentation of significant characters and the invention of significant situations in which they develop. A chemist must not only present simple substances and their principal compounds in a pure, genuine form, but expose them to reagents that bring out their distinctive properties clearly and strikingly. Likewise, the poet must do more than place significant characters before us as truly and faithfully as nature itself. To make them known, the poet must set them in situations where their distinctive features unfold completely and they appear clearly, in sharp outline. These are called significant situations.

In real life and history, chance rarely produces situations of this kind. When it does, they stand isolated, lost and concealed amid a multitude of insignificant events. The sustained significance of situations, as much as the selection and combination of significant characters, should distinguish novel, epic, and drama from real life. Yet strict truth is indispensable to the effect of both. Characters lacking unity, contradicting themselves or human nature in general, and impossible or nearly improbable events, even in secondary details, offend us in poetry as much as misshapen figures, false perspective, or faulty lighting do in painting. In both we ask for a faithful mirror of life, humanity, and the world, only made clearer by presentation and more significant by selection and arrangement.

All arts have one purpose: to present the Ideas. They differ essentially only in the grade of the will's objectivation belonging to the Idea they present, which in turn determines their material. We can therefore clarify even the most distant arts by comparing them. To apprehend fully the Ideas expressed in water, for example, seeing it in a still pond or an evenly flowing stream is not enough. Those Ideas unfold completely only when water appears under all the conditions and obstructions that act upon it and call forth the full expression of its properties. We therefore find it beautiful when it plunges, roars, foams, springs upward again, dissolves into spray as it falls, or, forced by art, rises as a jet. It behaves differently in different conditions, yet always remains true to its character. Springing up is as natural to it as lying still and reflecting. It is equally ready for either when the circumstances arise.

What the maker of waterworks achieves with fluid matter, the architect achieves with rigid matter, and the epic or dramatic poet with the Idea of humanity. To unfold and clarify the Idea expressed in the object of each art, the will objectivating itself at each grade, is the common purpose of all the arts. Human life as usually seen in reality resembles water as usually seen in pond and stream. In epic, novel, and tragedy, however, selected characters are placed in circumstances that unfold all their distinctive features. The depths of the human mind open and become visible in extraordinary, significant actions. Thus poetry objectivates the Idea of the human being, whose distinguishing feature is to appear in highly individual characters.

Tragedy is regarded, and must be regarded, as poetry's summit, both for the magnitude of its effect and the difficulty of its achievement. For our whole inquiry it is highly significant that this supreme poetic achievement aims to present life's terrible side. It shows us nameless pain, humanity's misery, the triumph of wickedness, the mocking rule of chance, and the hopeless downfall of the just and innocent. This offers a profound indication of the nature of the world and existence. The will's conflict with itself, fully unfolded at the highest grade of its objecthood, emerges here in terror.

We see this conflict in human suffering. It arises partly from chance and error, which appear as rulers of the world and are personified as fate because their malice almost seems deliberate. Partly it arises from humanity itself: from the intersecting strivings of individual wills and the wickedness and perversity of most people. One and the same will lives and appears in all of them, yet its appearances fight and tear one another apart. It emerges powerfully in one individual and more weakly in another; here it is brought more fully, there less fully, to reflection and moderated by the light of cognition.

At last, in one individual, this cognition, purified and intensified by suffering itself, reaches a point where appearance, the veil of Maya, no longer deceives it. It sees through the form of appearance, the *principium individuationis* (principle of individuation), and egoism founded on it dies. The formerly powerful *motives* then lose their power. In their place complete cognition of the world's essence acts as a *quietive* of the will, bringing resignation: the abandonment not merely of life but of the whole will to live itself.

Thus in tragedy we finally see the noblest people, after long struggle and suffering, renounce forever the goals they had pursued so fiercely and all the pleasures of life, or freely and gladly give up life itself. So it is with Calderón's *Steadfast Prince*, Gretchen in *Faust*, and Hamlet, whose Horatio would willingly follow him but whom Hamlet commands to remain and breathe painfully a while longer in this harsh world, to tell his fate and clear his memory. So too with *The Maid of Orleans* and *The Bride of Messina*. They all die purified by suffering, meaning that the will to live has already died within them. Voltaire's *Mahomet* even says this expressly in Palmira's dying words to Mahomet: “The world is for tyrants: live, then!”

The demand for so-called poetic justice, by contrast, rests on a complete misunderstanding of tragedy's nature, indeed of the world's nature. It comes forward in all its shallow boldness in Dr Samuel Johnson's criticism of Shakespeare's plays. He quite naively complains that poetic justice is consistently neglected—and indeed it is: for what wrong have Ophelia, Desdemona, or Cordelia done? Only a shallow, optimistic, Protestant rationalist—or, strictly speaking, Jewish—view of the world demands poetic justice and finds satisfaction when it is supplied. The true meaning of tragedy is the deeper insight that what the hero atones for is not his individual sins but original sin: the guilt of existence itself. Calderón says this directly:

> `Pues el delito mayor`<br>
> `Del hombre es haber nacido`
>
> Translation: For the greatest crime of a human being is to have been born.

I shall make just one more observation about the handling of tragedy. Presenting a great misfortune is essential to it. The various ways a poet brings this about fall into three kinds. First, an extraordinary wickedness, bordering on the utmost limits of possibility, may belong to a character who causes the misfortune. Examples are Richard III, Iago in *Othello*, Shylock in *The Merchant of Venice*, Franz Moor, Euripides' Phaedra, Creon in *Antigone*, and others.

Second, blind fate—chance or error—may bring the misfortune. Sophocles' *Oedipus the King* is a true model, as are *The Women of Trachis* and most ancient tragedies generally. Modern examples include *Romeo and Juliet*, Voltaire's *Tancred*, and *The Bride of Messina*.

Third, the mere positions of the characters relative to one another, their relationships, may bring it about. No monstrous error, unheard-of accident, or character reaching the limits of human evil is needed. Characters who are morally ordinary are placed in circumstances that occur frequently, yet their situation forces them knowingly and consciously to cause one another the greatest harm, without injustice lying exclusively on either side.

This last kind seems far preferable to the other two. It shows us the greatest misfortune not as an exception brought on by rare circumstances or monstrous characters, but as something arising easily and of itself from human actions and characters, almost as though it were essential to them. This brings it terribly close to us. In the other two kinds, mighty fate and dreadful wickedness appear as frightening powers threatening us only from afar; we might escape them without taking refuge in renunciation. The last kind shows us powers destructive of happiness and life to which a path toward us lies open at every moment. The greatest suffering is caused by entanglements that could substantially be our own fate and by actions we ourselves might be capable of committing, so that we could not complain of injustice. We shudder to feel ourselves already in the midst of hell.

This last kind is also the hardest to execute. The poet must achieve the greatest effect with the least expenditure of means and motives, solely through the characters' placement and arrangement. Many of the best tragedies therefore avoid this difficulty. A perfect model is a play surpassed in other respects by several works of the same great master: *Clavigo*. *Hamlet* belongs to this kind to some degree if we consider only Hamlet's relations with Laertes and Ophelia. *Wallenstein* has this advantage too. *Faust* belongs to it entirely if we take the episode involving Gretchen and her brother as the main action. So does Corneille's *Le Cid*, except that it lacks the tragic ending found in Max's analogous relationship with Thekla.[^6]
