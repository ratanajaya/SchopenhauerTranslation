The preceding reflections emphasized the subjective part of aesthetic pleasure: delight in sheer intuitive cognition as such, in contrast to willing. Closely connected with them is an explanation of the state of mind called the feeling of the *sublime*.

As noted above, we enter the state of pure contemplation most easily when objects meet us halfway: their varied yet definite and distinct forms readily become representatives of their Ideas. This is what beauty consists in on its objective side. Beautiful nature has this quality above all, and draws at least a passing aesthetic pleasure from even the least receptive person. The plant world in particular so strikingly invites aesthetic contemplation, almost pressing itself upon us, that one might be tempted to connect this with the fact that plants, unlike animal bodies, are not themselves immediate objects of cognition. They need another, intelligent individual to bring them from the world of blind willing into that of representation, and seem, as it were, to long for this entry, so as to gain indirectly what is denied them directly. I leave this daring thought, perhaps bordering on extravagance, entirely open; only a deeply intimate and devoted contemplation of nature could give rise to it or justify it.[^1]

So long as nature's invitation, the significance and clarity of its forms through which the Ideas individualized in them speak readily to us, draws us from cognition of mere relations in service of the will into aesthetic contemplation, thereby raising us to the will-free subject of cognition, it is simply the *beautiful* that affects us. The feeling aroused is that of beauty.

But suppose the very objects whose significant shapes invite us to contemplate them purely stand in a hostile relation to the human will as it appears in its objecthood, the human body. They oppose it, threatening it with a power that overwhelms all resistance, or reducing it to nothing before their immeasurable size. Suppose the viewer nevertheless does not direct attention to this forceful, hostile relation to the will. Though perceiving and acknowledging it, the viewer consciously turns away from it, forcibly detaches from the will and its relations, and, given over wholly to cognition, calmly contemplates these objects so fearful to the will as the pure, will-less subject of cognition. Apprehending only their Idea, outside every relation, the viewer willingly dwells on them and is thereby raised above the self, the person, its willing, and all willing. Then the feeling of the *sublime* fills the viewer. This is a state of elevation, and for that reason the object giving rise to it is called *sublime*.

The difference between the feelings of beauty and sublimity is therefore this. In beauty, pure cognition gains the upper hand without struggle. The object's beauty—its capacity to make cognition of its Idea easier—removes the will and the cognition of relations that serves it from consciousness without resistance and almost unnoticed. Only the pure subject of cognition remains, without even a memory of the will. In the sublime, by contrast, pure cognition is reached only by a conscious and forceful detachment from the object's recognized adverse relations to the will: a free, deliberate rising above the will and the cognition related to it.

This elevation must be consciously achieved and maintained. It therefore carries a continuing awareness of the will, though not of any particular individual willing, such as fear or desire. It is an awareness of human willing in general, expressed universally through its objecthood, the human body. If an actual individual act of will entered consciousness because the object posed a real, personal threat or danger, that aroused will would immediately take command. Calm contemplation would become impossible, and the impression of the sublime would yield to fear, in which the individual's effort to save itself displaces every other thought.

Examples will make this account of the aesthetically sublime clearer and show the different degrees of the feeling. In its essential features—the pure, will-free cognition and the accompanying cognition of Ideas outside every relation determined by the principle of sufficient reason—the sublime is one with beauty. It differs only by the added elevation above the contemplated object's recognized hostility to the will in general. The sublime accordingly has several degrees and passes by gradations out of beauty, depending on whether this added element is strong and urgent and close at hand, or weak, distant, and merely suggested. I shall begin with the transitions and weaker degrees, although readers with little aesthetic receptivity or a less lively imagination may understand only the later, more pronounced examples. They can attend to those and leave the first, faint examples aside.

The human being is at once an impetuous, dark urge to will, whose focal point is marked by the pole of the genitals, and an eternal, free, serene subject of pure cognition, marked by the pole of the brain. Correspondingly, the sun is both the source of *light*, the condition of the most perfect mode of cognition and so of the most delightful thing, and the source of *warmth*, the primary condition of life, that is, of every appearance of the will at its higher grades. What warmth is to the will, light is to cognition. Light is therefore the greatest jewel in beauty's crown and has a decisive influence on the cognition of every beautiful object. Its presence is indispensable, and a favourable angle heightens even the greatest beauty. Above all, light enhances architectural beauty; by its favour it can turn even the most insignificant thing into a beautiful object.

Now imagine a hard winter, when all nature is frozen, and the rays of a low sun reflected from masses of stone. They illuminate without warming: they favour only the purest mode of cognition, not the will. Contemplating their beautiful effect on the stones puts us, as beauty always does, into the state of pure cognition. Yet the faint reminder that these very rays lack life-giving warmth already calls for a certain elevation above the will's interests. It gently asks us to remain in pure cognition and turn away from all willing. Thus the feeling begins to pass from the beautiful to the sublime. This is the faintest breath of sublimity upon beauty, which itself appears here only in a slight degree. The next example is almost as faint.

Imagine a very solitary region under a wholly cloudless sky, with an unbounded horizon: trees and plants in still air, no animals, no people, no moving water, the deepest silence. Such surroundings seem to call us to seriousness and contemplation, to release ourselves from all willing and its poverty. This alone gives that lonely, deeply quiet setting a tinge of the sublime. It offers the will, which always needs to strive and attain, no favourable or unfavourable object. Nothing remains but pure contemplation. Anyone incapable of it is surrendered to the emptiness of an idle will and the torment of boredom, in a humiliation that reveals the person's limitations. Such surroundings therefore measure our own intellectual worth; in general, our ability to endure or love solitude is a good measure of it. This is a low degree of the sublime: the repose and self-sufficiency of pure cognition is set against a reminder of the dependence and poverty of the will, which needs constant activity. This is the kind of sublimity praised in the view over the endless prairies of inland North America.

Now remove even the plants from such a region, leaving only bare rock. The total absence of the organic things needed for our subsistence directly alarms the will. Desolation takes on a fearful character, and our mood becomes more tragic. The rise to pure cognition demands a more decisive detachment from the will's interests. If we remain in that state, the feeling of the sublime comes forward clearly.

It can arise more strongly still in a scene of nature in tumult: threatening black storm clouds casting shifting light and shadow; immense bare rocks overhanging one another and shutting off the view; roaring, foaming waters; utter desolation; wind wailing through the ravines. Our dependence and our struggle with hostile nature, our will thwarted by it, stand vividly before us. Yet so long as personal distress does not gain the upper hand and we remain in aesthetic contemplation, the pure subject of cognition looks out through that struggle and that image of a thwarted will. Calm and unshaken, untouched by the threat (`unconcerned`, or unaffected), it apprehends the Ideas in the very objects so frightening to the will. The feeling of the sublime lies precisely in this contrast.

The impression becomes still stronger when we see the struggle of unleashed natural forces on a vast scale: a plunging torrent whose roar makes it impossible to hear our own voice, or a wide sea in a storm. Waves as high as houses rise and fall, crash against steep cliffs, and fling spray into the air. The wind howls, the sea roars, lightning flashes from black clouds, and thunderclaps drown out wind and sea. In the unshaken spectator, the duality of consciousness now becomes clearest. One feels oneself to be an individual, a frail appearance of the will that the slightest blow from those forces could shatter: helpless before mighty nature, dependent and exposed to chance, a vanishing nothing beside immeasurable powers. At the same time, one feels oneself to be the eternal, tranquil subject of cognition. As the condition of the object, this subject is the bearer of that entire world; nature's terrible struggle is only its representation, while it remains in calm apprehension of the Ideas, free and remote from all willing and all distress. This is the full impression of the sublime, prompted here by the sight of a power incomparably greater than the individual and threatening its destruction.

The sublime can arise in a quite different way when we consider mere magnitude in space or time, whose immensity reduces the individual to nothing. Retaining Kant's terms and his sound division, we may call the first kind the *dynamically sublime* and the second the *mathematically sublime*. Our explanation of the inner nature of the impression, however, differs entirely from his: we assign no part in it to moral reflections or to entities invented by scholastic philosophy.

When we lose ourselves in contemplating the world's infinite extent in space and time, thinking of the millennia past and those to come, or when the night sky brings countless worlds before our eyes and their immensity presses upon consciousness, we feel reduced to nothing. As an individual, a living body, a transient appearance of the will, we seem to vanish like a drop in the ocean and dissolve into nothing. Yet against this spectre of our own insignificance, this deceptive impossibility, there arises the immediate consciousness that all these worlds exist only in our representation: they are modifications of the eternal subject of pure cognition, which we find ourselves to be when we forget individuality. That subject is the necessary bearer and condition of all worlds and all times. The vast world that troubled us now rests within us; our dependence on it is cancelled by its dependence on us.

This does not immediately enter reflective thought. Rather, it appears as a felt awareness that in some sense—one that philosophy alone can make clear—we are one with the world, and so its immensity lifts us instead of crushing us. It is a felt awareness of what the Upanishads of the Vedas express in many forms, especially in a saying cited earlier: `Hae omnes creaturae in totum ego sum, et praeter me aliud ens non est` (All these creatures, taken together, are I, and apart from me there is no other being; *Oupnek'hat*, vol. 1, p. 122). This is an elevation above our own individuality: the feeling of the sublime.

We can receive an immediate impression of the mathematically sublime from a space small beside the universe, provided we can perceive its full dimensions directly and its scale makes our own body seem almost infinitely small. Empty space cannot do this for perception, and thus neither can an open space. The space must be bounded in every dimension so that we can perceive it as a whole: a very high, vast vault such as that of St Peter's in Rome or St Paul's in London. Here the feeling of the sublime arises from our awareness of how nearly our body vanishes before a magnitude that, on the other hand, exists only in our representation, whose bearer we are as knowing subject. Here, as everywhere, the feeling comes from the contrast between our insignificance and dependence as individuals, appearances of the will, and our consciousness of ourselves as pure subjects of cognition. Even the vault of the starry sky, when contemplated without reflection, affects us as a stone vault does: by its apparent size, not its true size. Some objects stir the sublime because their spatial magnitude and great age, their duration in time, make us feel reduced to nothing while we still revel in looking at them. Such are very high mountains, Egyptian pyramids, and colossal ruins of antiquity.

Our account of the sublime can also be carried into ethics, to what is called a sublime character. This too arises when objects fully capable of stirring the will fail to do so because cognition retains the upper hand. A person of this character views other people purely objectively, not according to how they might relate to his own will. He notices their faults, even their hatred and injustice toward him, without himself being roused to hatred. He sees their happiness without envy, recognizes their good qualities without wanting a closer association, and perceives the beauty of women without desiring them. His own happiness and misfortune affect him little. He is, rather, like Horatio as Hamlet describes him:
