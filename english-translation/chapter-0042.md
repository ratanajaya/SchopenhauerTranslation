# §. 40.

Because opposites illuminate one another, this is the place to note that the true opposite of the sublime is something one would hardly recognize as such at first: the *alluring*. By this I mean what excites the will by directly holding out to it satisfaction and fulfilment. The feeling of the sublime arose when an object plainly hostile to the will became the object of pure contemplation. That contemplation could be sustained only by continually turning away from the will and rising above its interests; this is precisely what makes the state sublime. The alluring, by contrast, draws the viewer down from the pure contemplation required to apprehend beauty. It necessarily excites the will with objects that appeal to it directly. The viewer is then no longer a pure subject of cognition but a needy, dependent subject of willing.

People commonly call every cheerful kind of beauty “alluring.” That uses the word too broadly for want of a proper distinction, and I must set that usage aside—indeed, reject it. In the sense I have specified, I find only two kinds of the alluring in art, and both are unworthy of it. One is rather base and occurs in Dutch still-life painting when it strays into depicting food so deceptively that it necessarily whets the appetite. Such an excitation of the will ends all aesthetic contemplation of the object. Painted fruit is still permissible: it appears as a further development of the flower, a beautiful natural product in form and colour, without forcing us to think of eating it. Unfortunately, though, we often find prepared and served dishes rendered with deceptive naturalness—oysters, herrings, lobsters, bread and butter, beer, wine, and so on. These are altogether objectionable.

In history painting and sculpture, the alluring consists in nude figures whose pose, partial clothing, and entire treatment are designed to arouse lust in the viewer. Pure aesthetic contemplation is thereby immediately cancelled, in direct opposition to the purpose of art. This fault corresponds exactly to the one just criticized in the Dutch painters. Ancient works, despite all the beauty and complete nudity of their figures, are almost always free of it. The artist created them in a purely objective spirit filled with ideal beauty, rather than in a spirit of subjective, base desire. The alluring is therefore to be avoided everywhere in art.

There is also a *negatively alluring* kind, even more objectionable than the positive kind just discussed: the disgusting. Like the alluring in the proper sense, it arouses the viewer's will and thereby destroys pure aesthetic contemplation. What it arouses, however, is a vehement refusal, a recoil. It stirs the will by presenting objects of abhorrence. This is why it has long been recognized as wholly inadmissible in art, whereas even ugliness, so long as it is not disgusting, can be tolerated in its proper place, as we shall see below.
