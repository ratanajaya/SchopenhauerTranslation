# §. 43.

Matter as such cannot present an Idea. As we found in Book One, matter is causality through and through: its being consists entirely in acting. Causality, however, is a form of the principle of sufficient reason, whereas cognition of an Idea essentially excludes that principle's content. In Book Two we also found that matter is the common substrate of all the individual appearances of Ideas, and thus the connecting link between an Idea and its appearance, or the individual thing. For either reason, matter by itself cannot present an Idea. This is confirmed *a posteriori* (from experience): we can have no intuitive representation of matter as such, only an abstract concept of it. What appears in intuition is only the forms and qualities borne by matter, and in all these Ideas reveal themselves. Correspondingly, causality—the whole essence of matter—cannot itself be presented in intuition, but only a particular causal connection can.

Conversely, every *appearance* of an Idea must present itself as a quality of matter, since as an appearance it has entered the form of the principle of sufficient reason, or the *principium individuationis* (principle of individuation). In this sense, then, matter is, as I have said, the link between the Idea and the *principium individuationis*, which is the form of the individual's cognition, or the principle of sufficient reason. Plato was therefore quite right to posit matter as a third thing, distinct from both the Idea and its appearance, the individual thing—two categories that otherwise encompass all things in the world (*Timaeus*, p. 345). As an appearance of an Idea, the individual is always matter. Every quality of matter is likewise always the appearance of an Idea and thus capable of aesthetic contemplation, that is, cognition of the Idea presented in it. This holds even for the most universal qualities of matter, without which it never exists, and whose Ideas are the weakest objecthood of the will: gravity, cohesion, rigidity, fluidity, reaction to light, and so forth.

Consider *architecture* solely as a fine art, apart from its service to useful purposes. In that service it serves the will, not pure cognition, and is no longer art in our sense. We can then assign it no aim other than to bring certain Ideas, the lowest grades of the will's objecthood, distinctly before intuition: gravity, cohesion, rigidity, hardness—the general properties of stone, those first, simplest, dimmest visibilities of the will, nature's fundamental bass notes—and, beside them, light, which in many respects is their opposite.

Even at this low grade of the will's objecthood, we already see its nature reveal itself in conflict. The contest between gravity and rigidity is, in fact, the sole aesthetic material of fine architecture. Its task is to make that contest fully apparent in a variety of ways. Architecture does so by denying these indestructible forces the shortest path to their satisfaction and holding them on a circuitous course. The conflict is prolonged, and the inexhaustible striving of both forces becomes visible in many forms. Left to its original tendency, the entire mass of a building would be a mere lump, joined as firmly as possible to the earth. Gravity, in which the will appears here, presses it ceaselessly toward the earth, while rigidity, also an objecthood of the will, resists. Architecture prevents this tendency, this striving, from finding direct satisfaction and permits it only indirectly, by detours. A beam, for example, can press upon the earth only through a column; a vault must support itself and can satisfy its striving toward the earth's mass only through piers, and so on. Precisely through these enforced detours and restraints, the forces dwelling in the raw mass of stone unfold in the clearest and most varied ways. The purely aesthetic aim of architecture goes no further.

The beauty of a building therefore lies in the evident purposiveness of each part—not for an external, arbitrary human end, which belongs to useful building, but directly for the endurance of the whole. The position, size, and form of each part must bear so necessary a relation to that whole that, if possible, removing any one part would bring it down. Only when each part carries as much as it properly can, and each is supported exactly where and to the extent it needs support, does the play of opposition between rigidity and gravity become fully visible. These forces constitute the life and expressions of will in stone, and in this way the lowest grades of the will's objecthood reveal themselves distinctly.

The form of every part must likewise be determined by its purpose and relation to the whole, not by caprice. The column is the simplest form of support, determined solely by its purpose; the twisted column is tasteless. The square pier is in fact less simple than the round column, although it happens to be easier to make. The forms of frieze, beam, arch, and dome are likewise wholly determined and explained by their immediate purpose. Ornaments on capitals and the like belong to sculpture, not architecture. Architecture merely permits them as added decoration, and they could be omitted.

It follows that understanding and taking aesthetic pleasure in a work of architecture absolutely requires an immediate, intuitive acquaintance with its material—with its weight, rigidity, and cohesion. Our pleasure in such a work would suddenly diminish if we learned that it was made of pumice stone: it would seem to us almost a sham building. Learning that it was made only of wood when we had assumed stone would have much the same effect. The relation between rigidity and gravity, and therefore the significance and necessity of all the parts, would change, for these natural forces are manifested far more weakly in a wooden building. Thus no work of fine architecture can really be made of wood, however readily wood takes every shape. Only our theory explains why. If we were told, further, that the building we enjoyed looking at consisted of quite different materials, very unequal in weight and consistency but indistinguishable by sight, the whole building would become as inaccessible to our enjoyment as a poem in a language we do not know.

All this shows that architecture works not merely mathematically but dynamically. What speaks to us through it is not bare form and symmetry, but the fundamental forces of nature, the first Ideas, the lowest grades of the will's objecthood. Regularity in a building and its parts results partly from the immediate fitness of each member for sustaining the whole. It also helps us survey and understand the whole; and regular figures contribute to beauty by revealing the lawfulness of space as such. Yet all this is of subordinate value and necessity. It is by no means the main thing: even symmetry is not indispensable, as ruins too can be beautiful.

Works of architecture have a very special relation to light. They gain twice as much beauty in full sunlight against a blue sky, and have an altogether different effect by moonlight. For this reason the effects of light and the directions of the sky are always given special attention when a fine architectural work is built. Much of this is because bright, sharp illumination first makes all the parts and their relations properly visible. But I also think that architecture is meant to reveal the nature of light, as well as gravity and rigidity, which are quite opposed to it. Large, opaque, sharply bounded masses of varied shape intercept, obstruct, and reflect light. Its nature and properties thereby unfold most purely and distinctly, to the viewer's great enjoyment. For light is the most delightful of things, being the condition and objective correlate of the most perfect mode of intuitive cognition.

The Ideas brought distinctly before intuition by architecture are the lowest grades of the will's objecthood. What it reveals to us is therefore of relatively little objective significance. Aesthetic enjoyment in looking at a beautiful, favourably lit building lies less in apprehending the Idea than in the subjective correlate that accompanies this apprehension. Above all, the sight tears the viewer away from the individual's mode of cognition, which serves the will and follows the principle of sufficient reason, and raises the viewer to that of the pure subject of cognition, free of the will. Enjoyment lies chiefly in pure contemplation itself, freed from all the suffering of willing and individuality. In this respect the opposite of architecture, at the other extreme in the series of fine arts, is drama. Drama brings the most significant Ideas to cognition, so that the objective side of aesthetic enjoyment decidedly predominates in it.

Architecture differs from the visual arts and poetry in giving us the thing itself rather than a copy. It does not, as they do, repeat the cognized Idea, with the artist lending the viewer his eyes. Here the artist merely arranges the object for the viewer, making apprehension of the Idea easier by bringing the real, individual object to a clear and complete expression of its nature.

Unlike other works of fine art, architectural works are very seldom built for purely aesthetic purposes. Their aesthetic ends are usually subordinated to useful ends foreign to art itself. The architect's great achievement is to keep those aesthetic ends in view and realize them despite this subordination. By skilfully adapting them in various ways to each particular practical purpose, the architect judges rightly what kind of architectural beauty can be combined with a temple, a palace, an arsenal, and so forth. The more a harsh climate increases the demands of need and utility and makes them fixed and unavoidable, the less room beauty has in architecture. In the mild climates of India, Egypt, Greece, and Rome, where necessity made fewer and less definite demands, architecture could pursue its aesthetic ends most freely. Under northern skies those ends were greatly curtailed. Here, where box-like buildings, steep roofs, and towers were required, architecture could unfold its own beauty only within narrow limits. To compensate, it had to adorn itself all the more with decoration borrowed from sculpture, as can be seen in fine Gothic architecture.

The requirements of necessity and utility thus impose great restrictions on architecture. They also give it powerful support. Given the scale and expense of its works and the narrow range of its aesthetic effects, architecture could not survive as a fine art alone. It has a secure and honourable place among human occupations because it is also a useful and necessary craft. The lack of just such a place prevents another art from standing beside it as its sister, although aesthetically it is precisely its counterpart: the fine art of directing water. What architecture achieves for the Idea of gravity combined with rigidity, this other art achieves for the same Idea combined with fluidity: formlessness, ease of displacement, and transparency. Foaming, roaring waterfalls plunging over rocks; cascades dissolving quietly into spray; fountains striving upward as tall columns of water; and clear, reflecting lakes reveal the Ideas of heavy, fluid matter, just as architectural works unfold the Ideas of rigid matter. Fine waterworks receive no support from the practical craft of water management, since its purposes usually cannot be combined with theirs. Such a combination occurs only exceptionally, as in the `Cascata di Trevi` (Trevi Fountain) in Rome.[^1]
