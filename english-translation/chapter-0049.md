# §. 47.

Since beauty and grace are sculpture's principal subjects, it favours the nude and tolerates clothing only insofar as it does not conceal the body's forms. It uses drapery not to veil the form but to present it indirectly. This method gives the understanding much to do: it reaches intuition of the cause, the body's shape, only through the effect immediately given to it, the folds of the fabric. Drapery in sculpture is thus, in a sense, what foreshortening is in painting. Both are indications, though not symbolic ones. When successful, they compel the understanding to intuit what is indicated as immediately as if it were actually before us.

Allow me to insert here, in passing, an analogy concerning the literary arts. A beautiful bodily form shows to best advantage with the lightest clothing, or none at all. A very beautiful person who also had taste and could follow it would therefore prefer to go almost naked, clothed only in the manner of the ancients. Similarly, a fine and thoughtful mind will always express itself in the most natural, direct, and simple way. It strives, wherever possible, to communicate its thoughts to others and thereby ease the solitude it must feel in a world like this one.

Conversely, poverty, confusion, and eccentricity of mind dress themselves in the most affected expressions and obscure turns of phrase. They hide meagre, trivial, sober, or commonplace thoughts in difficult, pompous sentences. They resemble someone who lacks the majesty of beauty and tries to make up for it with clothes, concealing the smallness or ugliness of his person under barbaric finery, glitter, feathers, ruffles, puffed sleeves, and a cloak. Such a person would be embarrassed to go naked. So would many an author if forced to translate a pompous, obscure book into its slight, clear content.
