# Appendix.

Critique of Kantian Philosophy.

> *C'est le privilège du vrai génie, et surtout du génie qui*<br>
> *ouvre une carrière, de faire impunément de grandes fautes.*<br>
> —Voltaire

> “It is the privilege of true genius, especially a genius who opens a new path, to make great mistakes with impunity.”

It is much easier to point out the faults and errors in a great mind's work than to give a clear and complete account of its value. Faults are individual and finite and can therefore be surveyed fully. The mark that genius imprints on its works, however, is that their excellence is unfathomable and inexhaustible. They thus become the ageless teachers of century after century. The completed masterpiece of a truly great mind always has a profound and far-reaching effect on humanity as a whole. No one can calculate the distant centuries and lands its illuminating influence may reach. It always does so because, however cultivated and rich the age in which it arose, genius rises above the ground in which it is rooted like a palm tree.

Such a deep and widespread effect cannot arrive suddenly, because the distance between genius and ordinary humanity is so great. The knowledge a single person draws directly from life and the world in one lifetime, gains and prepares for others, cannot immediately become humanity's possession; humanity lacks even as much power to receive as he has to give. Even after fighting off unworthy opponents who threaten an immortal work's life at its birth and would suffocate humanity's benefit in the bud, like the serpent at Hercules' cradle, that knowledge must travel the roundabout paths of countless false interpretations and crooked applications. It must survive attempts to combine it with old errors and live in struggle until a new, unbiased generation grows up to meet it. Gradually, through a thousand derived channels, that generation partly receives the source's content in its youth, assimilates it little by little, and shares in the benefit the great mind was meant to bring humanity. So slow is the education of humanity, the weak and obstinate pupil of genius. Kant's teaching too will reveal its full force and importance only with time, when the spirit of the age, gradually remade by that teaching and changed in its most important depths, bears living witness to the power of his giant mind. I do not mean presumptuously to anticipate this and play the thankless role of Calchas and Cassandra. Let me merely, for the reasons given, treat Kant's works as still very new. Many now consider them outdated, lay them aside as finished, or say they have “moved beyond” them. Emboldened by this, others simply ignore them and brazenly continue philosophizing about God and the soul on the premises of old realist dogmatism and scholasticism. That is as though modern chemistry were to accept the teachings of alchemists. Kant's works need no weak praise of mine. They will praise their author forever and live on earth, perhaps not in their letter, but in their spirit.

Looking at the immediate outcome of his teaching—the attempts and events in philosophy since then—one may indeed find confirmation of Goethe's disheartening observation: “As the water displaced by a ship closes up again immediately behind it, so error, when superior minds have pushed it aside and made room for themselves, very quickly closes up again by nature behind them” (*Poetry and Truth*, part 3, p. 521). Yet this period was only an episode among the fortunes of every new and great insight, and it is now plainly nearing its end: the soap bubble blown up for so long is finally bursting. People are beginning to realize generally that serious philosophy still stands where Kant left it. At any rate, I acknowledge no philosophical achievement between him and me, and so attach myself directly to him.

My purpose in this appendix is really only to justify the doctrine set out in my work insofar as it differs from, and in many places contradicts, Kantian philosophy. Such a discussion is necessary. Though the content of my train of thought differs greatly from Kant's, it is plainly under his influence throughout, necessarily presupposes him, and starts from him. Next to the impression of the intuitive world, I acknowledge that I owe the best in my own development to Kant's works, the Hindus' sacred writings, and Plato. I can justify my disagreements with Kant only by showing error and exposing faults in the points concerned. I must therefore be thoroughly polemical against him in this appendix, earnestly and with all my strength. Only in this way can the error clinging to his teaching be rubbed away so that its truth shines more brightly and stands more securely.

No one should expect my deeply felt reverence for Kant to extend to his weaknesses and mistakes or to require such careful gentleness in uncovering them that circumlocution makes my account weak and dull. Such gentleness is needed toward someone living, since human weakness finds even the fairest correction of an error hard to bear without soothing and flattery. A teacher of centuries and benefactor of humanity deserves at least to have his human weakness spared pain. But someone dead has shed that weakness. His merit is established; time will increasingly cleanse it of exaggeration and disparagement. His errors must be separated from it, made harmless, and then forgotten. In this polemic, then, I have only Kant's faults and weaknesses in view and stand against them. I wage a relentless war of elimination on them, intent on exposing them in the clearest light, rather than covering them gently, to remove them the more surely. For the reasons already stated, I am conscious of no injustice or ingratitude to Kant in doing this. Yet to remove any appearance of malice in others' eyes, I shall first show my heartfelt respect and gratitude by briefly stating what I take to be his chief merit. I shall do so from general standpoints, so that I need not yet touch on the points where I must later contradict him.

*Kant's greatest merit is the distinction between appearance and the thing in itself*, based on the demonstration that the *intellect* always stands between things and us, so that we cannot know them as they may be in themselves. *Locke* led him onto this path (see *Prolegomena to Any Future Metaphysics*, §13, note 2). Locke had shown that things' secondary qualities—sound, smell, color, hardness, softness, smoothness, and the like—depend on affections of the senses and do not belong to the objective body, the thing in itself. To that he ascribed only primary qualities, presupposing merely space and impenetrability: extension, shape, solidity, number, and mobility. But Locke's readily discovered distinction, which stays on the surface of things, was only a youthful prelude to Kant's. Starting from a far higher standpoint, Kant declared that everything Locke had allowed as *qualitates primariae* (primary qualities), properties of the thing in itself, likewise belongs only to its appearance in our faculty of apprehension. Precisely because space, time, and causality, the conditions of that faculty, are known by us *a priori*, this is so. Locke thus subtracted from the thing in itself the contribution of the sense organs to its appearance. Kant went on to subtract the contribution of the brain's functions, though he did not call them that. The distinction between appearance and thing in itself thereby gained infinitely greater importance and far deeper meaning. For this Kant had to make the great separation between our cognition *a priori* and *a posteriori*. Before him it had never been made with proper rigor, completeness, and clarity of awareness. It became the chief subject of his profound inquiries.

We should note immediately that Kant's philosophy has a threefold relation to his predecessors: first, it confirms and extends *Locke*, as just shown; second, it corrects and uses *Hume*, most clearly stated in the preface to the *Prolegomena*, the finest and most accessible of Kant's chief works and read far too little despite how greatly it eases the study of his philosophy; third, it is decidedly polemical and destructive toward the Leibniz-Wolff philosophy. One should know all three teachings before studying Kant. If, as I have said, the distinction between appearance and thing in itself—the complete difference between ideal and real—is the fundamental feature of Kant's philosophy, the shortly subsequent assertion of their absolute identity sadly confirms Goethe's saying quoted earlier. All it rested on was the empty boast of intellectual intuition and so was merely a return to the crudity of the ordinary view, disguised by a grand manner, bombast, and gibberish. It became the fitting starting point for the still coarser nonsense of the dull and spiritless *Hegel*.

Kant's grounded distinction between appearance and thing in itself surpassed everything before it in profundity and circumspection and had consequences of immense reach. Discovered independently, in a wholly new way, from a new side and along a new path, it presented the very truth Plato tirelessly repeated, generally in these words: this world appearing to the senses has no true being but only ceaseless becoming; it is and is not, and our apprehension of it is not so much knowledge as illusion. Plato expresses this truth mythically in the most important passage of all his works, the opening of book seven of the *Republic*, mentioned in my third book. People chained in a dark cave see neither genuine original light nor real things, only the meager light of the cave's fire and the shadows of real things passing behind their backs before it. Yet they take the shadows for reality and regard discerning the order in which these shadows succeed one another as true wisdom. The same truth, presented quite differently, is a chief teaching of the Vedas and Puranas: the doctrine of Maya. By Maya they mean nothing other than what Kant calls appearance in contrast to the thing in itself. Maya's work is the visible world we inhabit, a conjured enchantment, a fleeting appearance without essence in itself, comparable to an optical illusion or a dream, a veil surrounding human consciousness. It is equally false and equally true to say of it that it is and that it is not.

Kant not only expressed the same doctrine in an entirely new and original way, but made it a demonstrated and indisputable truth through the calmest and most sober exposition. Plato and the Indians had based their claims on a general intuition of the world, presenting them as direct expressions of their consciousness, more mythically and poetically than philosophically and clearly. In this respect they stand to Kant as the Pythagoreans Hicetas, Philolaus, and Aristarchus, who asserted that the earth moves around a stationary sun, stand to Copernicus. A clear understanding and calm, considered account of this dreamlike character of the whole world is the real foundation and soul of Kant's philosophy, its greatest merit. He achieved it by taking apart the whole machinery of our faculty of cognition, through which the phantasmagoria of the objective world arises, and showing it piece by piece with admirable care and skill. All earlier Western philosophy seems unspeakably crude beside his, having missed this truth and therefore spoken as though in a dream. Kant suddenly awakened it, which is why the last sleepers, including Mendelssohn, called him the all-destroyer.

He showed that the laws ruling existence—that is, experience in general—with unbreakable necessity cannot be used to derive and explain *existence itself*. Their validity is only relative: it begins only after existence, the world of experience, has already been posited and is present. These laws therefore cannot guide us when we try to explain the existence of the world and ourselves. All previous Western philosophers had imagined that the laws connecting appearances to one another—time, space, causality, and inference, all of which I sum up under the principle of sufficient reason—were absolute and unconditioned, *aeternae veritates* (eternal truths). They thought the world itself existed only in consequence of and in accordance with them, and the whole riddle of the world could therefore be solved by following them. The assumptions made for this purpose, criticized by Kant under the name Ideas of reason, only served to raise mere appearance, Maya's work and Plato's shadow world, to the sole and highest reality. They put it in place of the inmost, true essence of things and made real knowledge of that essence impossible: in short, they put the dreamers to sleep still more soundly. Kant showed that these laws, and consequently the world itself, are conditioned by the subject's mode of cognition. Thus, however far we inquire and infer under their guidance, we advance not a step in the chief matter, knowledge of the world's essence in itself and apart from representation. We merely move like a squirrel in a wheel. The dogmatists might be compared to people who think that if they walk straight ahead long enough they will reach the end of the world. Kant circumnavigated it and showed that because it is round, horizontal motion cannot carry us beyond it, though vertical motion may perhaps do so. We may also say his doctrine teaches us to seek the world's end and beginning within, not outside, ourselves.

All this rests on the fundamental distinction between dogmatic and *critical*, or *transcendental*, philosophy. Someone wanting a clear, concrete example can briefly read Leibniz's essay *De rerum originatione radicali* (*On the Ultimate Origin of Things*), first published in Erdmann's edition of Leibniz's philosophical works, vol. 1, p. 147, as a specimen of dogmatic philosophy. It demonstrates the world's origin and excellent character *a priori* in a thoroughly realist, dogmatic manner, using the ontological and cosmological proofs and resting on the *veritates aeternae* (eternal truths). It admits in passing that experience shows the exact opposite of this demonstrated excellence of the world, then tells experience that it knows nothing and should hold its tongue when *a priori* philosophy has spoken. *Critical philosophy* appeared with Kant as an opponent of this whole method. It takes as its problem the very eternal truths forming the foundation of every such dogmatic construction, investigates their origin, and finds it in the human head. They arise from forms peculiar to that head, which it carries within itself to apprehend an objective world. Here, then, in the brain, lies the quarry supplying the material for that proud dogmatic building. To reach this result critical philosophy had to *go beyond* the eternal truths on which all earlier dogmatism relied, taking them themselves as objects of inquiry. This made it *transcendental* philosophy. It further follows that the objective world as we know it does not belong to the nature of things in themselves, but is merely their *appearance*, conditioned by those forms that lie *a priori* in the human intellect, that is, the brain. Consequently it can contain nothing but appearances.

Kant did not arrive at the knowledge that appearance is the world as representation and the thing in itself is will. He did show that the appearing world is conditioned as much by the subject as by the object. Isolating the most general forms of its appearance, or representation, he demonstrated that we can know and survey their whole lawfulness from the subject's side as well as from the object's. They are the boundary shared by both subject and object. He concluded that following this boundary takes us into the interior of neither subject nor object, and so can never reveal the world's essence, the thing in itself.

He did not derive the thing in itself correctly, as I shall soon show, but through an inconsistency that exposed this main part of his teaching to repeated and irresistible attacks. He did not directly recognize the will as the thing in itself. Yet he took a great step toward this pioneering discovery when he presented the undeniable moral significance of human action as wholly distinct from, independent of, and inexplicable by the laws of appearance, something immediately touching the thing in itself. This is the second chief aspect of his merit.

A third merit is the complete overthrow of scholastic philosophy. Here I use that name broadly for the whole period from the Church Father Augustine to just before Kant. As Tennemann rightly says, scholasticism's principal feature was the guardianship of philosophy by the prevailing state religion. Philosophy was left chiefly to prove and embellish the principal dogmas prescribed by that religion. The scholastics proper, down to Suárez, admit this openly; later philosophers do so more unconsciously or at least without admitting it. Scholastic philosophy is often said to end about a century before Descartes, who supposedly began an entirely new era of free inquiry independent of every positive religious doctrine. But this cannot truly be attributed to Descartes and his successors,[^2] only an appearance of it or at best an aspiration. Descartes was an exceptionally gifted mind and, considering his age, achieved very much. Set that consideration aside, however, and judge him by the liberation of thought from all fetters and beginning of independent, unbiased inquiry for which he is praised. His skepticism lacks real seriousness and is therefore abandoned so quickly and poorly. He makes a show of throwing off all the fetters of opinions instilled early and belonging to his nation and age, but does so only briefly and in appearance, to take them up immediately and hold them more tightly. The same is true of all his successors up to Kant. Goethe's verse aptly describes such a free thinker:

“He seems to me, with all respect to your grace,<br>
Like one of the long-legged cicadas,<br>
Always flying and leaping as it flies—<br>
Then singing its old little song in the grass.”

Kant had reason to appear as though he meant to do only that. But the supposed leap, permitted because everyone already knew it would lead back into the grass, this time became a flight. Those below were left watching and could no longer catch him.

Kant dared to show from his teaching that all those dogmas supposedly proved so often could not be proved. He dealt speculative theology and its companion, rational psychology, a deathblow. Since then they have vanished from German philosophy. One should not be misled if the words are retained here and there after the things are abandoned, or if some wretched professor of philosophy keeps his master's displeasure in view and lets truth take care of itself. Only someone who notices the harmful influence of those concepts on natural science and philosophy in even the best seventeenth- and eighteenth-century writers can appreciate the greatness of Kant's merit here. The change since Kant in the tone and metaphysical background of German scientific works is striking. Before him they read as English works still do.

This merit is connected with the fact that all earlier philosophy, ancient, medieval, and modern, had been governed by *realism*: unreflectingly following the laws of appearance, raising them to eternal truths and thereby raising fleeting appearance to the world's true essence, without reflection ever disturbing the delusion. *Berkeley*, like *Malebranche* before him, saw its one-sidedness and falsity but could not overturn it because his attack was limited to *one* point. It was left to *Kant* to establish in European philosophy the fundamental idealist view that is essentially even the religious view throughout the non-Islamic parts of Asia. Before Kant we were *in* time; now time is in us, and so forth.

Realist philosophy had also treated ethics according to laws of appearance, regarded as absolute and valid even for the thing in itself. Ethics was therefore based at one time on a doctrine of happiness, at another on the will of the world's creator, finally on the concept of perfection. By itself this concept is wholly empty. It denotes a mere relation that takes meaning only from the things to which it is applied. “To be perfect” means no more than “to correspond to some given concept presupposed here.” That concept must therefore be stated first. Without it, perfection is an unnamed number and says nothing by itself. If one silently presupposes the concept of “humanity” and sets the striving for perfect humanity as a moral principle, one says only, “People should be as they should be,” and is no wiser. “Perfect” is almost synonymous with “complete”: all the predicates contained in the concept of a kind are actually present in the particular case or individual. Used by itself *in abstracto* (in the abstract), “perfection” is therefore an empty word, as is talk of “the most perfect being” and the like. It is all mere verbal show. Nevertheless, in the previous century the concepts of perfection and imperfection became common currency, indeed the pivot of almost all moralizing and even theology. Everyone spoke of them until their abuse became a real nuisance. Even the best writers of the age, Lessing for example, were pitiably entangled in perfections and imperfections and struggled with them. Yet every thinking mind must at least have dimly felt that the concept has no positive content, indicating only a relation in the abstract like an algebraic symbol. As I have said, Kant separated the undeniable, great ethical significance of actions wholly from appearance and its laws and showed it to concern immediately the thing in itself, the world's inmost essence. Time, space, and everything filling them and ordered within them by the causal law, by contrast, are to be seen as a transient, insubstantial dream.

These brief remarks do not exhaust the matter, but they may suffice as testimony to my recognition of Kant's great merits, offered here for my satisfaction and because fairness calls for recalling them to everyone who follows my unreserved exposure of his faults, to which I now turn.

History alone suggests that Kant's great achievements must have been accompanied by great faults. He brought about the greatest revolution in philosophy, ending a scholasticism that, in the broad sense given above, had lasted fourteen centuries, and began a genuinely new, third world epoch of philosophy. Yet the immediate result of his appearance was almost wholly negative, not positive. He established no complete new system to which his followers could hold for a time. Everyone sensed that something very great had happened, but no one knew exactly what. They realized earlier philosophy had been a fruitless dream from which the new age awoke, but did not know what to hold to now. A great void and need arose. Public attention, even beyond specialists, was aroused. Prompted by this rather than by any inner drive and sense of power, which would show itself even in the least favorable age, as it did with Spinoza, men of no special talent made various weak, absurd, and sometimes mad attempts. The newly aroused public still paid attention and listened for a long time with a patience found only in Germany.

Something similar must once have happened in nature when a great revolution changed the whole face of the earth, sea and land exchanged places, and a new creation could begin. Nature took a long time to bring forth a new series of lasting forms, each in harmony with itself and the others. Strange, monstrous organisms arose, discordant within and among themselves, that could not endure. Their surviving remains recall the wavering experiments of a nature remaking itself. We all know that Kant caused a similar crisis in philosophy, an age of monstrous births. It suggests that his merit was incomplete, burdened with great defects, negative and one-sided. We shall now trace those defects.

First let us clearly state and examine the fundamental thought behind the whole *Critique of Pure Reason*. Kant adopted the standpoint of his predecessors, the dogmatic philosophers, and began with these assumptions: (1) Metaphysics is a science of what lies beyond all possible experience. (2) It can never be found through principles drawn from experience (*Prolegomena*, §1); only what we know *before* all experience, and so independently *of* it, can reach beyond possible experience. (3) Some such principles are indeed found in our reason and are called cognitions of pure reason. To this point Kant agrees with his predecessors; here he parts from them. They say, “These principles, or cognitions of pure reason, express the absolute possibility of things, *aeternae veritates* (eternal truths), sources of ontology. They stand above the world order as fate stood above the ancient gods.” Kant says instead that they are merely forms of our intellect, laws not of the existence of things but of our representations of them. They hold only for our apprehension of things and therefore cannot reach beyond possible experience, the aim assumed in (1). The very *a priori* character of these forms of cognition can rest only on their subjective origin. It forever cuts us off from knowledge of things' essence in itself and confines us to a world of mere appearances. We cannot know things as they may be in themselves even *a posteriori*, much less *a priori*. Metaphysics is consequently impossible, and the critique of pure reason takes its place. Kant's victory over the old dogmatism is complete. Every later dogmatic attempt has therefore had to take paths different from the earlier ones. In line with this critique's stated purpose, I shall now lead to the justification of my own.

Careful examination of that argument shows that its very first basic assumption is a *petitio principii* (begging of the question). It is the claim, especially clearly stated in *Prolegomena*, §1, that “The source of metaphysics must absolutely not be empirical; its principles and fundamental concepts must never be taken from experience, whether inward or outward.” No ground for this cardinal claim is given except an etymological argument from the word *metaphysics*. But this is how matters actually stand. The world and our own existence necessarily present themselves as a riddle. Without further argument, Kant assumes that its solution cannot emerge from a thorough understanding of the world itself, but must be sought in something wholly different from the world—for that is what “beyond all possible experience” means. He excludes from the solution anything of which we can have *immediate* knowledge, meaning possible inner or outer experience. He insists that it be sought only in what we can reach indirectly, by inference from general *a priori* propositions. After excluding the chief source of all knowledge and closing the direct path to truth, we need not wonder that dogmatic attempts failed and Kant could prove they had to fail. Metaphysics and *a priori* knowledge had been assumed identical from the outset. But it first needed to be proved that the material for solving the riddle of the world could not possibly lie in the world itself, and could be sought only outside it in something accessible solely by following the forms known to us *a priori*.

Until that is proved, we have no reason to close off the richest sources of knowledge, inner and outer experience, for the most important and difficult of all tasks and work only with forms empty of content. I say, then, that solving the world's riddle must arise from understanding the world itself. The task of metaphysics is not to fly beyond the experience in which the world stands, but to understand it from the ground up, for inner and outer experience are indeed the principal source of all knowledge. The riddle can be solved only by connecting outward with inward experience at the proper point and in the proper way, joining two very different sources of cognition. Even then it is possible only within limits inseparable from our finite nature. We may arrive at a correct understanding of the world itself without a final account of its existence that removes every further problem. Thus *est quaedam prodire tenus* (“one may advance to a certain point”): my path lies between the claim to omniscience of earlier dogmatism and the despair of Kant's critique. Yet the important truths Kant discovered and used to overthrow earlier metaphysical systems furnished my own with data and material. Compare what I said of my method in chapter 17 of the second volume. So much for Kant's basic thought; now let us look at its execution and details.

Kant's style throughout bears the stamp of a superior mind, genuine and firm originality, and quite extraordinary power of thought. Perhaps its character can best be called *brilliant dryness*. Through it he grasps concepts firmly, singles them out with great assurance, and tosses them back and forth with the greatest freedom, to the reader's amazement. I find the same brilliant dryness in Aristotle's style, though his is much simpler. Nevertheless Kant's exposition is often unclear, indefinite, inadequate, and sometimes obscure. Part of this obscurity is excused by the subject's difficulty and the depth of his thoughts. But a person who is himself wholly clear and knows exactly what he thinks and intends will never write obscurely or set up wavering, indefinite concepts. He will not collect elaborate, difficult foreign expressions to name them and use those continuously afterward, as Kant borrowed words and formulas from earlier, even scholastic, philosophy and combined them for his purposes. An example is “transcendental synthetic unity of apperception”; in general he writes “unity of synthesis” when “combination” would suffice. Such a writer will not keep explaining afresh what he has explained already, as Kant does with the understanding, the categories, experience, and other chief concepts. Nor will he repeat himself endlessly while leaving the same obscure spots in each new presentation of a thought given a hundred times. He will state his view clearly, thoroughly, and fully once, and let it stand. As Descartes says in his fifth letter, *Quo enim melius rem aliquam concipimus, eo magis determinati sumus ad eam unico modo exprimendam* (“The better we conceive something, the more determined we are to express it in one way”). The greatest harm of Kant's occasionally obscure exposition was that it served as an *exemplar vitiis imitabile* (a model imitable in its faults), indeed was misread as dangerous authorization. The public had been forced to see that obscurity is not always nonsense; nonsense immediately hid behind obscurity. *Fichte* first seized and exploited this new privilege. *Schelling* at least matched him, and an army of hungry scribblers without spirit or honesty soon outdid both. The greatest effrontery in serving up sheer nonsense and cobbling together empty, deranged tangles of words, previously heard only in madhouses, finally appeared in *Hegel*. It became the instrument of the crudest mass mystification ever, with a success that posterity will find fabulous and remember as a monument of German foolishness. Meanwhile *Jean Paul* wrote in vain his fine section “Higher Appreciation of Philosophical Madness at the Lectern and Poetic Madness on the Stage” (*Aesthetic After-School*). Earlier still, *Goethe* had said, also in vain:


“Thus people chatter and teach undisturbed;<br>
Who wants to bother with fools?<br>
Usually, when someone hears only words,<br>
He thinks there must be some thought behind them.”

But let us return to *Kant*. One cannot deny that he wholly lacks the grand simplicity of antiquity, its naïveté, *ingénuité*, and *candeur* (guileless candor). His philosophy is unlike Greek architecture, which offers large, simple relations immediately visible at a glance. Rather, it strongly recalls Gothic architecture. A peculiar feature of Kant's mind is his strange delight in *symmetry*. He loves colorful multiplicity so that he can order it and repeat the order in subordinate divisions, again and again, just as in Gothic churches. Sometimes he carries this to the point of play, doing clear violence to truth for the sake of that inclination. He treats truth like old-fashioned formal gardeners treated nature, making symmetrical avenues, squares and triangles, trees cut into pyramids and spheres, and hedges twisted into regular curves. I shall document this with facts.

After treating space and time separately, he dispatches the whole intuitive world that fills them, in which we live and exist, with the empty words “the empirical content of intuition is *given* to us.” With *one* leap he then reaches the *logical foundation of his whole philosophy: the table of judgments*. From it he deduces exactly twelve categories arranged symmetrically under four headings. They later become a dreadful Procrustean bed into which he forces everything in the world and everything occurring within us. He shuns no violence or sophistry to repeat the table's symmetry everywhere. The first thing derived symmetrically from it is the pure physiological table of general principles of natural science: axioms of intuition, anticipations of perception, analogies of experience, and postulates of empirical thought in general. The first two principles are simple; each of the latter two symmetrically sends out three offshoots. The bare categories are what he calls *concepts*; these principles of natural science are *judgments*. Following his highest guide to wisdom, symmetry, it is now the turn of *inferences* to prove fruitful, once more symmetrically and in step. The application of categories to sensibility gave rise to experience, with its *a priori* principles, for the *understanding*. In the same way the application of *inferences* to categories, carried out by *reason* according to its supposed principle of seeking the unconditioned, gives rise to the *Ideas* of reason. The three categories of relation yield the only three possible kinds of major premises for inferences; these consequently fall into three types. Each is like an egg from which reason hatches an Idea: from the categorical type, the Idea of the *soul*; from the hypothetical, the Idea of the *world*; and from the disjunctive, the Idea of *God*. In the middle Idea, that of the world, the category table's symmetry is repeated again: its four headings produce four theses, each paired symmetrically with an antithesis.

We admire the genuinely acute combinations that produced this elegant structure, though we shall later examine it thoroughly in its foundations and parts. First, however, some observations are needed.

It is astonishing how Kant proceeds without pausing to reflect, following his symmetry and ordering everything by it, without ever considering any object so treated on its own terms. Let me explain. He considers intuitive cognition only in mathematics, neglects completely the rest of intuitive cognition, where the world lies before us, and holds only to abstract thought. Yet abstract thought derives all its meaning and worth from the intuitive world, which is infinitely more significant, general, and rich in content than the abstract part of our cognition. Indeed, he nowhere clearly distinguishes intuitive from abstract cognition. This is a central point and, as we shall see, involved him in irreconcilable self-contradictions. After dispatching the whole sensory world with the empty “it is given,” he makes the logical table of judgments his foundation. But again he never pauses to reflect on what lies before him. The forms of judgments are *words* and *combinations of words*. He should first have asked what they signify immediately, and would have found that they signify *concepts*. Next he should have asked what *concepts* are. The answer would have revealed their relation to intuitive representations in which the world exists, and intuition would have been distinguished from reflection. He would then have had to ask not only how pure, merely formal *a priori* intuition, but also how its content, empirical intuition, comes to consciousness. He would have seen what part the *understanding* plays in this, and so what the understanding generally is and what *reason*, which he proposes to critique, really is.

It is striking that he never defines reason properly and adequately even once. He only gives incomplete and incorrect explanations in passing as each context requires, contrary to the rule of Descartes cited above.[^3] For example, in the *Critique of Pure Reason*, p. 11; fifth edition p. 24, reason is the faculty of *a priori principles*. At p. 299; fifth edition p. 356, it is again the faculty of *principles*, contrasted with the understanding as the faculty of *rules*. One would expect a vast difference between principles and rules if each warrants its own faculty of cognition. Yet the distinction is merely this: what is known *a priori* from pure intuition or through the forms of the understanding is a *rule*, while only what comes *a priori* from mere concepts is a principle. I shall return to this arbitrary, untenable distinction in discussing the Dialectic. At p. 330; fifth edition p. 386, reason is the faculty of inference, while he often says judging alone belongs to the understanding (p. 69; fifth edition p. 94). This amounts to saying that judging is the understanding's business when a judgment's ground is empirical, transcendental, or metalogical (see my essay on the principle of sufficient reason, §§31–33), but when the ground is logical, as in an inference, a wholly separate and much superior faculty, reason, acts. Worse, at p. 303; fifth edition p. 360, Kant says immediate consequences of a proposition belong to the understanding and only those requiring an intervening concept to reason. Thus from “All humans are mortal,” “Some mortals are humans” follows through the understanding alone, but “All scholars are mortal” requires a quite different, much superior faculty, reason. How could a great thinker advance such a claim? At p. 553; fifth edition p. 581, reason suddenly becomes the abiding condition of all voluntary actions. At p. 614; fifth edition p. 642, it consists in our capacity to give an account of our assertions. At pp. 643–644; fifth edition pp. 671–672, it combines the concepts of the understanding into Ideas as the understanding combines the manifold of objects into concepts. At p. 646; fifth edition p. 674, it is simply the faculty of deriving the particular from the general.

The *understanding* is likewise redefined repeatedly. At seven places in the *Critique of Pure Reason*: p. 51; fifth edition p. 75, it is the faculty of producing representations itself. At p. 69; fifth edition p. 94, it is the faculty of judging, that is, thinking or cognizing through concepts. At p. 137 of the fifth edition it is, in general, the faculty of cognitions. At p. 132; fifth edition p. 171, it is the faculty of rules. At p. 158; fifth edition p. 197, Kant says that “it is not only the faculty of rules but the source of principles according to which everything stands under rules,” although he had contrasted it with reason because only the latter was the faculty of principles. At p. 160; fifth edition p. 199, the understanding is the faculty of concepts; at p. 302; fifth edition p. 359, the faculty of unifying appearances by means of rules.

I need not defend my fixed, sharp, definite, simple definitions of these two faculties, always agreeing with how people of every nation and age use the words, against such confused and groundless remarks, though they come from Kant. I have cited them only to support my charge that Kant followed his symmetrical logical system without thinking adequately about the subject he treated.

Had Kant seriously asked how two different faculties of cognition, one distinguishing human beings from other animals, disclose themselves, and what every nation's and philosopher's usage means by reason and understanding, he would never have divided reason into theoretical and practical without better authority than the scholastics' *intellectus theoreticus* and *practicus*, used in quite another sense, or made practical reason the source of virtuous action. Likewise, before carefully separating concepts of the understanding, by which he means partly his categories and partly all general concepts, from concepts of reason, his so-called Ideas, and making both the material of a philosophy largely about their validity, use, and origin, he should first have investigated what a *concept* is in general. That indispensable inquiry was unfortunately omitted, greatly contributing to the disastrous confusion of intuitive and abstract cognition I shall soon show. The same lack of reflection that led him to pass over “What is intuition? What is reflection? What is a concept? What is reason? What is understanding?” also led him to pass over equally necessary questions: What do I mean by an *object* distinguished from a *representation*? What are existence, object, subject, truth, appearance, and error? He proceeds without stopping or looking around, following his logical scheme and symmetry. The table of judgments is supposed to be the key to all wisdom.

I identified Kant's chief merit above as distinguishing appearance from thing in itself, declaring the whole visible world appearance, and denying that its laws hold beyond appearance. Oddly, he did not derive appearance's merely relative existence from the simple, obvious, undeniable truth “*No object without a subject*.” At the root he could have shown that an object exists only in relation to a subject and is therefore dependent on and conditioned by it: mere appearance, not something existing in itself and unconditionally. Berkeley had already made that important proposition the foundation of his philosophy and thereby won an immortal name, though he himself drew neither the proper consequences nor sufficient understanding or attention. Kant is unfair to his merit. In my first edition I explained Kant's avoidance of Berkeley's proposition as an evident fear of decisive idealism, though I found idealism plainly expressed in many passages of the *Critique of Pure Reason*. I therefore accused Kant of contradicting himself. The charge was justified so long as one knew, as I then did, only its second and five subsequent editions. Later I read Kant's great work in its now rare first edition and, to my delight, found that all these contradictions disappeared. Though Kant does not use “no object without a subject,” he declares as decisively as Berkeley and I that the external world in space and time is merely the representation of a cognizing subject. On p. 383, for example, he says plainly: “If I take away the thinking subject, the entire corporeal world must fall away, being nothing but appearance in our subject's sensibility and a kind of its representations.”

But Kant removed the entire passage on pp. 348–392, where he had so beautifully and clearly expounded his resolute idealism, from the second edition, replacing it with many statements opposed to it. The text of the *Critique of Pure Reason* circulating from 1787 to 1838 was thus mutilated and corrupted, a self-contradictory book whose meaning no one could fully grasp. I gave the details and my conjectures about the reasons and weaknesses that could have moved Kant to disfigure his immortal work in a letter to Professor Rosenkranz. He included the principal part in his preface to volume two of his edition of Kant's complete works, to which I refer readers. Following my representations, Rosenkranz restored the *Critique of Pure Reason* to its original form in 1838 by printing the *first* edition of 1781 in that volume. He thus earned an invaluable merit in philosophy, perhaps rescuing the most important work of German literature from oblivion; it must never be forgotten. No one should imagine that he knows the *Critique of Pure Reason* or has a clear conception of Kant's teaching if he has read only the second or a later edition. That is impossible: he has read only a mutilated, corrupted, in a sense inauthentic text. It is my duty to say so decisively as a warning to everyone.


But Kant's way of introducing the *thing in itself* undeniably contradicts the resolutely idealist fundamental view so clearly stated in the first edition of the *Critique of Pure Reason*. This was doubtless the main reason he removed the major idealist passage from the second edition and declared himself outright against Berkeley's idealism. He thereby introduced inconsistencies without remedying the work's chief defect: his manner of introducing the *thing in itself*. G. E. Schulze explained its invalidity at length in *Aenesidemus*, and it was soon recognized as the untenable point in Kant's system. The issue is easily clarified. Though he conceals it in various ways, Kant bases the assumption of the thing in itself on an inference under the causal law: empirical intuition, or more properly the *sensation* in our sense organs from which intuition begins, must have an external cause. Yet by his own correct discovery the law of causality is known to us *a priori*, a function of our intellect and therefore *subjective* in origin. The sensory sensation to which we apply it is itself undeniably *subjective*. Even the space into which we place the sensation's cause as an object through this application is an *a priori*, and therefore *subjective*, form of our intellect. The entire empirical intuition thus remains on *subjective* ground throughout, a mere process within us. Nothing wholly different from and independent of it can be brought in as a *thing in itself* or shown to be a necessary presupposition. Empirical intuition is and remains our mere representation: the world as representation. We can reach its essence in itself only by the wholly different path I have taken, bringing in self-consciousness, which discloses the will as the in-itself of our own appearance. The thing in itself then differs *toto genere* (in kind altogether) from representation and its elements, as I have explained.

This serious defect in Kant's system, demonstrated early on, illustrates the fine Indian proverb “No lotus without a stalk.” The faulty derivation of the thing in itself is the stalk. But only the manner of derivation is faulty, not the recognition that the given appearance has a thing in itself. *Fichte* misunderstood the criticism as denying the latter, possible only because he was concerned not with truth but with attracting attention for personal ends. Bold and thoughtless enough to deny the thing in itself altogether, he constructed a system supposedly deriving *a priori* from the subject not merely the formal element of representation, as Kant did, but also its material, its entire content. He rightly counted on the public's poor judgment and foolishness. It accepted bad sophisms, mere hocus-pocus, and nonsensical chatter as proofs, enabling him to turn its attention from Kant to himself and set German philosophy on the path Schelling continued and that ended in Hegel's absurd sham wisdom.

I return now to the great Kantian fault mentioned above: he did not properly distinguish intuitive from abstract cognition. This produced a disastrous confusion that we must examine more closely. Had he sharply separated intuitive representations from concepts thought only *in abstracto*, he would have kept the two distinct and always known which he was dealing with. Sadly he did not, though the charge has not yet been made and may come as a surprise. His constantly mentioned “object of experience,” the proper object of the categories, is neither intuitive representation nor abstract concept. It differs from both while somehow being both, and is an absolute absurdity. Incredible as it seems, he lacked either the thoughtfulness or the willingness to make clear to himself and others whether his “object of experience,” meaning the cognition produced by applying the categories, was an intuitive representation in space and time, my first class of representations, or merely an abstract concept. Strange though it is, some intermediate thing constantly floats before his mind. This brings about the unfortunate confusion I must now expose, for which I need to survey the Doctrine of Elements as a whole.

The *Transcendental Aesthetic* is such an extraordinarily meritorious work that it alone could immortalize Kant's name. Its proofs are so convincing that I count its propositions among unshakable truths. They are doubtless among the most consequential, and thus are one of the rarest things in the world: a real, great discovery in metaphysics. His rigorously established fact that we are conscious of some knowledge *a priori* permits no other explanation than that it comprises forms of our intellect. This is less an explanation than simply a clear statement of the fact. *A priori* means only “not acquired through experience, therefore not brought into us from outside.” What is present in the intellect without coming from outside belongs originally to it: it is its own nature. If what is thus within it determines the general way all objects must appear to it, we have said that these are the forms of its cognition, the fixed way it carries out its function. “*A priori* knowledge” and “forms inherent in the intellect” are thus essentially two expressions of the same matter, virtually synonyms.

I know of nothing I would remove from the Transcendental Aesthetic, then, though I would add something. In particular, Kant did not carry his thought to its end by discarding the whole Euclidean method of demonstration after saying at p. 87; fifth edition p. 120, that all geometrical knowledge has immediate evidence from intuition. Remarkably, even his sharpest opponent, G. E. Schulze (*Critique of Theoretical Philosophy*, II, p. 241), concluded that Kant's doctrine would call for a treatment of geometry quite unlike the usual one. He thought this an indirect proof against Kant, while in fact he was unwittingly making war on the Euclidean method. I refer readers to §15 of the first book of the present work.

After the Transcendental Aesthetic's detailed account of the general *forms* of intuition, one expects some explanation of its *content*: how *empirical* intuition enters our consciousness and how we come to know this world so real and important to us. Kant's whole teaching offers nothing more than the repeated, empty phrase “The empirical element of intuition is *given* from outside.” He therefore leaps straight from the *pure forms of intuition* to *thinking*, to Transcendental Logic. At its very beginning (*Critique of Pure Reason*, p. 50; fifth edition p. 74), where he cannot avoid touching the material content of empirical intuition, he takes his first false step, the πρῶτον ψεῦδος (first falsehood). “Our cognition,” he says, “has two sources: receptivity to impressions and spontaneity of concepts. The first is the capacity to receive representations; the second is that of knowing an object through these representations. Through the first an *object* is given to us; through the second it is thought.” This is false. On that account the *impression*, for which alone we are merely receptive, which comes from outside and alone is properly “*given*,” would already be a *representation*, even an *object*. But it is nothing more than a mere sensation in the sense organ. Only by applying the *understanding*, that is, the law of causality, and the intuitive forms of space and time does our *intellect* turn this mere *sensation* into a *representation*. It then stands as an *object* in space and time. The representation and object can be distinguished only if one asks about the thing in itself; otherwise they are identical. I explained this process fully in my essay on the principle of sufficient reason, §21.

The understanding and intuitive cognition have completed their work at this point; they require neither concepts nor thought. Animals too therefore have such representations. If concepts and thought are added, a spontaneity may indeed be ascribed to them, but *intuitive* cognition has been left behind entirely. An entirely different class of representations enters consciousness: non-intuitive, abstract concepts. This is the activity of *reason*, which nevertheless draws the whole content of its thought solely from the earlier intuition and its comparison with other intuitions and concepts. Kant instead brings thought into intuition and lays the groundwork for the disastrous blending of intuitive and abstract cognition that I am criticizing. He makes intuition in itself without understanding, purely sensory and wholly passive, and has an object apprehended only through thinking, a category of understanding. Thus he brings *thinking into intuition*. Yet the object of *thought* is in turn an individual real object. Thought loses its essential generality and abstraction and receives individual things rather than general concepts as its objects; thus he brings *intuition into thinking*. From this springs the confusion described, and the consequences of this first false step run through his whole theory of cognition. Throughout, intuitive and abstract representations are fused into something midway between them, which he presents as the object known through the understanding and its categories, calling this cognition *experience*. It is difficult to believe Kant had anything fully definite or clear in mind as this object of understanding. I shall prove it through the immense contradiction running through Transcendental Logic, the true source of its obscurity.

In the *Critique of Pure Reason*, pp. 67–69; fifth edition pp. 92–94; pp. 89–90; fifth edition pp. 122–123; and again in the fifth edition at pp. 135, 139, and 153, Kant repeatedly insists that the understanding is not a faculty of intuition. Its cognition is not intuitive but discursive. The understanding is the faculty of judging (p. 69; fifth edition p. 94), and a judgment is mediated cognition, a representation of a representation (p. 68; fifth edition p. 93). It is the faculty of thinking, and thinking is cognition through concepts (p. 69; fifth edition p. 94). The categories of the understanding are in no way the conditions under which objects are given in intuition (p. 89; fifth edition p. 122), and intuition has no need of the functions of thought (p. 91; fifth edition p. 123). Our understanding can only think, not intuit (fifth edition pp. 135, 139). Likewise the *Prolegomena*, §20, assigns intuition, perception, *perceptio*, to the senses alone and judging to the understanding alone; §22 says that the senses' task is to intuit and the understanding's to think, that is, judge. Finally, in the *Critique of Practical Reason*, fourth edition p. 247; Rosenkranz edition p. 281, the understanding is discursive: its representations are thoughts, not intuitions. These are all Kant's own words.


It follows from these statements that the intuitive world would exist for us even without an understanding, entering our heads in an utterly inexplicable way. Kant often marks this with the peculiar phrase that intuition is *given*, without ever explaining that vague metaphor further.

But his entire remaining account of the understanding, its categories, and the possibility of experience in Transcendental Logic flagrantly contradicts those statements. In the *Critique of Pure Reason*, p. 79; fifth edition p. 105, the understanding brings unity into the manifold of *intuition* through its categories, and pure concepts of the understanding relate *a priori* to objects of *intuition*. At p. 94; fifth edition p. 126, categories are conditions of experience, whether of the *intuition* or of the thought found within it. At fifth edition p. 127, the understanding creates experience; at p. 128, categories determine the *intuition* of objects. At p. 130, everything we represent as connected in the object, surely something intuitive rather than an abstraction, is first connected by an act of understanding. At p. 135, the understanding is newly defined as the faculty of combining *a priori* and bringing the manifold of given representations under the unity of apperception. But in all ordinary usage, apperception is not thinking a concept; it is *intuition*. At p. 136 we even find a supreme principle for the possibility of all intuition in relation to the understanding. At p. 143 a heading says all sensory intuition is conditioned by the categories. There the *logical function of judgments* also brings the manifold of given *intuitions* under apperception in general, and the manifold of a given intuition necessarily falls under the categories. At p. 144, the understanding brings unity into *intuition* by the categories. At p. 145, the understanding's thinking is oddly explained as synthesizing, combining, and ordering the manifold of *intuition*. At p. 161, experience is possible only through the categories and consists in connecting *perceptions*, which surely are intuitions. At p. 159, the categories are *a priori* cognitions of objects of *intuition* in general.

Here and at fifth edition pp. 163 and 165, Kant also presents a major doctrine: *the understanding first makes nature possible*, prescribing *a priori* laws for it, so that it conforms to the understanding's lawfulness. But nature is surely something intuited, not an abstraction; the understanding must therefore be a faculty of intuition. At p. 168, concepts of the understanding are called the principles of the possibility of experience, which is the determination of appearances in space and time generally; appearances surely exist in intuition. Finally, at pp. 189–211; fifth edition pp. 232–265, comes the long proof, whose error I explained fully in my essay on the principle of sufficient reason, §23, that objective succession and coexistence of objects of experience are not sensibly perceived but brought into nature solely by the understanding, which first makes nature possible. Yet nature, the succession of events, and the coexistence of states are surely all intuitive, not merely abstractly thought.

I challenge anyone who shares my reverence for Kant to reconcile these contradictions and show that he thought anything definite and clear when he spoke of the object of experience and its determination by the understanding and its twelve functions. I am convinced the contradiction running through the whole Transcendental Logic is the real reason for its great obscurity. Kant dimly knew of it and struggled inwardly with it, yet would not or could not bring it to clear awareness. He thus veiled it from himself and others and evaded it by all sorts of indirect paths. Perhaps that is why he made the faculty of cognition into such a strange, complicated machine with so many wheels: twelve categories; transcendental synthesis by imagination, by inner sense, and by the transcendental unity of apperception; the schematism of pure concepts of the understanding; and so on. For all this apparatus, no attempt is even made to explain intuition of the outer world, surely the principal thing in our knowledge. The demand is repeatedly put off with the same poor, empty metaphor: “Empirical intuition is given to us.” On p. 145 of the fifth edition we learn further that it is given *by the object*, which must therefore differ from intuition.

If we try to discover Kant's inmost thought, which he did not state clearly himself, we find that such an object, distinct from *intuition* but by no means a *concept*, really is for him the proper object of the understanding. The strange assumption of this unrepresentable object is supposed first to turn intuition into experience. I think its ultimate source is an old, ingrained prejudice in Kant, beyond the reach of inquiry: the assumption of an *absolute object*, an object in itself, even without a subject. It is not the *intuited object*. Through a concept it is added in thought to intuition as something corresponding to it. Only now is intuition experience, acquiring worth and truth through reference to a concept, exactly opposite to my account, on which a concept gains worth and truth only from intuition. Adding this directly unrepresentable object to intuition is then the categories' proper function. “Only through intuition is the object given, which is subsequently thought according to the category” (*Critique of Pure Reason*, first edition, p. 399). A passage in the fifth edition, p. 125, makes this especially clear: “Now the question is whether *a priori* concepts also precede, as conditions under which alone something may be *thought* as an *object* in general, though *not intuited*.” He answers yes. Here the source of the error and of the confusion enveloping it is plain.

An *object* as such exists only for and in *intuition*, whether through the senses or, in its absence, imagination. What is thought is always a general, non-intuitive *concept*, perhaps the concept of an object in general. Thought relates to *objects* only indirectly, through concepts; objects themselves always are and remain *intuitive*. Thought does not serve to confer reality on intuitions; they have whatever empirical reality they can have of themselves. It serves to gather their common features and results so we can preserve and handle them more easily. Kant instead assigns objects themselves to *thought*, making experience and the objective world dependent on the *understanding* while refusing to make it a faculty of *intuition*. In this respect he does distinguish intuiting from thinking, yet makes individual things partly objects of intuition and partly of thought. In fact they are only the former. Our empirical intuition is already *objective* because it begins from causal connection. Its immediate objects are things, not representations distinct from them. Individual things as such are intuited through the senses and understanding; imagination immediately supplements the *one-sided* impression on the senses. Once we pass to *thinking*, by contrast, we leave individual things and deal with general, non-intuitive concepts, though we may later apply the results to individual things. Holding to this distinction, we see that intuitive things do not first gain reality and become experience through thought applying twelve categories. Empirical reality, and thus experience, is already given in intuition itself. Yet intuition can occur only by applying cognition of causal connection, the understanding's sole function, to sensory sensation. Intuition is therefore genuinely intellectual, precisely what Kant denies.

The Kantian assumption criticized here is particularly clear not only in the cited passage but at the start of §36 of the *Critique of Judgment* and in the note to the first definition of “Phenomenology” in the *Metaphysical Foundations of Natural Science*. It is stated most plainly, with a naïveté Kant least dared at this delicate point, in the work of a Kantian, Kiesewetter's *Outline of General Logic*, third edition, part 1, p. 434 of the exposition, and part 2, §§52–53 of the exposition, as well as in Tieftrunk's *Theory of Thought in Pure German*. The unthinking students of a thinker become magnifying glasses for his faults. Kant treaded softly throughout his account of the category doctrine he had once decided on; his students speak boldly, exposing its falsity.

On this account, Kant's object of the categories is not the thing in itself, but is its nearest relative. It is the *object in itself*, an object needing no subject: an individual thing but not in space and time, since it is not intuitive; an object of thought but not an abstract concept. Kant thus really distinguishes three things: (1) representation; (2) the object of representation; and (3) the thing in itself. The first belongs to sensibility, which for him includes sensation and the pure intuitive forms of space and time. The second belongs to the understanding, which *adds it in thought* through twelve categories. The third lies beyond everything knowable. See pp. 108–109 of the first edition of the *Critique of Pure Reason* for evidence. Yet the distinction between a representation and its object is groundless. Berkeley had already proved this, and it follows from my whole account in the first book, especially chapter 1 of the supplements, and even from Kant's own fully idealist fundamental view in the first edition. If one did not count the object of representation as representation and identify them, one would have to place it with the thing in itself. Ultimately it depends on what one means by “object.” In any case, clear reflection finds only representation and thing in itself. The unwarranted insertion of this hybrid, the object of representation, is the source of Kant's errors. Remove it and his doctrine of categories as *a priori* concepts also falls. They are not supposed to contribute anything to intuition or apply to the thing in itself; through them we are supposed only to think those “objects of representations” and thereby turn representation into experience.

Every empirical intuition, however, already is experience. Every intuition beginning with sensory sensation is empirical. The understanding relates that sensation to its cause by its sole function, *a priori* cognition of the causal law. The cause thus appears in space and time, forms of pure intuition, as an object of experience, a material object persisting through time in space, yet even as such always remaining representation, just as space and time do. If we wish to go beyond this representation, we face the question of the thing in itself, the theme of my whole work and indeed of all metaphysics. Connected with Kant's error is the fault criticized earlier: he offers no theory of how empirical intuition arises. He simply takes it as *given*, identifying it with mere sensory sensation, to which he adds the intuitive forms of space and time, both included under sensibility. Yet those materials alone do not yield an objective representation. That strictly requires relating sensation to its cause, applying the causal law, and therefore the understanding. Without this, sensation remains subjective and places no object in space, even if space is supplied. But for Kant the understanding must not be used in intuition; it was only supposed to *think*, so that he could remain within Transcendental Logic. This connects with another of his faults: he left to me the only valid proof of the correctly recognized *a priori* nature of the causal law, namely the proof from the very possibility of objective empirical intuition. Instead he gave a plainly false proof, as I showed in my essay on the principle of sufficient reason, §23.

It is clear that Kant's “object of representation” (2) is assembled from what he took partly from representation (1) and partly from the thing in itself (3). If experience truly arose only when our understanding applied twelve different functions and used as many *a priori* concepts to *think* objects previously merely intuited, each real thing would have many determinations. Given *a priori*, like space and time, they could not be thought away and would belong essentially to a thing's existence, yet could not be derived from the properties of space and time. Only one such determination exists: causality. Materiality rests on it, since matter's essence lies in acting and is causality through and through (see volume 2, chapter 4). Materiality alone distinguishes a real thing from an image of fantasy, which is still a representation. As persisting, matter gives a thing persistence through time in its material, while forms change according to causality. Everything else in a thing is either a determination of space or time or an empirical property, all ultimately referring to its efficacy and therefore to more specific determinations of causality. Causality already enters empirical intuition as a condition. Intuition is thus the understanding's work; it makes intuition possible and contributes nothing further to experience or its possibility beyond the causal law. Except for what I have specified, the old ontologies contain only relations among things or to our reflection, and a gathered *farrago* (jumble).

The very exposition of the category doctrine is a sign that it lacks foundation. What a contrast between the Transcendental *Aesthetic* and Transcendental *Analytic*! *There* we find clarity, definiteness, assurance, firm conviction openly expressed and communicated without fail. All is bright; no dark corners are left. Kant knows what he wants and knows he is right. *Here* all is dark, confused, indefinite, wavering, uncertain; the exposition is anxious, full of excuses and appeals to what will come later or has supposedly been held back. The entire second and third sections of the deduction of pure concepts of understanding were changed completely in the second edition because Kant himself was dissatisfied. They differ wholly from the first but have not become clearer. One really sees him struggling against the truth to maintain a doctrine he had settled on. In the Transcendental *Aesthetic*, he actually proves all his propositions from undeniable facts of consciousness. In the Transcendental *Analytic*, examination reveals only assertions that this is and must be so. Here as everywhere the exposition bears the character of the thinking from which it came: style is the physiognomy of the mind.

It is also worth noting that almost whenever Kant wants to illustrate a claim he chooses the category of causality. His claim then turns out right, precisely because the causal law is the real and sole form of the understanding; the other eleven categories are blind windows. The deduction of the categories is simpler and more direct in the first edition than in the second. He tries to show how the understanding, thinking through categories after intuition is given by sensibility, produces experience. He repeats “recognition,” “reproduction,” “association,” “apprehension,” and “transcendental unity of apperception” to exhaustion without attaining clarity. Most strikingly, in this explanation he never once touches the point that must occur to everyone first: relating sensory sensation to its external cause. If he rejected that relation, he should have said so explicitly, but he does not. He slips around it, followed by all the Kantians. The hidden motive is that he reserves causal connection under the name “ground of appearance” for his false derivation of the thing in itself. Moreover, relating sensation to its cause would make intuition intellectual, which he cannot admit. He also seems to have feared that accepting a causal connection between sensation and object would turn the latter immediately into the thing in itself and introduce Locke's empiricism. But reflection removes this difficulty by reminding us that the causal law is subjective in origin, as sensation itself is, and that our own body insofar as it appears in space already belongs to representations. His fear of Berkeley's idealism prevented Kant from conceding it.


Kant repeatedly calls “combining the manifold of intuition” the understanding's essential operation through its twelve categories. Yet he never properly explains this or shows what the manifold of intuition is before the understanding combines it. Time and space, with its three dimensions, are *continua*: their parts are originally connected, not separate. They are the universal forms of our intuition. Everything appearing or given in them thus already appears as a continuum, its parts connected from the start and needing no added combination of a manifold. Perhaps one interprets the supposed combination as relating the various sensory impressions of an object to one object: for example, intuiting a bell, I recognize that what affects my eyes as yellow, my hands as smooth and hard, and my ears as sounding is one body. But this follows from *a priori* knowledge of causal connection, the real and sole function of the understanding. The diverse effects on my sense organs direct me to one common cause, the character of the body before me. Despite the variety and number of effects, my understanding apprehends the unity of the cause as one object that becomes intuitive through precisely that process.

In the fine summary of his teaching in the *Critique of Pure Reason*, pp. 719–726; fifth edition pp. 747–754, Kant explains the categories perhaps more clearly than anywhere: as “the mere rule for synthesizing what perception has given *a posteriori*.” He seems to have something in mind like angles giving a rule for putting lines together when constructing a triangle; that image best illustrates what he says of the categories' function. The preface to *Metaphysical Foundations of Natural Science* contains a long note likewise explaining the categories. They “differ in nothing from the formal acts of understanding in judging,” it says, except that in judgments subject and predicate may exchange places. It defines judgment as “an act through which given representations first become cognitions of an object.” Animals, being unable to judge, must then know no objects at all. For Kant generally, there are only concepts of *objects*, not intuitions. I say instead: objects exist first for intuition, and concepts are always abstractions from it. Abstract thought must therefore conform exactly to the world present in intuition, since reference to that world alone gives concepts content. We should assume no other *a priori* form for concepts than the faculty of reflection generally. Its nature is the formation of concepts, abstract, non-intuitive representations, the sole function of *reason*, as shown in the first book. I therefore demand that we throw eleven categories out the window and retain only causality, recognizing that its activity is already a condition of empirical intuition. Intuition is thus not merely sensory but intellectual. The object so intuited, the object of experience, is one with representation; only the thing in itself remains to be distinguished from it.

Repeated study of the *Critique of Pure Reason* at different ages has led me to a conviction about the origin of Transcendental Logic that I share because it greatly helps one understand it. The discovery based on objective apprehension and the highest human reflection is solely the insight that we know time and space *a priori*. Delighted by this fortunate find, Kant wanted to follow the vein further, guided by his love of architectural symmetry. He had found pure *a priori* intuition underlying *empirical intuition* as its condition. He therefore supposed that certain *pure concepts* must likewise underlie *empirically acquired concepts* as presuppositions in our faculty of cognition, and that actual empirical thinking must first be made possible by pure *a priori* thinking. This pure thinking would have no objects of its own, but would take them from intuition. As the *Transcendental Aesthetic* demonstrated an *a priori* foundation for mathematics, there should be one for logic, so that a *Transcendental Logic* formed a symmetrical counterpart to the Aesthetic. From this point Kant was no longer unbiased, no longer simply inquiring into and observing what is in consciousness. A presupposition guided him and he pursued an aim: to find what he had presupposed so that an analogous, symmetrical Transcendental Logic could be built as a second story on the happily discovered Aesthetic. He turned to the table of judgments and constructed from it, as best he could, the *table of categories*, a doctrine of twelve pure *a priori* concepts. They were supposed to be conditions of our *thinking* the same *things* whose *intuition* is conditioned by the two *a priori* forms of sensibility. A *pure understanding* now corresponded symmetrically to *pure sensibility*.

Then another thought offered him a way of making the account more plausible through the *schematism* of pure concepts of the understanding. This most clearly betrays the course of his procedure, of which he himself was unaware. Seeking an *a priori* analogue for every empirical function of cognition, he noticed that our empirical intuiting and our empirical thinking in abstract, non-intuitive concepts are often, though not always, mediated. Occasionally we try to return from abstract thought to intuition, but only as a test: we want to assure ourselves that our abstract thought has not moved too far from intuition's firm ground, gone astray, or become empty words, rather as someone walking in the dark reaches now and then for the wall that guides him. Tentatively and momentarily, we return to intuition by summoning in imagination an intuition corresponding to the concept occupying us. It can never fully match the concept, but serves only as its temporary *representative*. I explained this in my essay *On the Principle of Sufficient Reason*, §28. Kant calls such a fleeting phantom, in contrast to a complete imaginative image, a *schema*, a kind of monogram of imagination. He then asserts an analogous intermediate *a priori schema of pure concepts of the understanding* between the faculty of *a priori* intuition of pure sensibility and the *a priori* thinking of pure understanding, the categories. He describes them one by one as monograms of pure *a priori* imagination, assigning each to its category in the strange “chapter on the schematism of pure concepts of understanding.” Famous for its obscurity, it has never made anyone wiser. Seen from my standpoint, its darkness clears, but its deliberately contrived character and Kant's prior determination to find something serving analogy and architectural symmetry become even more conspicuous here than anywhere, almost to the point of comedy.

In assuming schemas of pure, *contentless a priori* concepts corresponding to the empirical schemas that represent actual concepts in imagination, he overlooks that the purpose of schemas is wholly absent here. In empirical, actual thinking, schemas serve only a concept's *material content*. Since empirical concepts were abstracted from intuition, we help and orient ourselves in abstract thought by glancing back at the intuition from which they came, assuring ourselves that the thought still has real content. This necessarily presupposes that the concepts arose from intuition. It is merely a look back at their material content, an aid to our weakness. But *a priori* concepts have no content yet and cannot admit of such a look back. They did not arise from intuition but approach it from within to receive content for the first time. I dwell on this because it illuminates the hidden course of Kant's philosophizing. After the fortunate discovery of the two *a priori* forms of intuition, he sought by analogy an *a priori* counterpart to every feature of our empirical cognition. With the schemas, he extended this even to a merely psychological fact. The apparent depth and difficulty of the exposition conceal from the reader that its content remains a wholly arbitrary and unproved assumption. Someone who finally penetrates its meaning is easily tempted to mistake this hard-won understanding for conviction that the doctrine is true.

Had Kant instead proceeded without prejudice, purely observing as he did in discovering *a priori* intuitions, he would have found that when pure intuition of space and time becomes empirical, two things are added: sensation on one side and cognition of causality on the other. The latter transforms mere sensation into objective empirical intuition. For just this reason it is not borrowed or learned from intuition but exists *a priori* as the form and function of pure understanding, its sole function, though so consequential that all our empirical knowledge rests on it. It is often said that refuting an error is complete only when one has shown how it arose psychologically. I believe I have done so here for Kant's doctrine of categories and their schemas.

Having introduced such grave errors into the first simple outlines of a theory of representation, Kant was led to many complicated assumptions. One is the synthetic unity of apperception, a very strange thing strangely presented: “The *I think* must be able to accompany all my representations.” Must—be able to: this is a problematic-apodictic proposition, one that takes with one hand what it gives with the other. What does this precariously balanced claim mean? That all representation is thinking? No. That would be disastrous: there would be nothing but abstract concepts, least of all a pure intuition free from reflection and will, like contemplation of beauty, the deepest apprehension of things' true essence, their Platonic Ideas. Animals too would then either have to think or lack representations entirely. Or does the claim mean “no object without subject”? If so, it says it very badly and comes too late. Taking Kant's statements together, his synthetic unity of apperception seems to be the extensionless center of the sphere of all our representations, toward which their radii converge. It is what I call the subject of cognition, the correlate of every representation. I also described and discussed it fully in chapter 22 of the second volume as the focal point toward which the rays of brain activity converge. I refer there to avoid repetition.

It follows from my critique above that I reject the whole doctrine of the categories and count it among the groundless assumptions with which Kant encumbered his theory of cognition. I showed contradictions in Transcendental Logic arising from the mixture of intuitive and abstract cognition and the lack of any clear, definite conception of understanding and reason. Kant's works offered only disconnected, inconsistent, meager, and incorrect remarks about those two faculties. I have given definite, clear accounts of them in the first book and its supplements, and more fully in *On the Principle of Sufficient Reason*, §§21, 26, and 34. They follow plainly from examining our cognition's nature and agree fully with the meanings of these faculties expressed, if not clearly formulated, in ordinary speech and writing across peoples and ages. Exposing the faults in Kant's account has largely defended my own against it. But the table of judgments underlying Kant's theory of thought, indeed his whole philosophy, is by and large correct in itself. I must therefore show how these general forms of judgment arise from our faculty of cognition and reconcile them with my account. In the discussion I shall always use “understanding” and “reason” in the sense of my own explanations, which I presume the reader knows.

An essential difference between Kant's method and mine is that he begins from mediated, reflective cognition and I from immediate, intuitive cognition. He resembles someone measuring a tower's height from its shadow, while I apply the measuring rod directly. Philosophy is for him a science *from* concepts; for me it is a science *in* concepts, drawn from intuitive cognition, the sole source of evidence, and fixed in general concepts. He skips over the whole varied, meaningful intuitive world surrounding us and holds to forms of abstract thought. An unstated premise underlies this: reflection is a copy of all intuition, so everything essential in intuition must be expressible in reflection, and in compressed forms and outlines easily surveyed. The essential laws of abstract cognition would then put into our hands all the strings moving the colorful puppet show of the intuitive world before our eyes. Had Kant at least stated this highest methodological principle clearly and followed it consistently, he would have had to keep intuitive and abstract cognition distinct, sparing us insoluble contradictions and confusion. But the way he carried out his task shows that the principle was only very dimly before his mind. Only after thorough study of his philosophy can one infer it.


The method and guiding maxim just described have much to recommend them and are a brilliant idea. The nature of every science is to bring the endless variety of intuitive appearances under comparatively few abstract concepts, arranging from them a system by which we gain full cognitive command of those appearances, explain what has happened, and predict what will happen. The sciences divide among themselves the broad domain of appearances according to their varied particular kinds. It was a bold and fortunate thought to isolate what is essential to concepts as such, apart from their content, and to learn from these forms of all thinking what must be essential to all intuitive cognition and thus to the world as appearance generally. Because the necessary forms of thinking would be found *a priori*, their origin would be subjective, serving Kant's purposes. Before going further, however, he needed to investigate how reflection relates to intuitive cognition, which presupposes that the two be carefully distinguished. How exactly does it reproduce and represent intuition? Does it do so faithfully, or does it alter and partly obscure intuition by taking it into its own forms? Is abstract, reflective cognition's form determined more by intuitive cognition's form or by the fixed character belonging to reflection itself? Very different things in intuitive cognition may become indistinguishable when brought into reflection; conversely, distinctions noticed in reflective cognition may arise only there, without corresponding differences in intuition.

Such an inquiry would have shown that intuitive cognition changes on entering reflection almost as much as food changes on entering an animal body, whose own forms and mixtures determine the result and whose composition no longer reveals the food's original character. Or, since that says a little too much, it would at least have shown that reflection is not to intuition as an image in water is to the objects reflected. It is more nearly their shadow, reproducing only some outer contours while uniting very different things in one shape and presenting unlike things with the same outline. We could never confidently and completely reconstruct the forms of things from such a shadow.

All reflective cognition, or reason, has one chief form, the abstract concept. It belongs to reason itself and has no necessary direct relation to the intuitive world, which exists without it for animals and could be quite different while still fitting the same form of reflection. Combining concepts into judgments has certain fixed, lawful forms. Found through induction, these make up the table of judgments. Most can be derived from reflective cognition itself, directly from reason, particularly insofar as they arise through the four laws of thought, which I call metalogical truths, and the *dictum de omni et nullo* (“what is affirmed or denied of all is affirmed or denied of each”). Some forms are grounded instead in intuitive cognition, and thus in the understanding. Yet they do not indicate a corresponding plurality of forms of understanding: they all derive from its one function, immediate cognition of cause and effect. Others arise from the meeting and connection of reflective with intuitive cognition, more properly from taking the latter into the former. I shall examine the features of judgment one by one and trace each to these sources. It will follow that no deduction of categories from them remains possible, and that the categories are as groundless in assumption as their presentation is confused and self-contradictory.

**1. Quantity.** The so-called *quantity* of judgments springs from concepts as such. It is grounded solely in reason, without any direct connection to understanding or intuitive cognition. As explained in the first book, concepts essentially have an extension or sphere: a wider, less definite one contains a narrower, more definite one, which can be separated out. One may do so by marking it merely as some indefinite part of the wider concept, or by isolating it definitely and completely under a special name. In the first case the judgment is particular; in the second universal. The same part of the sphere of “tree,” for example, can be isolated in either way: “Some trees bear gallnuts,” or “All oaks bear gallnuts.” The operations differ very little, and their very possibility depends on the language's richness. Yet Kant calls the difference two fundamentally distinct acts, functions, or categories of pure understanding, determining experience *a priori*.

Finally one may use a concept to reach a definite individual intuitive representation from which, along with many others, that concept was abstracted. This is done by a singular judgment, marking the boundary of abstract and intuitive cognition and leading directly to the latter: “This tree here bears gallnuts.” Kant made even that a separate category. After everything said above, no further argument is needed here.

**2. Quality.** The *quality* of judgments likewise lies wholly within reason and is no copy of, or clue to, a law of the understanding that makes intuition possible. As also explained in the first book, the nature of abstract concepts, the essence of reason itself grasped objectively, permits their spheres to be joined and separated. The general laws of identity and contradiction presuppose this possibility. I call their truth *metalogical* because they spring purely from reason and admit no further explanation. They require what has been joined to remain joined and what has been separated to remain separate: what is posited cannot simultaneously be cancelled. They therefore presuppose the possibility of joining and separating conceptual spheres, that is, of judging. In its *form*, this activity lies solely in reason. Unlike the *content* of judgments, the form is not brought across from intuitive cognition in the understanding. No correlate or analogue for it is to be sought there. Once intuition has arisen through and for the understanding, it stands complete, subject to neither doubt nor error and knowing neither affirmation nor negation. It presents itself directly, rather than depending, as abstract rational cognition does, for worth and content on reference to something outside itself according to the principle of sufficient reason of knowing. Intuition is therefore sheer reality; negation is foreign to it and can only be added by reflection, remaining within abstract thought.

Kant adds infinite judgments to affirmative and negative ones, borrowing a whim of the old scholastics. An ingenious gap-filler needing no discussion, it is one of the many blind windows he installed for the sake of symmetrical architecture.


**3. Relation.** Under the very broad heading of relation Kant brings together three quite different features of judgments. To find their origins we must consider each separately.

**(a) Hypothetical judgment.** In general, a *hypothetical judgment* abstractly expresses the most general form of all our knowledge, the principle of sufficient reason. I showed in my essay of 1813 on that principle that it has four quite different meanings, each arising from a different faculty of cognition and concerning a different class of representations. Thus hypothetical judgment as a general form of thought cannot originate solely, as Kant wishes, in the understanding and its category of causality. On my account, the causal law is the one form of cognition of pure understanding, and only one form of the principle of sufficient reason encompassing all pure or *a priori* cognition. Each meaning of the principle is expressed in hypothetical judgment. Here we see clearly how cognitions quite different in origin and significance can nonetheless appear, when reason thinks them *in abstracto*, in the same form of connection among concepts and judgments. In that form they can no longer be distinguished; we have to leave abstraction and return to intuition to distinguish them. Kant's method of trying to discover the elements and inner workings of intuitive cognition from the standpoint of abstract cognition was therefore entirely mistaken. In a sense my whole introductory essay *On the Principle of Sufficient Reason* thoroughly examines the meaning of hypothetical judgment, so I shall not dwell on it here.

**(b) Categorical judgment.** The form of the *categorical judgment* is simply the form of judgment in its strictest sense. Strictly, to judge means only to think the joining or incompatibility of conceptual spheres. Hypothetical and disjunctive connections are not, in fact, distinct forms of judgment. They apply only to judgments already made, within which concepts remain connected categorically; the new forms link judgments to one another, the hypothetical expressing dependence and the disjunctive incompatibility. Mere concepts have only *one* kind of relation to one another, expressed by categorical judgment. More exact determinations, or kinds, of that relation are the overlap and complete separation of conceptual spheres, affirmation and negation. Kant makes these separate categories under another heading, *quality*. Overlap and separation in turn differ according to whether spheres overlap wholly or partly, a distinction forming the *quantity* of judgments. Kant makes yet another separate category heading from this. He thus separated closely related, even identical matters—readily surveyed variations of the sole possible relations among mere concepts—while uniting quite different matters under the heading of relation.

Categorical judgments have the laws of thought of identity and contradiction as their metalogical principle. The *ground* for joining conceptual spheres, which gives the judgment consisting in that union its *truth*, may nevertheless be of very different kinds. Its truth may accordingly be logical, empirical, metaphysical, or metalogical, as explained in my introductory essay, §§30–33, without need of repetition here. Thus immediate cognitions of the most diverse sorts can all present themselves *in abstracto* as a connection of the spheres of two concepts, subject and predicate. No one function of understanding can be posited as producing and corresponding to all of them. For example, “The water boils,” “The sine measures the angle,” “The will decides,” “Occupation distracts,” and “The distinction is difficult” express very different relations in the same logical form. This again confirms the mistake of starting from abstract cognition to analyze immediate intuitive cognition.

A categorical judgment does arise from cognition of the understanding in my sense when it expresses causality. This applies even to judgments naming a physical quality. To say “This body is heavy, hard, liquid, green, acidic, alkaline, organic,” and so on always names its action or effect, a cognition possible only through pure understanding. Yet physical qualities and quite different matters, such as the subordination of very abstract concepts, are both expressed *in abstracto* by subject and predicate. People therefore transferred those merely conceptual relations back to intuitive cognition, thinking that subject and predicate in a judgment must have special intuitive correlates, substance and accident. I shall show below that “substance” has no true content other than “matter,” and that accidents are simply modes of action. Supposed cognition of substance and accident thus remains pure understanding's cognition of cause and effect. I discussed how the representation of matter arises in §4 of the first book and more accessibly at the end of §21, p. 77, of *On the Principle of Sufficient Reason*. We shall examine it further in discussing the principle of substance's persistence.

**(c) Disjunctive judgment.** *Disjunctive judgments* arise from the law of thought of the excluded middle, a metalogical truth. They thus belong wholly to pure reason and do not originate in understanding. Deriving the category of community or *reciprocal interaction* from them is a glaring example of the violence Kant sometimes does to truth merely to indulge architectural symmetry. G. E. Schulze in his *Critique of Theoretical Philosophy* and Berg in his *Epicritique of Philosophy*, among others, have rightly criticized this derivation and shown it invalid on several grounds. What real analogy is there between leaving a concept's determination open among mutually exclusive predicates and thinking reciprocal interaction? The two are actually opposites. In a disjunctive judgment, positing one member of the division necessarily abolishes the other. In reciprocal interaction, positing one thing is supposedly a necessary positing of the other and conversely. The real logical analogue of reciprocal interaction is surely the *circulus vitiosus* (vicious circle), where what is grounded becomes the ground in turn, just as reciprocal interaction supposedly does. As logic rejects the vicious circle, metaphysics should ban reciprocal interaction. I intend seriously to show that no reciprocal interaction exists in the proper sense. However popular the term is because it is so vague, close inspection reveals it as empty, false, and void.

First recall what causality is in general, consulting my introductory essay, §20; my prize essay on freedom of the will, chapter 3, p. 27 ff. [2nd ed., p. 26 ff.]; and chapter 4 of our second volume. Causality is the law by which emerging *states* of matter determine their places in time. It concerns states, strictly *changes*, not matter as such or persistence without change. *Matter* itself does not stand under the causal law: it neither comes to be nor passes away. Neither does the whole *thing*, as people commonly say; only *states* of matter do. The causal law likewise has nothing to do with *persistence*: where nothing *changes*, there is no *acting* and no causality, only an enduring state at rest. When such a state changes, either the new state persists or it immediately produces a third state. The necessity with which this happens is the causal law, a form of the principle of sufficient reason. It cannot be explained further, for the principle of sufficient reason is the principle of every explanation and necessity. It is clear that cause and effect are necessarily and closely tied to *succession in time*. State A is a cause and state B an effect only insofar as A precedes B and their succession is necessary rather than accidental, not merely a following but a following-from.

*Reciprocal interaction*, however, means that each is both cause and effect of the other. That would make each both earlier and later: a contradiction. Nor can we assume both *states* exist at once and necessarily together. If they necessarily belong together and coexist, they make only *one* state whose persistence requires all its determinations to remain present. We are then speaking not of change and causality but only of duration and rest. If *one* determination of the whole state changes, the resulting new state may not endure; it causes changes in the other determinations of the first state, producing another, third state. All this occurs under the simple causal law and establishes no new law of reciprocal interaction.


I also assert flatly that *reciprocal interaction* cannot be supported by a single example. Whatever is offered is either a state at rest, to which causality, meaningful only for changes, does not apply at all, or an alternating succession of states of the same kind conditioning one another, fully explained by simple causality. Balanced scales with equal weights provide the first sort. Nothing acts here because nothing changes. It is a state of rest. Weight strives, equally distributed as in every body supported at its center of gravity, but cannot display its force in an effect. Removing *one* weight gives a second state that immediately causes a third, the other pan's descent. This follows the simple law of cause and effect and needs neither a special category of understanding nor even a special name.

A continuing fire illustrates the second sort. Oxygen's combination with combustible matter causes heat, which in turn causes that chemical combination to occur anew. This is simply a chain of causes and effects whose links alternate between states bearing the *same names*: burning A produces free heat B; this produces new burning C, another effect of the same kind as cause A but not individually identical with it; C produces new heat D, identical with effect B only conceptually, by name, not in reality; and so on. Humboldt's account of deserts (*Views of Nature*, second edition, vol. 2, p. 79) gives an appealing example of what ordinary speech calls reciprocal interaction. Sandy deserts receive no rain, while the forested mountains bordering them do. The cause is not the mountains attracting clouds. A rising column of heated air from the sand prevents vapor droplets from condensing and drives clouds upward. Above the mountains the upward air current is weaker; clouds sink and precipitation occurs in cooler air. Lack of rain and lack of vegetation in the desert thus stand in so-called reciprocal interaction: no rain falls because the hot sandy surface radiates more heat, and the desert fails to become a steppe or grassy plain because rain does not fall. Plainly this is again a succession of causes and effects bearing the same names, not something essentially different from simple causality. The same holds of a pendulum's swing and even of an organism's self-preservation: each state produces a new one of the same kind as the state that produced it, though individually new. In the organism the matter is more complex. The chain has links of many kinds rather than two, and a link of the same kind returns only after several others have intervened. Yet we still see only the one simple causal law giving order to a succession of states, nothing to be grasped by a new and special faculty of understanding.

Perhaps someone would cite the equality of action and reaction as an instance of reciprocal interaction. But that rests on what I have insisted on and explained in *On the Principle of Sufficient Reason*: cause and effect are not two bodies but two successive states of bodies. Each state includes all the bodies involved. The effect, the newly arising state after an impact, for example, extends to both bodies proportionately. The striking body changes as much as the struck body, each in proportion to its mass and speed. If one chooses to call this reciprocal interaction, then absolutely every effect is reciprocal interaction; no new concept or function of understanding is introduced, only a redundant synonym for causality. Kant himself carelessly says just this in *Metaphysical Foundations of Natural Science*, where the proof of the fourth proposition of mechanics begins: “All external action in the world is reciprocal interaction.” How, then, can simple causality and reciprocal interaction have separate *a priori* functions in the understanding? How can real succession be possible and knowable only by one and coexistence only by the other? If every action is reciprocal interaction, succession and simultaneity would be the same, and everything in the world simultaneous. Were there genuine reciprocal interaction, a *perpetuum mobile* (perpetual motion machine) would be possible, even *a priori* certain. The claim that it is impossible rests instead on the *a priori* conviction that no genuine reciprocal interaction or form of understanding for it exists.

Aristotle too denies reciprocal interaction in the strict sense. He observes that two things can be causes of one another only if “cause” has a different meaning for each: one may act on the other as an aim, the other on the first as a cause of motion. In both *Physics*, II, chapter 3, and *Metaphysics*, V, chapter 2, he says: Ἔστι δέ τινα καὶ ἀλλήλων αἴτια· οἷον τὸ πονεῖν αἴτιον τῆς εὐεξίας, καὶ αὕτη τοῦ πονεῖν· ἀλλ’ οὐ τὸν αὐτὸν τρόπον, ἀλλὰ τὸ μὲν ὡς τέλος, τὸ δὲ ὡς ἀρχὴ κινήσεως. (*Sunt praeterea quae sibi sunt mutuo causae, ut exercitium bonae habitudinis, et haec exercitii: at non eodem modo, sed haec ut finis, illud ut principium motus.* “Some things are mutually causes, as exercise causes good condition and good condition causes exercise, but not in the same way: one as an end, the other as a source of motion.”) Had he accepted reciprocal interaction proper, he would have listed it there, since he is enumerating all possible kinds of causes in both places. In *Posterior Analytics*, II, chapter 11, he speaks of a cycle of causes and effects but not reciprocal interaction.

**4. Modality.** The categories of *modality* have one advantage over all the others: what each expresses really does correspond to the form of judgment from which it is derived. That is scarcely so for the other categories, mostly extracted from judgment forms by the most arbitrary force. It is perfectly true that the concepts possible, actual, and necessary occasion the problematic, assertoric, and apodictic forms of judgment. It is not true that they are special, original forms of understanding's cognition that admit no further derivation. Rather, they spring from the one original form of all cognition known to us *a priori*, the principle of sufficient reason. Knowledge of *necessity* comes directly from it; chance, possibility, impossibility, and actuality arise only when reflection is applied to necessity. None springs from a single mental faculty, the understanding. They arise instead from the encounter of abstract and intuitive cognition, as will now become clear.

I maintain that to be necessary and to follow from a given ground are convertible concepts, wholly identical. We can know, indeed think, something as necessary only insofar as we regard it as following from a given ground. Necessity contains absolutely nothing beyond dependence, being posited by another, and inevitably following from it. The concept thus arises and exists solely through application of the principle of sufficient reason. According to that principle's different forms, there is physical necessity, an effect following a cause; logical necessity, through a ground of knowing in analytic judgments and inferences; mathematical necessity, through the ground of being in space and time; and practical necessity. By the last I do not mean determination by a supposed categorical imperative, but an act necessarily occurring from the empirical character when motives are presented. Everything necessary is so only relatively, on the presupposition of the ground from which it follows; *absolute necessity* is therefore a contradiction. For more, see §49 of my essay on the principle of sufficient reason.

The contradictory opposite of necessity, its negation, is *contingency*. This concept has negative content: nothing more than the absence of the connection expressed by the principle of sufficient reason. The contingent is therefore always relative, contingent in relation to something that is *not* its ground. Every object, of whatever kind, and every event in the actual world, is at once necessary and contingent: *necessary* in relation to its cause and *contingent* in relation to everything else. Its contact in time and space with everything else is merely a meeting without a necessary connection; hence the words *chance*, σύμπτωμα, and *contingens* (what happens to come together). An absolutely contingent thing is no more conceivable than an absolutely necessary one. It would be an object bearing no relation of consequence to ground with any other object. That it cannot be represented is precisely the negative content of the principle of sufficient reason. One would have to abolish that principle to think an absolutely contingent thing; then “contingent” itself would lose all meaning, since it has meaning only in relation to the principle: it says two objects are not related as ground and consequence.

In nature as intuitive representation, everything that happens is necessary, since it follows from its cause. Consider that event in relation to something else that did not cause it, and we call it contingent; this is already abstract reflection. Now abstract from both causal relation and necessity or contingency in considering a natural object. The remaining kind of cognition is covered by *actuality*: one considers only the *effect*, without looking for the cause in relation to which it would be called *necessary* or for other things in relation to which it would be *contingent*. Ultimately this rests on the fact that a judgment's modality indicates less an objective feature of things than the relation of our cognition to them. Everything in nature arises from a cause, so everything *actual* is also *necessary*, but only insofar as it exists at *this time* and *this place*: causal determination extends no further. Leaving intuitive nature for abstract thought, we can reflect on all natural laws, some known *a priori*, some only *a posteriori*. The abstract representation contains everything existing in nature at *any* place and time, while abstracting from every specific place and time. Reflection thus takes us into the broad realm of *possibility*. What has no place even there is *impossible*. Possibility and impossibility plainly exist only for reflection and abstract rational cognition, not for intuition. Nevertheless intuition's pure forms provide reason with the means of determining possible and impossible. Depending on whether the natural laws from which we proceed are known *a priori* or *a posteriori*, possibility or impossibility is metaphysical or merely physical.

This account needs no proof beyond knowledge of the principle of sufficient reason and the development of the concepts necessary, actual, and possible. It shows how entirely groundless Kant's assumption of three distinct functions of understanding for those concepts is; again no misgiving checked his architectural symmetry.

There is a further, very serious error. Following earlier philosophy, Kant confuses necessity and contingency. Earlier philosophy abused abstraction in this way. Plainly, when a ground is posited, what follows cannot fail to occur and is therefore necessary. But it fixed on this last feature alone and said: necessary is what cannot be otherwise, or whose opposite is impossible. It forgot the ground and root of necessity and overlooked the resulting relativity of all necessity. Thus it invented the utterly unthinkable *absolutely necessary*: something whose existence would be as unavoidable as a consequence following its ground, yet would not itself follow from any ground or depend on anything. The last addition is an absurd request contradicting the principle of sufficient reason. Starting from this fiction, philosophy then called precisely everything posited by a ground *contingent*, in direct opposition to the truth. It compared the relativity of its necessity with that entirely invented, self-contradictory *absolute* necessity.[^4] Kant retains and offers this fundamentally mistaken definition of contingency in the *Critique of Pure Reason*, fifth edition pp. 289–291, 243, 301, 419, 447, 486, and 488. He even plainly contradicts himself at p. 301: “Everything contingent has a cause,” while adding, “Contingent is what can fail to exist.” Yet if something has a cause, it cannot fail to exist; it is necessary.

The source of the mistaken definition of necessary and contingent is already in Aristotle, *De generatione et corruptione*, II, chapters 9 and 11. He defines the necessary as what cannot fail to exist and opposes it to what cannot exist. Between them lies what may exist or not exist, that is, what comes to be and passes away, which he calls contingent. As explained above, this definition, like many of Aristotle's, stops at abstract concepts without returning to concrete intuition, the source that must always check them. “Something whose nonexistence is impossible” can perhaps be thought *in abstracto*, but when we seek an instance in concrete reality or intuition, we find nothing even potentially corresponding to it except the consequence of a given ground. Its necessity is relative and conditional.


I add a few more remarks here on these concepts of modality. Since all necessity rests on the principle of sufficient reason and is therefore relative, every *apodictic* judgment is originally and in its ultimate meaning *hypothetical*. It becomes *categorical* only when an *assertoric* minor premise is added, that is, in the conclusion. If the minor premise remains undecided and this uncertainty is expressed, the result is a *problematic* judgment.

What is apodictic generally, as a rule or law of nature, is only problematic in an individual case, since the condition bringing the case under the rule must first occur. Conversely, what is necessary, or apodictic, in an individual case—every particular change necessarily produced by its cause—is only problematic when stated generally. The cause that occurred affected only this case, whereas an apodictic and always hypothetical judgment states general laws, not individual cases directly. This follows because possibility exists only in reflection for reason, actuality in intuition for understanding, and necessity for both. Indeed, the distinction among necessary, actual, and possible exists strictly only *in abstracto*, in concepts. In the real world all three coincide. Everything that happens happens *necessarily* because it follows from causes that in turn have causes. All events in the world, large and small, form a strict chain of necessary occurrences. Thus everything actual is necessary, and in reality there is no difference between actuality and necessity. Nor is there a difference between actuality and possibility. What did not happen, and so did not become actual, was not possible either. Its necessary causes did not occur, and could not have occurred within the great causal chain. It was impossible. Every event, then, is either necessary or impossible.

This applies only to the empirically real world, the complex of individual things taken as wholly individual. If we use reason to consider things generally and apprehend them *in abstracto*, necessity, actuality, and possibility separate again. We recognize everything compatible with the *a priori* laws of our intellect as possible generally, and what accords with empirical natural laws as possible in this world, even if it never occurs. Thus we clearly distinguish possible from actual. Although the actual is always necessary in itself, it is recognized as such only by someone who knows its cause. Apart from the cause, it is called contingent. This account also unlocks the *contentio* (dispute) περὶ δυνατῶν (“about possibilities”) between Diodorus the Megarian and Chrysippus the Stoic recounted in Cicero's *De fato*. Diodorus says: “Only what becomes actual was possible; everything actual is also necessary.” Chrysippus says: “Much that never becomes actual is possible, for only what is necessary becomes actual.”[^modality-text] We can explain it as follows. Actuality is the conclusion of an inference whose premises furnish possibility. Both the major and minor premises are needed; together they yield full possibility. The major offers merely theoretical, general possibility *in abstracto*. It does not yet make anything possible in the sense of capable of becoming actual. The minor supplies that by placing the individual case under the rule. The case thereby becomes actual at once. For example:

Major: All houses, including mine, can burn down.

Minor: My house catches fire.

Conclusion: My house burns down.

Every general proposition, every major premise, determines things with respect to actuality only under a presupposition and hence hypothetically: a house's capacity to burn down presupposes its catching fire. The minor provides this presupposition. The major always loads the cannon; only when the minor brings the fuse does the shot, the conclusion, follow. This holds throughout for the relation of possibility to actuality. Since the conclusion stating actuality always follows *necessarily*, everything actual is necessary. The same follows from the meaning of necessity: being a consequence of a given ground. The ground of something actual is a cause; therefore all actuality is necessary. We see possible, actual, and necessary coincide. The last presupposes the first two, but they also presuppose the last. Our intellect's limitation by time keeps them apart: time mediates between possibility and actuality. Knowing all the causes of an individual event reveals its necessity completely. But the meeting of these different, independent causes seems *contingent* to us. Their independence of one another is precisely the concept of contingency. Yet each cause is in turn the necessary result of *its* cause in a beginningless chain. Contingency thus proves to be a merely subjective appearance arising from the limited horizon of our understanding, as subjective as the horizon of sight where sky touches earth.

Necessity, identical with following from a given ground, must appear in a distinct way in each form of the principle of sufficient reason, with an opposite in possibility and impossibility. That opposite arises only when reason applies abstract consideration to the object. Thus the four kinds of necessity mentioned above have four matching kinds of impossibility: physical, logical, mathematical, and practical. If we remain wholly within abstract concepts, possibility attaches to the broader concept and necessity to the narrower: “An animal *can* be a bird, fish, amphibian,” and so on; “a nightingale *must* be a bird, a bird an animal, an animal an organism, an organism a body.” This is because logical necessity, expressed by syllogism, proceeds from general to particular, never conversely. In intuitive nature, the first class of representations, everything is necessary under the causal law. Reflection alone can also regard it as contingent by comparing it with things that did not cause it, or as simply actual by ignoring every causal connection. Only this class of representations properly admits the concept *actual*, as the word's derivation from causality indicates. In the third class, pure mathematical intuition, there is only necessity so long as one remains within it. Possibility arises only by relating it to reflective concepts: “A triangle *can* be right-, obtuse-, or equiangular; it *must* have three angles equal to two right angles.” Here too we reach the *possible* only by passing from intuition to abstraction.

This account presupposes familiarity with my essay on the principle of sufficient reason and the first book of this work. It should leave no doubt about the true, very different origins of the forms of judgment in the table, or about the invalidity and complete groundlessness of positing twelve separate functions of understanding to explain them. Some simple observations already expose the latter. It takes great love of symmetry and faith in it as a guide to assume, for example, that an affirmative, a categorical, and an assertoric judgment are so fundamentally different that each warrants its own function of understanding.

Kant himself betrays awareness that his category doctrine cannot be sustained. In the third chapter of the Analytic of Principles, “Phenomena and Noumena,” he omitted several long passages from the first edition—pp. 241, 242, 244–246, and 248–253—in the second, because they disclosed its weakness too openly. On p. 241, for example, he says he did not define the individual categories because he could not, even if he wanted to: they are incapable of definition. He had forgotten his earlier claim on p. 82 of the same edition: “I deliberately refrain from defining the categories, though I might possess the definitions.” That was, if I may say so, bluster. He left the latter passage standing. All the passages prudently removed reveal that the categories admit of no clear conception and the whole doctrine stands on weak legs.

The table of categories is supposed to guide every metaphysical, indeed every scientific inquiry (*Prolegomena*, §39). It is the foundation of Kant's whole philosophy and the pattern of symmetry everywhere carried through it, as shown above. It became a true Procrustean bed into which he forces every possible consideration, by a violence I shall now examine further. What, then, did the *imitatores, servum pecus* (“imitators, a servile herd”) do? We have seen. The violence consists in setting aside and forgetting the meanings of the words naming the headings, forms of judgment, and categories, and clinging only to the words themselves. Some come from Aristotle's *Prior Analytics*, I, 23, περὶ ποιότητος καὶ ποσότητος τῶν τοῦ συλλογισμοῦ ὅρων (*de qualitate et quantitate terminorum syllogismi*; “on the quality and quantity of the terms of a syllogism”). But the names are arbitrary. The extension of concepts could have been called something other than *quantity*, although this term fits its subject better than the other category titles. *Quality* was plainly chosen through habit of pairing it with quantity, though it is an arbitrary name for affirmation and negation.

In every inquiry Kant undertakes, however, he puts any quantity in space and time, and any possible physical, moral, or other quality of things under those category headings. These matters share nothing with the headings for forms of judgment and thought but accidental, arbitrary names. One must remember all the respect owed Kant to avoid expressing anger at this procedure in harsh words. The pure physiological table of general principles of natural science gives the first example. What in the world has the quantity of judgments to do with the extensive magnitude of every intuition, or the quality of judgments with every sensation's degree? The former rests instead on space as the form of our outer intuition. The latter is merely an empirical and wholly subjective observation drawn from examining our sense organs. Again, in the table foundational to rational psychology (*Critique of Pure Reason*, p. 344; fifth edition p. 402), the soul's *simplicity* appears under quality. Yet simplicity is a quantitative property without any connection to affirmation or negation in judgment. Quantity was already filled by the *unity* of the soul, which is included in simplicity. Modality is then forced in absurdly: the soul stands in relation to *possible* objects. But relation belongs under relation, already occupied by substance. The four cosmological Ideas supplying the antinomies are likewise forced under the category headings. I shall discuss them in examining the antinomies. The table of *categories of freedom* in the *Critique of Practical Reason* gives still more glaring examples, as does the first book of the *Critique of Judgment*, which treats judgments of taste under the four category headings. Finally, the *Metaphysical Foundations of Natural Science* is entirely cut to the category table. This may be the principal cause of the false elements mixed here and there with that important work's true and excellent parts. Look at the end of its first chapter, where unity, plurality, and totality of the directions of lines supposedly correspond to categories named after the quantity of judgments.


The principle of the *persistence of substance* is derived from the category of subsistence and inherence. Yet we know that category only from the form of categorical judgments, the joining of two concepts as subject and predicate. How violently this great metaphysical principle is made to depend on a simple, purely logical form! It is only *pro forma* (for form's sake) and for symmetry. The proof Kant gives for it ignores its supposed origin in the understanding and category and proceeds from pure intuition of time. But this proof too is wholly wrong. It is false that mere time contains *simultaneity* and *duration*. These notions first arise from uniting space with time, as I showed in *On the Principle of Sufficient Reason*, §18, and more fully in §4 of this work; both discussions are needed to understand what follows. It is false that time itself *remains* amid all change. It is precisely what flows; a persisting time is a contradiction. Kant's proof is untenable despite his sophisms, indeed he plainly contradicts himself. At p. 177; fifth edition p. 219, he wrongly makes *coexistence* a mode of time; at p. 183; fifth edition p. 226, he rightly says, “*Coexistence* is not a mode of time, since no parts of time coexist, but all follow one another.” In truth, coexistence involves space as much as time. If two things coexist without being one, space distinguishes them. If two states of *one* thing coexist, such as a hot iron's glow and heat, they are simultaneous effects of *one* thing and presuppose matter and therefore space. Strictly, coexistence is a negative determination saying only that two things or states are not distinguished by time; their difference must be sought elsewhere.

Our knowledge of substance's persistence, that is, matter's, must indeed rest on *a priori* insight. It is beyond doubt and cannot be drawn from experience. I derive it from the fact that the principle of all arising and passing away, the causal law known to us *a priori*, concerns only *changes*, successive *states* of matter, and so is limited to form, leaving *matter* untouched. Matter thus stands in our consciousness as the basis of all things, neither arising nor passing away, always having been and always remaining. A deeper ground for substance's persistence, drawn from analyzing our intuitive representation of the empirical world, appears in §4 of my first book. There I show that *matter* consists in the complete *union of space and time*. This union is possible only through representation of causality and therefore only for the understanding, the subjective correlate of causality. Matter is consequently known only as acting, entirely as causality; its being and acting are one, as the word *actuality* itself suggests. The intimate union of space and time, causality, matter, and actuality are thus one, whose subjective correlate is understanding. Matter must carry the conflicting properties of the two factors from which it arises. The representation of causality resolves their contradiction and makes their coexistence intelligible to the understanding, through and for which alone matter exists and whose whole faculty is knowing cause and effect. For it, time's impermanent flow appears in matter as changing accidents and unites with space's rigid immobility, appearing as the persistence of substance. If substance vanished as accidents do, appearance would be torn away from space and belong to time alone; the world of experience would dissolve through matter's annihilation. The principle that substance persists, acknowledged by everyone as certain *a priori*, must be derived from the part *space* plays in matter and all appearances of actuality. Space opposes time and knows no change in itself apart from their union. It cannot be derived from time alone, to which Kant absurdly ascribed *persistence* for this purpose.

I demonstrated at length in *On the Principle of Sufficient Reason*, §23, the error of the next proof, which tries to derive the causal law's *a priori* necessity from mere temporal succession, and refer to it here.[^5] The same holds for his proof of reciprocal interaction, whose concept I have already shown to be empty. Enough has likewise been said about modality and the principles Kant next derives from it.

There are many more details in the Transcendental Analytic I could refute, but I fear tiring the reader and leave them to his own thought. Kant's chief fault, discussed above, keeps confronting us throughout the *Critique of Pure Reason*: his utter failure to distinguish abstract, discursive cognition from intuition. It spreads a constant obscurity over his whole theory of cognition. The reader never knows exactly what is being discussed and must guess rather than understand, trying each statement alternately as referring to thought or intuition and left perpetually uncertain. This astonishing lack of reflection on intuitive and abstract representation brings Kant, in the chapter “On Distinguishing All Objects into Phenomena and Noumena,” to the monstrous claim I shall discuss: without thinking and abstract concepts there is no cognition of an object at all; intuition is not thought and so is not cognition, but only an affection of sensibility, mere sensation. Worse, intuition without a concept is wholly empty, while a concept without intuition is still something (p. 253; fifth edition p. 309). That is the direct opposite of the truth. Concepts receive all meaning and content only through relation to intuitive representations. They are abstracted from intuition by dropping what is inessential, so without it they are empty and void. Intuitions, on the other hand, have immediate and immense significance in themselves, for in them the thing in itself objectivates itself. They represent and express themselves rather than borrowing their content, as concepts do. The principle of sufficient reason governs them only as the causal law, determining their position in space and time. It does not condition their content and significance as it does for concepts, where it operates as the ground of knowing.

Oddly, Kant seems here to want to distinguish intuitive and abstract representations properly. He faults Leibniz for treating everything as abstract representation and Locke for treating everything as intuitive. Yet he reaches no distinction. Even if Locke and Leibniz committed those errors, Kant committed a third containing both, so mixing intuition and abstraction that a monstrous hybrid resulted, something inconceivable that could only confuse, stupefy, and set his students quarreling.

Thought and intuition do separate more than anywhere else in the chapter “On Distinguishing All Objects into Phenomena and Noumena,” but in a fundamentally false way. At p. 253; fifth edition p. 309, Kant says: “If I remove all thinking (through categories) from an empirical cognition, no cognition of an object remains. For mere intuition thinks nothing, and the fact that this affection of sensibility is in me does not establish any relation between such representations and any object.” This one sentence contains all Kant's errors in miniature. It shows that he misconceived the relation among sensation, intuition, and thought. He identifies intuition, whose form is supposedly three-dimensional space, with mere subjective sensation in a sense organ, and allows cognition of an object to enter only through thinking distinct from intuition. I say instead that objects are first given in intuition, not thought, and all cognition of *objects* is originally and in itself intuitive. But intuition is by no means mere sensation; understanding already acts within it. *Thinking*, added only for humans and not animals, is merely abstraction from intuition. It supplies no fundamentally new cognition and first posits no objects absent before. It changes only the form of cognition already gained through intuition, making it abstract, conceptual, and no longer intuitive. This loss makes combination possible and immensely widens cognition's uses. The *material* of our thought is nothing other than our intuitions, not something thought adds that intuition lacked. The material of everything occurring in thought must therefore be traceable in intuition, or thought is empty. Thought may work and reshape it in many ways, but it must be possible to recover it and return thought to it, just as a piece of gold can be recovered whole and metallic from every dissolution, oxidation, sublimation, and compound. That would be impossible if thinking itself added something, still more the chief thing, to the object.

The entire following chapter on the Amphiboly is a critique of Leibniz's philosophy and is on the whole right. Yet its whole arrangement serves architectural symmetry. To create an analogy with Aristotle's *Organon*, Kant sets up a transcendental Topic: every concept must be considered in four respects before one can decide which faculty of cognition it belongs to. These four respects are arbitrary; ten others could be added with equal right. But four matches the category headings, under which Leibniz's chief teachings are distributed as best they can be. The critique also turns what were only *Leibniz's* false abstractions into supposedly natural errors of reason. Rather than learning from his great contemporaries Spinoza and Locke, Leibniz preferred to present his own strange inventions. At the end of the Amphiboly of Reflection, Kant says a mode of intuition wholly different from ours might exist yet admit application of our categories. The objects of this supposed intuition would be *noumena*, things we can only *think*. But since we lack the intuition that would give that thought meaning, and its very existence is doubtful, the object of the thought remains an entirely indefinite possibility.

I have cited passages showing Kant's profound self-contradiction in presenting the categories sometimes as conditions of intuitive representation, sometimes as functions of merely abstract thought. Here they appear only in the second role, as though he intended them to have only discursive thought. If that truly was his meaning, at the start of Transcendental Logic, before elaborating its various functions, he should have characterized thought generally and distinguished it from intuition, showing what cognition mere intuition gives and what thought adds. We would then have known what he meant—or rather, he would have spoken quite differently, first of intuition and then of thought. As it is he always treats some impossible intermediate thing. He would not have left the great gap between Transcendental Aesthetic and Logic. After presenting intuition's mere form, he dismisses its content, all empirical perception, with “it is *given*,” never asking whether and how it arises *with or without understanding*. He jumps to abstract thinking, and not even to thinking generally but straight to particular forms of it, saying nothing about what thinking or a concept is, the relation of abstract and discursive to concrete and intuitive cognition, the difference between human and animal cognition, or the nature of reason.

The very distinction Kant overlooked between abstract and intuitive cognition is what ancient philosophers marked by φαινόμενα and νοούμενα (phenomena and noumena).[^6] Its opposition and incommensurability occupied them in the Eleatics' teachings, Plato's Ideas, the Megarians' dialectic, and later the scholastics' dispute between nominalism and realism, whose slowly developing seed was already in the opposite tendencies of Plato and Aristotle. Kant, quite inexcusably neglecting the subject for which these terms had already been taken, seizes them as though unowned and uses them for his things in themselves and appearances.

Having rejected Kant's doctrine of categories as he rejected Aristotle's, I would suggest a third route to what they sought. Both were surely looking for the most general concepts under which even the most diverse things must be subsumed, through which everything existing is thought. Kant therefore took them as the *forms* of all thought.

Grammar stands to logic as clothing stands to the body. Might not these highest concepts, the bass line of reason underlying every more specific thought and without whose use no thinking can occur, ultimately lie in concepts so extremely general, or transcendental, that they are expressed not by particular words but by entire classes of words? Every word already carries one of them. Their sign would thus be found in grammar, not a dictionary. Might they be the distinctions by which a word is a noun, adjective, verb, adverb, pronoun, preposition, or other particle: the *partes orationis* (parts of speech)? These plainly mark the forms all thinking first takes and moves within. They are therefore essential forms and basic components of every language. We cannot imagine a language without at least nouns, adjectives, and verbs. Beneath these fundamental forms we would place forms of thought expressed by their inflections, declension and conjugation. Whether articles and pronouns help express them matters little. Let us examine this more closely and ask anew: What are the forms of thought?

**1.** Thought consists throughout of judgments; judgments are the threads of its entire fabric. Thought cannot move without a verb, and every use of a verb is a judgment.

**2.** Every judgment recognizes a relation between subject and predicate, separating or joining them with various restrictions. It joins them when they are truly identical, possible only with interchangeable concepts. In a universal affirmative it recognizes that one is always thought within the other, though not conversely; in a particular affirmative, that one is sometimes thought within the other. Negative propositions follow the reverse course. Every judgment must therefore have subject, predicate, and an affirmative or negative copula, though not always separately expressed by words. One word often contains predicate and copula, as in “Caius ages”; sometimes one word contains all three, as in *concurritur* (“the armies engage”). Thus the forms of thought cannot be sought directly in words or even parts of speech. The same judgment can be expressed by different words and even different parts of speech in different languages or in one language, while remaining the same thought and form. A thought could not be the same if its own form differed. The verbal construction may vary while thought and its form stay the same: words are merely its outer clothing, while thought is inseparable from its form. Grammar thus explains only how thought's forms are clothed. Parts of speech may be derived from original forms of thought independent of every language. Their purpose is to express those forms and all their variations. They are thought's tool and garment, which must fit its structure closely enough for it to be recognized beneath them.


**3.** These real, original, unchanging forms of thought are indeed those in Kant's *logical table of judgments*, except that it includes blind windows for symmetry and the category table, which must all be removed, and is wrongly ordered. An arrangement would be:

**(a) Quality:** affirmation or negation, joining or separating concepts. These are two forms and attach to the copula.

**(b) Quantity:** the subject concept is taken wholly or partly: universality or particularity. Individual subjects belong to universality; “Socrates” means “all Socrates.” Thus there are only two forms. Quantity attaches to the subject.

**(c) Modality:** this really has three forms. It determines quality as necessary, actual, or contingent and therefore also attaches to the copula.

These three forms of thought arise from the laws of thought of contradiction and identity. The principle of sufficient reason and the law of excluded middle, however, yield:

**(d) Relation:** this arises only when we judge judgments already made. It can indicate that one judgment depends on another, or on several, joining them in a *hypothetical* proposition. Or it can indicate that judgments exclude one another, separating them in a *disjunctive* proposition. It attaches to the copula, which joins or separates completed judgments here.

The *parts of speech* and grammatical forms express the three elements of judgment—subject, predicate, copula—and their possible relations, the forms of thought just listed, with their finer determinations and variations. Nouns, adjectives, and verbs are therefore essential building blocks of language generally and must occur in every language. One could, however, imagine a language where adjective and verb were always fused, as they sometimes are in all languages. Provisionally, nouns, articles, and pronouns serve to express the *subject*; adjectives, adverbs, and prepositions the *predicate*; and verbs the *copula*, though verbs other than “to be” also contain the predicate. Philosophical grammar should teach the exact mechanism for expressing the forms of thought, as logic teaches operations with the forms themselves.

*Note.* To warn against a wrong turn and clarify what I have said, I mention S. Stern's *Provisional Foundation for a Philosophy of Language* (1835), a wholly unsuccessful attempt to construct the categories from grammatical forms. Stern confuses thought completely with intuition and therefore tries to derive supposed categories of intuition, rather than thought, from grammar, relating grammatical forms directly to *intuition*. His great error is to suppose *language* relates immediately to *intuition*. It relates immediately only to *thought* as such, to *abstract concepts*, and through them first to intuition. But their relation to intuition brings about a complete change of form. What exists in intuition, including relations arising from time and space, can certainly become an object of thought, and linguistic forms must express it, but only *in abstracto*, as concepts. Concepts are always thought's immediate material; logical forms relate only to them, never *directly* to intuition. Intuition determines a proposition's material truth, never its formal truth, which depends solely on logical rules.

I return to Kant and come to the *Transcendental Dialectic*. He begins by defining *reason*, the faculty that is to play the leading role, while sensibility and understanding have occupied the stage until now. Among his various definitions of reason already discussed, I mentioned the one here: “the faculty of principles.” He now teaches that all the *a priori* cognitions considered so far, making pure mathematics and pure natural science possible, provide only *rules*, not *principles*. They arise from intuitions and forms of cognition rather than from mere *concepts*, which is required to count as a principle. A principle would thus have to be cognition *from mere concepts* and yet *synthetic*. This is absolutely impossible. Only *analytic* propositions can arise from mere concepts. If concepts are to be joined synthetically and yet *a priori*, a third thing must mediate the joining: pure intuition of the formal possibility of experience, as empirical intuition mediates synthetic *a posteriori* judgments. No synthetic *a priori* proposition can therefore arise from mere concepts. Generally we know *a priori* only the principle of sufficient reason in its different forms. No other synthetic *a priori* judgments are possible than those arising from what supplies content to that principle.

At last Kant offers a supposed principle of reason meeting his demand, but only *one* from which he then derives consequences. It is the proposition set out by Christian Wolff in *Cosmologia*, section 1, chapter 2, §93, and *Ontologia*, §178. Earlier, in the Amphiboly, Kant had treated mere Leibnizian doctrines as natural, necessary errors of reason and critiqued them accordingly. He now does the same with Wolff's doctrines. He presents the principle still veiled by obscurity, vagueness, and fragmentation (pp. 307 and 322; fifth edition pp. 361 and 379). Stated plainly, it is: “If the conditioned is given, the totality of its conditions, and thus the *unconditioned* that alone completes that totality, must also be given.” Its seeming truth is most vivid if we imagine conditions and conditioned things as links in a hanging chain whose upper end is unseen and might extend without end. Because the chain hangs rather than falls, *one* link at the top must be first and somehow attached. More briefly, reason would like an attachment point for the chain of causes receding into infinity. That would be convenient. But we must test the proposition itself, not an image.

It is indeed synthetic; only the concept of a condition follows analytically from the conditioned. But it has no truth either *a priori* or *a posteriori*. It steals an appearance of truth in a subtle way I shall expose. We know the principle of sufficient reason immediately and *a priori* in its four forms. All abstract expressions of it are derived from and thus less immediate than those cognitions, and its consequences still less immediate. As I explained above, *abstract* cognition often unites very different *intuitive* cognitions in *one* form or concept, so that they can no longer be distinguished. It stands to intuitive cognition as a shadow stands to real objects, reducing their great variety to *one* common outline. The supposed principle of reason exploits this shadow. To draw from the principle of sufficient reason an unconditioned that directly contradicts it, the proposition wisely leaves behind immediate intuitive knowledge of the principle's content in its distinct forms. It uses only the abstract concepts drawn from them, whose worth and significance come solely from those forms, and smuggles an unconditioned into their broad scope.

A dialectical presentation makes the procedure plain: “If a conditioned thing exists, its condition must be given, and given wholly, so completely: the totality of its conditions. If those form a series, the whole series must be given, including its first beginning, the unconditioned.” Already it is false that a conditioned thing's conditions as such can form a *series*. The totality of conditions for any conditioned thing must be contained in its *nearest* ground, from which it follows immediately and which is *sufficient* only for that reason. For instance, all the determinations of the causal state must come together before the effect occurs. A series, such as a chain of causes, arises only when we take what was just a condition and consider it anew as a conditioned thing. The whole operation then begins again, and the principle of sufficient reason raises its demand afresh. There can never be a proper successive *series* of conditions existing merely as conditions for one final conditioned thing. Rather, it is always an alternating series of conditioned things and conditions. At every link already passed, the chain has been broken and the principle's demand completely met. It starts again only when the condition becomes something conditioned. The principle of *sufficient* reason thus requires only the completeness of the *nearest condition*, never the completeness of a *series*.

The concept “complete condition” leaves open whether its conditions are simultaneous or successive. Choosing succession creates a demand for a complete series of successive conditions. Only by arbitrary abstraction can a series of causes and effects be treated as a series of mere causes existing for the final effect and required as its *sufficient* ground. More careful consideration, descending from abstraction's vague generality to specific reality, finds instead that the demand for a *sufficient* ground concerns the completeness of the *nearest* cause, not a series. The principle's demand expires completely in each given sufficient ground. It arises again as soon as that ground is considered as an effect, but it never immediately demands a series of grounds. Remaining among abstract concepts erases these distinctions. Then a chain alternating causes and effects, or logical grounds and consequences, is passed off as a chain consisting solely of causes or grounds for the final effect. The *completeness of conditions* that makes one ground *sufficient* is presented as completeness of that supposed *series* of grounds existing only for a final consequence. The abstract principle of reason can then boldly demand the unconditioned.

We need no critique of reason through antinomies and their solutions to expose this demand, only a critique of reason in my sense: examine how abstract relates to immediate intuitive cognition by descending from abstraction's vague generality to intuition's firm specificity. We then find that reason's nature is by no means to demand an unconditioned. Reflecting fully, reason itself must recognize that an unconditioned is an absurdity. As a faculty of cognition, it can deal only with objects. Every object for a subject is necessarily and irrevocably subject to the principle of sufficient reason, both *a parte ante* (with respect to what precedes) and *a parte post* (with respect to what follows). The principle is so inherent in consciousness that we cannot represent any objective thing of which no further “why?” can be asked, no absolute absolute confronting us like a board. Some people may find it convenient to stop somewhere and posit such an absolute at will, but their grand manner cannot overcome this unshakable *a priori* certainty. The whole talk of “the absolute,” almost the sole subject of philosophies attempted since Kant, is the cosmological proof *incognito*. After Kant tried and outlawed it, it cannot show itself in its true form. It wears disguises: at times noble, cloaked in intellectual intuition or pure thought; at others a suspicious vagrant, half begging and half extorting what it gets in more modest philosophies. If these gentlemen insist on an absolute, I will give them one that fits all their demands far better than their imagined phantoms: matter. It has neither origin nor end, and is thus truly independent, *quod per se est et per se concipitur* (“what is in and conceived through itself”). Everything emerges from its womb and returns there. What more can one ask of an absolute? Rather, to those whom no critique of reason has reached, we should cry:


“Are you not like women who forever<br>
Return to their first word again,<br>
Though reason has been spoken for hours?”

That going back to an unconditioned cause, a first beginning, is not inherent in reason is also established by fact. The oldest religions of our race, still followed by the greatest number of people on earth, Brahmanism and Buddhism, know and admit no such assumptions. They carry the chain of mutually conditioned appearances back without end. I refer to my note below on the first antinomy and to Upham's *Doctrine of Buddhism*, p. 9, or any authentic account of Asian religions. Judaism should not be identified with reason.

Kant insists his supposed principle of reason has only subjective necessity, not objective validity. Yet he derives even that through a shallow sophism (p. 307; fifth edition p. 364). We try, as long as possible, to subsume every known truth under a more general one; he takes this as already a pursuit of the unconditioned, which we supposedly presuppose. In truth this search is simply a proper use of reason, the faculty of abstract, general cognition distinguishing reflective, speaking, thinking humans from animals enslaved by the present. We simplify and improve our knowledge by surveying it. Reason's use consists in knowing the particular through the general, the case through a rule, and that rule through a still more general one, seeking the broadest standpoints. Such a survey makes knowledge much easier and better and accounts for the great difference between animal and human lives and, again, between the lives of educated and uneducated people.

The chain of *grounds of knowing*, found only in abstraction and reason, does indeed end at something unprovable: a representation no longer conditioned under this form of the principle of sufficient reason, the directly intuitive *a priori* or *a posteriori* ground of the highest proposition in an inferential chain. In *On the Principle of Sufficient Reason*, §50, I showed that here the chain of grounds of knowing actually passes over into grounds of becoming or being. One can use this to prove an unconditioned under the causal law, even merely as a demand, only by failing to distinguish the forms of that principle and confusing them all under the abstract name. Kant even tries to justify the confusion with a pun on *universalitas* and *universitas* (p. 322; fifth edition p. 379). It is thus fundamentally false that our search for higher grounds of knowing, for general truths, arises from assuming an object unconditioned in its existence or has anything in common with that assumption. How could reason essentially presuppose something it must recognize as absurd when it reflects? The concept of an unconditioned has no other source than the laziness of someone wanting to end others' and his own further questions, without justification.

Kant denies objective validity to this alleged principle of reason, yet makes it a subjectively necessary presupposition. This creates an irresolvable division within our cognition that soon becomes plainer. He develops the principle by his favored architecturally symmetrical method (p. 322; fifth edition p. 379). The three categories of relation yield three kinds of inferences, each guiding a search for a particular unconditioned. These are therefore three: soul, world regarded as an object in itself and a closed totality, and God. Kant ignores an immediate, major contradiction because it threatens the symmetry. Two of the supposedly unconditioned things, soul and world, are themselves conditioned by the third, God, as their producing cause. They therefore share with God not the relevant property of being unconditioned, but only that of being inferred from principles of experience beyond its possible scope.

Setting that aside, the three supposedly necessary products of every reason turn out to be the three main subjects around which philosophy under Christianity's influence revolved from the scholastics to Christian Wolff. Those concepts are familiar and accessible to our reason after centuries of such philosophy, but that does not show that without revelation they would arise in every reason as products of its own nature. Establishing this would require historical inquiry. Did ancient and non-European peoples, especially those of India, and many early Greek philosophers actually reach these concepts? Or do we kindly but falsely attribute them to those peoples, as the Greeks found their own gods everywhere? Is it wrong to translate Hindu *Brahman* and Chinese *Tian* as “God”? Perhaps theism proper occurs only in Judaism and the two religions descended from it. Their adherents therefore group all other religions' followers under “pagans,” incidentally an extremely foolish and crude term that should at least be barred from scholarly writing. It throws Brahmins, Buddhists, Egyptians, Greeks, Romans, Germans, Gauls, Iroquois, Patagonians, Caribs, Tahitians, Australians, and others into one sack. Priests may find the term fitting; scholarship should show it the door at once and send it to settle in Oxford. Buddhism in particular, the religion with the most followers on earth, certainly contains no theism and indeed abhors it.

As for Plato, I believe he owed the theism that periodically came over him to the Jews. *Numenius* therefore called him *Moses graecisans* (“Moses speaking Greek”; see Clement of Alexandria, *Stromata*, I, chapter 22; Eusebius, *Praeparatio Evangelica*, XIII, 12; and Suidas under Numenius): Τί γάρ ἐστι Πλάτων, ἢ Μωσῆς ἀττικίζων; (“What is Plato but Moses speaking Attic Greek?”). He accused Plato of stealing (ἀποσυλήσας) his doctrines of God and creation from the Mosaic writings. Clement repeatedly returns to Plato's having known and used Moses, for instance in *Stromata*, I, 25; V, chapter 14, §90; *Paedagogus*, II, 10 and III, 11. In *Cohortatio ad gentes*, chapter 6, after scolding and mocking all Greek philosophers like a Capuchin friar in the previous chapter for not being Jews, he praises Plato alone and bursts into rejoicing: just as Plato learned geometry from the Egyptians, astronomy from the Babylonians, magic from the Thracians, and much from the Assyrians, so he learned theism from the Jews. Οἶδά σου τοὺς διδασκάλους, κἂν ἀποκρύπτειν ἐθέλῃς ... δόξαν τὴν τοῦ θεοῦ παρ’ αὐτῶν ὠφελήσει τῶν Ἑβραίων (*tuos magistros novi, licet eos celare velis, – – ‒ ‒ ‒ illa de Deo sententia suppeditata tibi est ab Hebraeis*; “I know your teachers, even if you wish to hide them ... that view about God was supplied to you by the Hebrews”). A touching scene of recognition! I find a curious confirmation in the following. According to Plutarch (*Marius*), and more clearly Lactantius (I, 3, 19), Plato thanked nature that he had become a human and not an animal, a man and not a woman, a Greek and not a barbarian. Isaac Euchel's *Prayers of the Jews*, translated from Hebrew, second edition, 1799, p. 7, includes a morning prayer thanking and praising God that the speaker was born a Jew, not a pagan, free, not a slave, and a man, not a woman.

Such historical inquiry would have spared Kant his awkward position. He makes the three concepts spring necessarily from reason's nature, yet shows they cannot be sustained or grounded by reason. He thereby makes reason itself a sophist: “These are sophistications not of man, but of pure reason itself, from which even the wisest cannot free himself. Perhaps with much effort he can avoid the error, but never escape the illusion that continually vexes and mocks him” (p. 339; fifth edition p. 397). Kant's “Ideas of reason” would then resemble the focal point where rays reflected by a concave mirror meet a few inches before it. By an unavoidable process of understanding, an object seems to appear there though it has no reality.

The name *Ideas* is especially unfortunate for these three supposedly necessary products of pure theoretical reason. Kant took it from Plato, who used it for imperishable forms imperfectly visible in countless individual, transient things through multiplication in time and space. Plato's Ideas are thus entirely intuitive, as the word he chose plainly indicates; it might be translated “visible forms” or “things seen.” Kant appropriated it for what lies so far beyond all possible intuition that even abstract thought can only half reach it. For twenty-two centuries after Plato introduced “Idea,” it kept his meaning. Ancient philosophers, scholastics, Church Fathers, and medieval theologians all used it in that Platonic sense, as the Latin *exemplar* (model), as Suárez expressly notes in his twenty-fifth disputation, section 1. Later English and French writers abused the word because their languages were poor, but that carries no weight. Kant's misuse introduces a new meaning on the frail connection that his Ideas, like Plato's and every possible chimera, are not objects of experience. It is indefensible. The abuse of a few years cannot outweigh centuries of usage. I have therefore always used “Idea” in its original Platonic sense.


The refutation of *rational psychology* is much fuller and more thorough in the first edition of the *Critique of Pure Reason* than in the second and later ones, so the first must be used here. On the whole this refutation has great merit and contains much truth. Yet I am convinced Kant derives the concept of soul as a necessary result of applying the demand for the unconditioned to *substance*, the first category of relation, through that paralogism solely for the sake of symmetry. He thus claims the idea of a soul arises in every speculative reason in this way. If it truly came from assuming a final subject of all a thing's predicates, we would necessarily assume a soul in every lifeless thing as in a human being, since each demands a final subject of its predicates. In general Kant uses an inadmissible expression when he speaks of something able to exist only as subject and never as predicate (*Critique of Pure Reason*, p. 323; fifth edition p. 412; *Prolegomena*, §§4 and 47), though Aristotle's *Metaphysics*, IV, chapter 8, already offers a precedent. Nothing exists *as subject and predicate*: these belong exclusively to logic and name relations among abstract concepts. Their supposed intuitive-world counterparts are substance and accident. But we need not seek far for something that always exists as substance, never as accident: matter is immediately before us. It is the substance of every thing's properties, which are its accidents. If we retain Kant's criticized expression, matter is the last subject of all predicates of every empirically given thing: what remains after subtracting all properties of every kind. This applies to humans, animals, plants, and stones. So obvious is it that overlooking it requires a determined wish not to see. I shall soon show that matter really is the prototype of substance.

Subject and predicate stand to substance and accident as the logical principle of sufficient reason stands to causality in nature. Confusing or identifying the one pair is as wrong as confusing the other. Kant pushes the confusion to its utmost in *Prolegomena*, §46, to derive the concept of soul from the final subject of all predicates and the form of the categorical inference. To expose the sophistry, one need only remember that subject and predicate are purely logical determinations concerning only abstract concepts in their relation within a judgment. Substance and accident belong instead to the intuitive world and its apprehension by the understanding, where they occur only as identical with matter and form or quality. More on this presently.

The real opposition that prompted belief in two fundamentally different substances, body and soul, is that of objective and subjective. When someone apprehends himself objectively through external intuition, he finds a being extended in space and wholly corporeal. Apprehending himself in mere self-consciousness, purely subjectively, he finds only a willing and representing being, free of all intuitive forms and therefore of bodily properties. He then forms the concept of soul, like all the transcendent concepts Kant calls Ideas, by applying the principle of sufficient reason, the form of every object, to what is not an object: here, the subject of knowing and willing. He treats knowing, thinking, and willing as effects and seeks their cause. Unable to take the body as that cause, he posits one wholly distinct from it. This is how the first and the last dogmatists alike prove a soul's existence: Plato already in the *Phaedrus*, and Wolff much later. Thinking and willing are taken as effects pointing to it as cause. Only after thus making a cause corresponding to the effect into a thing does the school develop and prove an immaterial, simple, indestructible being from the concept of *substance*. But it had first fashioned that concept specifically for this purpose by a noteworthy trick.

The first class of representations, the intuitively real world, includes representation of matter. The causal law ruling it determines changes of states, which presuppose something persisting whose changes they are. Discussing substance's persistence above, I referred to earlier passages showing how matter arises as a representation. In the understanding, through and for which alone matter exists, time and space are intimately joined by causality, its sole form of cognition. Space's contribution appears as the persistence of *matter*, time's as the change of its *states*. Pure matter can only be thought *in abstracto*, not intuited, since in intuition it always already has form and quality. *Substance* is another abstraction from *matter*, a higher *genus*, formed by keeping only persistence while thinking away matter's other essential properties: extension, impenetrability, divisibility, and so forth. Like any higher genus, substance *contains less in itself* than matter. But unlike a proper higher genus it does not *contain more under it*. It comprises no lower genera alongside matter, which remains its one genuine species, the sole example giving it real content. The usual purpose of forming a higher genus by abstraction, to think several species differing in secondary features under one concept, is absent. The abstraction was thus either idle and pointless or served a hidden aim. The aim appears when a second species, an immaterial, simple, indestructible substance called soul, is placed beside genuine matter under the concept “substance.”

The concept was smuggled in by first forming the higher genus *substance* unlawfully and illogically. Reason properly forms a higher genus only by placing several species concepts side by side and comparing them. It drops their differences and keeps their common features, arriving at a broader, less detailed genus. Species must therefore precede genus. Here the reverse occurred. Only *matter* existed before the genus *substance*. The latter was made from it idly, without occasion or justification, by arbitrarily dropping all determinations but one. Only afterward was a second, spurious species placed alongside matter. Forming it required simply expressly negating what had been silently removed in forming the genus: extension, impenetrability, and divisibility. “Substance” was thus formed solely as a vehicle to smuggle in “immaterial substance.” Far from being a category or necessary function of understanding, it is highly dispensable. Its only true content is already in “matter”; besides this it holds a great void that can be filled only by the smuggled-in species “immaterial substance,” the very reason it was formed. Strictly it should be rejected entirely and “matter” substituted everywhere.

The categories were a Procrustean bed for every possible thing; the three forms of inference serve the same role for the three so-called Ideas. The soul was forced to find its origin in categorical inference. Now dogmatic ideas of the world as a whole, taken as an object in itself enclosed between the smallest, the atom, and the largest, limits of the world in time and space, must arise from hypothetical inference. This requires less force in itself. Hypothetical judgment has its form from the principle of sufficient reason; all these so-called Ideas, not only the cosmological ones, do indeed arise from the unreflective, unqualified application of that principle followed by arbitrary abandonment of it. Following it, one seeks one object's dependence on another without end until a tired imagination creates an end to the journey. One overlooks that every object, the whole series, and the principle of sufficient reason itself are far more directly and profoundly dependent on the knowing subject. That principle applies only to the subject's objects, its representations, determining merely their positions in space and time. Since the form of cognition from which Kant derives only the cosmological Ideas, the principle of sufficient reason, actually gives rise to all rationalizing hypostases, no sophism is needed on this occasion. Considerably more is needed, however, to classify those Ideas under the four category headings.


**1.** The cosmological Ideas concerning time and space, the world's limits in both, are boldly assigned to *quantity*. Their only link is the chance use in logic of “quantity,” a metaphorical name that could easily have been replaced, for the extension of the subject concept in a judgment. Kant's love of symmetry is enough to exploit this fortunate name and attach transcendent doctrines of the world's extent to it.

**2.** Still more boldly he attaches transcendent Ideas about matter to *quality*, that is, affirmation or negation in judgment, without even an accidental likeness of names. Matter's mechanical, rather than chemical, divisibility concerns precisely its *quantity*, not its quality. Moreover, the whole Idea of divisibility does not belong among consequences of the principle of sufficient reason, though all cosmological Ideas are supposed to spring from it as the content of hypothetical judgment. Kant assumes that the relation of parts to whole is that of condition to conditioned, a relation under the principle of sufficient reason. This is subtle but groundless sophistry. The relation rests rather on the law of contradiction. Neither whole nor parts arise from one another as ground and consequence. Both necessarily belong together because they are one; their separation is only an arbitrary act. If parts are thought away, the whole is also thought away, and conversely, under the law of contradiction. It is not that parts as *ground* condition the whole as *consequence*, driving us to seek ultimate parts under the principle of sufficient reason in order to understand the whole from them. Such difficulties are no match for the love of symmetry.

**3.** The Idea of a first cause of the world would properly belong under *relation*. Kant must save it for the fourth heading, modality, otherwise left empty. He forces it there by having the first cause turn the contingent—on his definition, directly opposite to truth, every consequence of its ground—into the necessary. The concept of *freedom* thus enters as the third Idea for symmetry's sake, but it really is simply the Idea of the world's cause, the one Idea that fits here at all, as the note on the thesis of the third antinomy plainly says. The third and fourth antinomies are essentially tautological.

I maintain that the entire Antinomy is sham combat. Only the *antitheses* are actually grounded in the forms of our cognition or, stated objectively, in the necessary and most general laws of nature known with *a priori* certainty. Their proofs alone proceed from objective grounds. The *theses* and their proofs have only a subjective basis. They rest wholly on the weakness of the reasoning individual, whose imagination tires of an infinite regress and brings it to an end by arbitrary assumptions made as plausible as possible. His judgment is further paralyzed by prejudices firmly instilled early in life. The proof of each thesis is therefore a sophism, whereas each antithesis follows inevitably from reason applying the *a priori* laws of the world as representation. Only with much effort and art could Kant keep the theses on their feet and make them appear to attack a foe endowed with original strength. His constant first device is not to show the *nervus argumentationis* (core of the argument) by isolating it as plainly as possible, as someone confident of the truth would do. Instead he buries it on both sides in a flood of lengthy, superfluous sentences.

These theses and antitheses recall the δίκαιος and ἄδικος λόγος (“just” and “unjust” arguments) that Socrates makes debate in Aristophanes' *Clouds*. But the likeness is only formal, not one of content, however much those who attach moral significance to this most speculative issue of theoretical philosophy would like to take the thesis as the just and the antithesis as the unjust argument. I will not accommodate such narrow, perverse minds. Giving the honor to truth rather than to them, I shall expose Kant's proofs of the theses as sophisms and show that those of the antitheses are honest, correct, and grounded objectively. I presume the reader has Kant's Antinomy before him throughout this examination.

If the proof of the thesis in the first antinomy were accepted, it would prove too much. It applies to time itself as well as to change within time, and would thus prove that time began, which is absurd. The sophism is that a series of states *without a beginning*, the original topic, suddenly becomes a series *without an end*, and Kant proves only the undisputed claim that an endless series cannot be completed, while every present is the end of the past. We can *think* an end to a beginningless series without compromising its lack of a beginning, just as we can think a beginning to an endless series. Kant offers nothing against the antithesis's sound argument that changes in the world necessarily presuppose an infinite sequence of prior changes. We can conceive that the causal chain might someday stop absolutely, but not that it could begin absolutely.[^7]

For the world's spatial limits, the thesis proves that if the world is called a *given whole* it must have limits. The inference is correct, but its premise is exactly what required proof and is left unproved. Totality presupposes limits and limits presuppose totality, and both are assumed arbitrarily here. The antithesis's proof on this point is less satisfactory than on the first, because causality supplies necessary determinations with respect to time, not space. It gives us *a priori* certainty that filled time cannot border on earlier empty time and no change can be first, but not that filled space cannot have empty space beside it. On that issue no *a priori* decision is possible in the same way. Still, conceiving a spatially bounded world is difficult because space itself is necessarily infinite. A finite world within it, however large, becomes infinitely small by comparison, a disproportion imagination cannot overcome. It must think the world as either infinitely large or infinitely small. Ancient philosophers recognized this: Μητρόδωρος ὁ καθηγητὴς Ἐπικούρου, φησὶν ἄτοπον εἶναι ἐν μεγάλῳ πεδίῳ ἕνα στάχυν γεννηθῆναι, καὶ ἕνα κόσμον ἐν τῷ ἀπείρῳ (*Metrodorus, caput scholae Epicuri, absurdum ait, in magno campo spicam unam produci, et unum in infinitum mundum*; “Metrodorus, head of Epicurus's school, says it is absurd for a single ear of grain to grow in a vast field or for there to be only one world in the infinite”) (Stobaeus, *Eclogae*, I, chapter 23). Many therefore taught, as the next passage reports, ἀπείρους κόσμους ἐν τῷ ἀπείρῳ (*infinitos mundos in infinito*; “infinite worlds in the infinite”). This is also the sense of Kant's argument for the antithesis, distorted by scholastic and contorted presentation. The same argument could be used against the world's temporal limits if causality did not provide a much better one. A spatially limited world also prompts the unanswerable question why the filled portion should be privileged over the infinite space left empty. Giordano Bruno gives a detailed and very readable discussion on both sides of the world's finitude in the fifth dialogue of *Del infinito, universo e mondi* (*On the Infinite, the Universe and Worlds*). Kant himself seriously affirms the world's spatial infinity on objective grounds in *Universal Natural History and Theory of the Heavens*, part 2, chapter 7. Aristotle agrees in *Physics*, III, chapter 4; this and the following chapters repay reading in connection with the antinomy.

The thesis in the second antinomy immediately begs the question, and not subtly: “*Every composite* substance consists of simple parts.” Once composition has been assumed arbitrarily, proving simple parts is easy. But the claim that “all matter is composed” is precisely what needs proof and receives none. What opposes simple is not composite, but extended, having parts, divisible. Here it is silently assumed that parts existed before the whole and were brought together to make it; “composite” says just that. But we cannot assert it any more than its opposite. Divisibility means only that a whole can be divided into parts, not that it arose by assembling them. It asserts parts *a parte post* (afterward), whereas composition asserts them *a parte ante* (beforehand). Parts and whole are not essentially related in time; they condition each other and are simultaneous, since what is extended spatially exists only with both. Kant's note on the thesis, that space should be called a *totum* (whole) rather than a *compositum* (composite), holds just as much for matter, which is only space made perceptible. Matter's infinite divisibility, maintained by the antithesis, follows *a priori* and undeniably from the infinite divisibility of the space it fills. Nothing stands against this proposition. Kant himself presents it as objective truth at p. 513; fifth edition p. 541, where he speaks seriously in his own name rather than as spokesman for the ἄδικος λόγος (unjust argument). In *Metaphysical Foundations of Natural Science*, p. 108 of the first edition, “matter is infinitely divisible” also heads the proof of the first proposition of mechanics as established truth, having appeared and been proved earlier as the fourth proposition of dynamics. Here, however, Kant spoils the proof of the antithesis with exceedingly confused exposition and a flood of useless words, cunningly preventing its evident truth from too thoroughly eclipsing the thesis's sophisms. Atoms are no necessary thought of reason, merely a hypothesis to explain differences in specific weight. Kant himself showed in the Dynamics of his *Metaphysical Foundations* how these could be explained differently, indeed better and more simply than by atomism. Priestley had done so before him in *On Matter and Spirit*, section 1; Aristotle already has the basic idea in *Physics*, IV, 9.

The argument for the third thesis is a very subtle sophism, Kant's supposed principle of pure reason itself, undiluted and unchanged. It tries to prove a finite series of causes from the claim that a cause, to be *sufficient*, must include the full sum of conditions producing the subsequent state, the effect. Into this completeness of determinations existing *together* in the causal state, it slips the completeness of the causal series through which that state itself became actual. Since completeness supposedly requires closure and therefore finitude, it infers a first, unconditioned cause closing the series. The trick is obvious. To understand state A as a sufficient cause of B, I assume it contains all the required determinations whose conjunction inevitably produces B. This fully satisfies my demand that A be a *sufficient* cause. How A itself became actual is a different question, with no immediate connection to the first. In the new inquiry I no longer treat A as a cause, but as an effect needing another state related to it as it was to B. Nowhere does this require a finite causal series or first beginning, just as the present moment does not presuppose a beginning of time. An idle speculator adds that assumption. To say it lies in calling a cause a *sufficient ground* is a false smuggling-in, as I showed in examining Kant's principle of reason, identical with this thesis. To illustrate the false thesis, Kant even offers his rising from a chair as an unconditioned beginning, as if he could rise without a motive any more than a ball could roll without a cause. I need hardly use Lucan, the Eleatics, and others, much less the Hindus, to show the weakness of his appeal to ancient philosophers, prompted by a sense of weakness. As in the earlier antinomies, nothing can be objected to the antithesis's proof.

As noted, the fourth antinomy is essentially a repetition of the third. Its thesis has substantially the same proof. The claim that every conditioned thing presupposes a complete *series* of conditions ending in an unconditioned is a *petitio principii* (begging of the question) to be denied outright. A conditioned thing presupposes only its condition. Asking whether that condition is conditioned opens a new inquiry not immediately contained in the first.


The Antinomy has a certain plausibility. Remarkably, however, no part of Kant's philosophy has met so little opposition or won so much approval as this highly paradoxical doctrine. Nearly every philosophical party and textbook has accepted, repeated, and even developed it, while almost all his other teachings have been challenged. There have even been odd thinkers who rejected the Transcendental Aesthetic. The Antinomy's unanimous welcome may be due to the inward pleasure some take in contemplating a point where understanding is supposed to stop because it encounters something that both is and is not. They seem to see before them the sixth trick performed by the conjurer Philadelphus on Lichtenberg's bill.

Kant's subsequent *Critical Resolution* of the cosmological dispute, when we examine its true meaning, is not what he claims: that both sides start from false assumptions, so that in the first two disputes both are wrong and in the last two both are right. It actually confirms the antitheses by clarifying their claims.

First, Kant wrongly maintains that both parties start from the premise that the conditioned brings with it the complete, closed *series* of its conditions. Only the *thesis* bases itself on this proposition, Kant's pure principle of reason. The antithesis expressly denies it at every point and maintains the opposite. Kant further accuses both parties of assuming the world exists in itself, independently of being known and of the forms of cognition. Once again, only the thesis assumes this. The antithesis depends so little on it that the two are incompatible. It directly contradicts the concept of an infinite series to be wholly given. Such a series essentially exists only in relation to traversing it, not independently of that process. Assuming definite limits, on the other hand, assumes a whole existing in itself and independently of measuring it. Only the thesis thus wrongly assumes a world-whole existing in itself before all cognition, to which cognition is merely added. From the beginning the antithesis contests this assumption: the endlessness it attributes to series under the principle of sufficient reason exists only insofar as the regress is performed, not independently. As an object generally presupposes a subject, an object determined as an *endless* chain of conditions necessarily presupposes in the subject its corresponding mode of cognition: *continually following* the chain's links. This is precisely Kant's repeatedly stated solution: “The infinity of the world's magnitude exists only *through* the regress, not *before* it.” His solution actually decides for the antithesis, which already contains that truth, and is wholly incompatible with the thesis.

Had the antithesis asserted that the world consists of infinite series of grounds and consequences yet exists independently of representation and its regressive series, in itself as a given whole, it would contradict both the thesis and itself. An infinite thing can never be *wholly* given; an *endless* series exists only as it is traversed without end; something without limits cannot make a whole. Only the thesis makes the assumption Kant says misled both parties.

Aristotle already taught that an infinite thing cannot exist *actu* (actually, as a completed given), only *potentia* (potentially): Οὐκ ἔστιν ἐνεργείᾳ εἶναι τὸ ἄπειρον – – – – – ἀλλ’ ἀδύνατον τὸ ἐντελεχείᾳ ὂν ἄπειρον (*infinitum non potest esse actu ... sed impossibile, actu esse infinitum*; “The infinite cannot exist as an actual thing ... it is impossible for an infinite thing to be actual”) (*Metaphysics*, K, 10). Again: κατ’ ἐνέργειαν μὲν γὰρ οὐδέν ἐστιν ἄπειρον, δυνάμει δὲ ἐπὶ τὴν διαίρεσιν (*nihil enim actu infinitum est, sed potentia tantum, nempe divisione ipsa*; “Nothing infinite exists actually, but only potentially, in division itself”) (*On Generation and Corruption*, I, 3). He develops this at length in *Physics*, III, 5–6, virtually giving the right solution to all the antinomies. He states them in his concise way, says “an arbitrator (διαιτητοῦ) is needed,” and resolves them: the infinite in the world's space, time, and divisibility exists never *before* regress or progress, only *in* it. The truth is therefore already contained in the rightly understood concept of the infinite. We misunderstand ourselves if we think any sort of infinite is something objectively present and finished, independent of the regress.

Indeed, beginning with Kant's own supposed resolution produces the antithesis directly. If the world is no unconditioned whole, existing not in itself but only in representation, and if its series of grounds and consequences exist not *before* but only *through* our regress through them, then those series cannot be definite and finite. Definite limits would have to exist independently of the representation subsequently added. All series must therefore be endless, incapable of exhaustion by any representation.

At p. 506; fifth edition p. 534, Kant tries to infer the transcendental ideality of appearance from both parties' being wrong. He begins, “If the world is a whole existing in itself, it is either finite or infinite.” This is false: a whole existing in itself cannot possibly be infinite. One could instead infer ideality from the infinity of worldly series as follows. If series of grounds and consequences in the world are wholly endless, the world cannot be a given whole independent of representation. Such a whole always presupposes definite limits, while infinite series presuppose an endless regress. The supposed infinity of series must therefore be determined by the form of ground and consequence, which is determined by the subject's mode of cognition. The world as known thus exists only in the subject's representation.

I cannot say whether Kant knew that his Critical Resolution actually favored the antithesis. It depends on whether what Schelling aptly called Kant's system of accommodation went so far, or whether his mind had unconsciously accommodated itself to the influence of his age and surroundings.

The resolution of the third antinomy, whose subject is freedom, deserves special attention. Remarkably for us, it is here, with the Idea of *freedom*, that Kant must speak more fully of the *thing in itself*, until now in the background. This is readily intelligible after we have recognized the thing in itself as *will*. Here Kant's philosophy points toward mine, or mine grows from it as from a trunk. Attentive reading of the *Critique of Pure Reason*, pp. 536–537; fifth edition pp. 564–565, will show this. Compare the introduction to the *Critique of Judgment*, pp. XVIII–XIX of the third edition or p. 13 of Rosenkranz's, which even says: “The concept of freedom can represent in its object [surely the will] a thing in itself, though not in intuition; by contrast, the concept of nature can represent its object in intuition, though not as a thing in itself.” Above all, read §53 of the *Prolegomena* on the solution of the antinomies and ask sincerely whether everything there does not sound like a riddle to which my doctrine provides the answer. Kant did not think his matter through to the end; I have carried it out. I accordingly extended to all appearances, which differ from the human one only in degree, Kant's statement about human appearance: its essence in itself is absolutely free, that is, a will. The fertility of this insight joined to his teaching on the ideality of space, time, and causality is clear from my work.

Kant nowhere devotes a distinct analysis or clear derivation to the thing in itself. Whenever he needs it, he immediately draws it in by inferring that appearance, the visible world, must have a ground or intelligible cause that is not appearance and thus belongs to no possible experience. He does this after insisting repeatedly that the categories, including causality, apply only within possible experience. They are merely forms of understanding used to spell out sensory appearances and have no meaning beyond them. He strictly forbids applying them to what lies beyond experience and rightly explains and overthrows all earlier dogmatism as a breach of that rule. Kant's incredible inconsistency was quickly noticed by his earliest opponents, who used it in attacks his philosophy could not resist. We do indeed apply the causal law wholly *a priori*, before any experience, to changes sensed in our organs. But for that reason it is subjective in origin, just like the sensations themselves, and cannot lead to the thing in itself.

The truth is that representation can never take us beyond representation. It is a closed whole, with no thread in its own means leading to the essence of the thing in itself, which differs from it *toto genere* (in kind altogether). Were we beings that only represented, the path to the thing in itself would be entirely closed. Only the other side of our own being can disclose the other side of things' essence in itself. That is the path I took. Kant's inference to the thing in itself, though forbidden by his own rule, gains some appearance of justification in this way. He does not simply and absolutely make object depend on subject, and subject on object, as truth requires. He makes only the *manner of the object's appearance* depend on the subject's cognitive forms, which can therefore be conscious *a priori*. What is known only *a posteriori*, in contrast, he already takes as an immediate effect of the thing in itself, becoming appearance only on passing through those *a priori* forms. This may explain why he missed that being an object at all belongs to appearance's form and is conditioned by being a subject at all, just as the object's manner of appearing is conditioned by the subject's cognitive forms. If a thing in itself is assumed, it cannot be an object either, as he always presupposes. It must lie in a realm wholly different *toto genere* from representation, knowing and being known, and can least of all be inferred under laws linking objects to one another.

Kant's proof of the thing in itself met the same fate as his proof that the causal law is *a priori*. Both doctrines are true, but their proofs are false: correct conclusions from false premises. I have retained both while grounding them quite differently and securely.


I have neither smuggled in nor inferred the thing in itself by laws that exclude it, since those laws already belong to its appearance; nor have I reached it by any roundabout route. Rather, I have pointed it out immediately where it immediately lies: in the will, which reveals itself to everyone directly as the in-itself of his own appearance.

It is also from this immediate knowledge of one's own will that the concept of *freedom* arises in human consciousness. For the will as creator of the world, as thing in itself, is indeed free from the principle of sufficient reason and thus from all necessity: it is wholly independent, free, even omnipotent. Strictly speaking, however, this is true only of the will in itself, not of its appearances, the individuals, who, precisely through the will itself, are already unalterably determined as its appearances in time. Ordinary consciousness, not clarified by philosophy, at once confuses the will with its appearance and attributes to the latter what belongs only to the former. Thus arises the illusion of the individual's unconditional freedom. For just this reason Spinoza rightly says that even a thrown stone, if it had consciousness, would believe it was flying of its own free will. For the in-itself of the stone too is indeed the one free will; but here, as in all its appearances, where it appears as a stone it is already entirely determined. Enough has already been said about all this in the main body of this work.

Kant, failing to recognize or notice this immediate origin of the concept of freedom in every human consciousness, instead places its origin, at p. 533; V, 561, in a very subtle speculation. According to him, the unconditioned, toward which reason is always supposed to strive, occasions the hypostatization of the concept of freedom; and it is only on this transcendent idea of freedom that the practical concept of it is supposed to rest. In the *Critique of Practical Reason*, § 6, and at p. 185 of the fourth edition, p. 235 of Rosenkranz's edition, however, he derives this latter concept differently again, from the categorical imperative's presupposition of it. On this account the speculative idea would be merely the first origin of the concept of freedom, while here it would first acquire its proper meaning and application. Neither account is right. The illusion that the individual is perfectly free in each of his actions is most vividly held by the most uncultivated person, who has never reflected; it is therefore founded on no speculation, though it is often carried into speculation. Those free of this illusion are only philosophers, and indeed the most profound philosophers, as well as the most thoughtful and enlightened writers of the Church.

In view of all that has been said, the true origin of the concept of freedom is in no essential respect an inference, whether from the speculative idea of an unconditioned cause or from the categorical imperative's presupposition of it. It springs directly from consciousness, in which everyone immediately knows himself as *will*: as that which, being a thing in itself, does not have the principle of sufficient reason as its form, and which itself depends on nothing, while everything else depends on it. Yet, lacking philosophical criticism and reflection, he does not at the same time distinguish himself, as an appearance of this will already entered into time and determined—one might say a force of will—from the will to life itself. Hence, instead of recognizing his whole existence as an act of his freedom, he looks for that freedom in his individual actions. On this point I refer to my prize essay on the freedom of the will.

If Kant had merely inferred the thing in itself, as he claims here and apparently did on earlier occasions, and had done so with the great inconsistency of an inference he himself absolutely forbade, what a strange coincidence it would be that here, where for the first time he approaches the thing in itself more closely and illuminates it, he at once recognizes in it the *will*: the free will that manifests itself in the world only through temporal appearances! I therefore actually assume, although it cannot be proved, that whenever Kant spoke of the thing in itself, in the darkest depths of his mind he was already thinking, indistinctly, of the will. Some evidence for this is given in the preface to the second edition of the *Critique of Pure Reason*, pp. XXVII–XXVIII, or p. 677 of the supplements in Rosenkranz's edition.

Moreover, it is this intended solution of the supposed third conflict that gives Kant the opportunity to express beautifully the deepest thoughts of his entire philosophy. He does so throughout the “Sixth Section of the Antinomy of Pure Reason,” but above all in the exposition of the contrast between empirical and intelligible character, pp. 534–550; V, 562–578, which I count among the finest things ever said by a human being. (A parallel passage in the *Critique of Practical Reason*, pp. 169–179 of the fourth edition, or pp. 224–231 of Rosenkranz's edition, should be read as a supplementary explanation.) It is all the more regrettable, then, that these thoughts do not stand in the right place. In part, they were not reached by the path the exposition describes, and should therefore be derived differently; in part, they do not fulfill the purpose for which they appear here, namely the solution of the supposed antinomy. From the appearance Kant infers its intelligible ground, the thing in itself, by the inconsistent application of the category of causality beyond all appearance that I have already criticized at length. For this case he presents the human will as the thing in itself—calling it *reason*, quite unjustifiably and in an inexcusable violation of ordinary usage—and appeals to an unconditional ought, the categorical imperative, which he simply postulates.

Instead, the plain and open procedure would have been to start directly from the will and show it to be the in-itself of our own appearance, known without any mediation. One could then give that account of empirical and intelligible character, showing how all actions, though necessitated by motives, are nevertheless necessarily and unconditionally attributed, both by their agent and by another who judges them, to that agent alone, as depending solely on him, so that guilt and merit are assigned to him accordingly. This was the only direct path to knowledge of what is not appearance and hence cannot be discovered according to the laws of appearance, but is what reveals itself, becomes knowable, and objectifies itself through appearance: the will to life. By analogy alone, it should then have been presented as the in-itself of every appearance. But Kant could then certainly not have said (p. 546; V, 574) that in inanimate, and even in animal, nature no faculty can be thought of except as conditioned by the senses. In Kant's language this really means that explanation by the law of causality exhausts even the innermost essence of those appearances; thus, quite inconsistently, the thing in itself disappears in their case. Kant's placing of the thing in itself in the wrong position, and the circuitous derivation that follows from it, have also falsified the whole concept. For when the will, or thing in itself, is reached through a search for an unconditioned cause, it enters into the relation of cause to effect with appearance. But this relation obtains only *within* appearance and therefore already presupposes it. It cannot join appearance itself to what lies outside it and differs from it *toto genere* (in kind altogether).

Furthermore, the announced aim of solving the third antinomy is not achieved by deciding that both sides are right, each in a different sense. For neither thesis nor antithesis speaks of the thing in itself: both speak throughout of appearance, the objective world, the world as representation. It is of this, and nothing else, that the thesis seeks to prove, by the sophism already exposed, that it contains unconditioned causes; and of this same world the antithesis rightly denies it. The entire account of the transcendental freedom of the will, insofar as it is a thing in itself, offered here in defense of the thesis is therefore, however excellent in itself, quite literally a μετάβασις εἰς ἄλλο γένος (a shift to another kind of subject). The transcendental freedom of the will described here is by no means the unconditioned causality of a cause asserted by the thesis. A cause must essentially be an appearance, not something lying beyond all appearance and differing from it *toto genere* (in kind altogether).

When cause and effect are under discussion, the relation of the will to its appearance—or of intelligible to empirical character—must never be introduced, as it is here, for it is entirely different from the causal relation. Nevertheless this solution of the antinomy also says, correctly, that the empirical character of a human being, like that of every other cause in nature, is unalterably determined and that actions consequently issue from it of necessity in accordance with external influences. Thus, despite all transcendental freedom—that is, the independence of the will in itself from the laws connecting its appearances—no human being has the power to begin a series of actions of himself, although this is precisely what the thesis asserted. Freedom, then, has no causality either. Only the will is free, and it lies outside nature or appearance, which is merely its objectivation; it does not stand in a causal relation to appearance. That relation is first found within appearance, and so already presupposes it: it cannot include appearance itself and join it to what is expressly not appearance. The world itself can be explained only from the will, since it is the will itself insofar as it appears, and not by causality. But *in the world* causality is the sole principle of explanation, and everything happens solely according to laws of nature. Right is therefore entirely on the side of the antithesis, which keeps to the subject under discussion and uses the principle of explanation valid for it; it needs no defense. The thesis, by contrast, can be defended only by moving away from the question: first by leaping to something altogether different from what was asked about, and then by carrying over to it a principle of explanation that cannot be applied there.

The fourth conflict, as I have said, is in its innermost meaning a repetition of the third. In its solution Kant develops still further the untenability of the thesis. Yet he gives no reason for its truth or for its supposed compatibility with the antithesis, just as he can offer nothing against the antithesis. He introduces the assumption of the thesis almost as a plea, although he himself calls it (p. 562; V, 590) an arbitrary presupposition whose object might well be impossible in itself. He merely makes a quite feeble attempt to find some safe corner for it against the antithesis's overwhelming force, so as to avoid revealing the emptiness of the whole claim, once he has grown attached to it, that human reason necessarily falls into antinomy.


Next comes the chapter on the transcendental ideal, which suddenly transports us back into the rigid scholasticism of the Middle Ages. One seems to hear Anselm of Canterbury himself. The *ens realissimum* (most real being), the sum of all realities and the content of all affirmative propositions, appears with the claim to be a necessary thought of reason! For my part, I must confess that such a thought is impossible for my reason and that the words meant to express it give me no definite idea.

I do not doubt, moreover, that Kant was driven to this strange chapter, so unworthy of him, solely by his fondness for architectural symmetry. The three main objects of scholastic philosophy—which, as I have said, can in the broader sense be taken to extend up to Kant—soul, world, and God, were to be derived from the three possible major premises of syllogisms. Yet plainly they arose, and can arise, solely through an unconditional application of the principle of sufficient reason. Once the soul had been forced into the categorical judgment and the hypothetical judgment had been used for the world, nothing remained for the third idea but the disjunctive major premise. Fortunately there was some earlier material to hand: the scholastics' *ens realissimum*, together with the ontological proof of God's existence, outlined by Anselm of Canterbury and then perfected by Descartes. Kant gladly made use of it, perhaps also recalling a Latin work of his youth. But the sacrifice he makes to his love of architectural symmetry in this chapter is exceedingly great. In defiance of all truth, the grotesque notion—one must call it that—of a sum of all possible realities is made an essential and necessary thought of reason. To derive it Kant takes up the false claim that our knowledge of individual things arises by progressively narrowing general concepts, and hence also a most general concept containing all reality *within itself*. In this he contradicts his own teaching as much as the truth. Our knowledge proceeds in just the opposite way, extending from the individual to the universal. Every general concept arises by abstraction from actual individual things known in intuition. Abstraction can proceed up to the most general concept, which then comprises everything *under* it but almost nothing *within* it. Kant has thus turned the procedure of our cognitive faculty upside down. He might even be accused of having provided an occasion for a philosophical charlatanry made famous in our own day: instead of recognizing concepts as thoughts abstracted from things, it makes concepts primary and sees in things only concrete concepts. In this way it puts an inverted world on the market as a philosophical farce, which naturally was bound to win great applause.

Even if we assume that every reason must, or at least can, arrive at the concept of God without revelation, it clearly does so only by following the thread of causality; this is so evident that it needs no proof. Thus Christian Wolff too says (*Cosmologia generalis*, preface, p. 1): *Sane in theologia naturali existentiam Numinis e principiis cosmologicis demonstramus. Contingentia universi et ordinis naturae, una cum impossibilitate casus, sunt scala, per quam a mundo hoc a adspectabili a Deum ascenditur* (“Indeed, in natural theology we demonstrate the existence of the Deity from cosmological principles. The contingency of the universe and of the order of nature, together with the impossibility of chance, are the ladder by which one ascends from this visible world to God”). Before him Leibniz said of the law of causality: *Sans ce grand principe nous ne pourrions jamais prouver l'existence de Dieu* (“Without this great principle we could never prove the existence of God”; *Théodicée*, § 44). Likewise, in his controversy with Clarke, § 126: *J'ose dire que sans ce grand principe on ne saurait venir à la preuve de l'existence de Dieu* (“I venture to say that without this great principle one could not arrive at a proof of God's existence”). By contrast, the thought developed in this chapter is so far from being essential and necessary to reason that it must be regarded as a real masterpiece among the monstrous productions of an age driven by strange circumstances onto the strangest wrong paths and into the strangest errors, as the scholastic age was: an age without parallel in world history, which can never recur. It is true that scholasticism, when fully developed, based its chief proof of God's existence on the concept of the *ens realissimum*, using the other proofs only as accessories. But that is merely a method of teaching and proves nothing about the origin of theology in the human mind. Kant has mistaken the procedure of scholasticism for that of reason, as he has done on other occasions too. If the idea of God really arose, according to essential laws of reason, from the disjunctive syllogism in the form of an idea of the most real being, it would have occurred among the philosophers of antiquity as well. Yet there is no trace of the *ens realissimum* in any ancient philosopher. Some do indeed teach a maker of the world, but only as a shaper of matter that existed independently of him, a δημιουργός (demiurge); and they infer him solely according to the law of causality. Sextus Empiricus (*Adversus mathematicos*, IX, § 88) does report an argument of *Cleanthes* which some have taken for the ontological proof. It is no such thing, but merely an inference by analogy: experience teaches that on earth *one* being is always more excellent than another and that the human being, as the most excellent, completes the series, though still with many defects. There must therefore be a still more excellent being and finally one most excellent of all, κράτιστον, ἄριστον (“strongest, best”); and that would be God.

As for the extensive refutation of speculative theology that now follows, I have only this brief observation. Like the entire critique of the three so-called ideas of reason—that is, the whole Dialectic of Pure Reason—it is in a sense the goal and purpose of the entire work. Yet this polemical part lacks the wholly general, lasting, and purely philosophical interest of the preceding doctrinal part, the Aesthetic and Analytic. It is more bound to its time and place, because it relates specifically to the chief features of the philosophy prevailing in Europe up to Kant. His complete overthrow of that philosophy through this polemic nevertheless remains to his immortal credit. He eliminated theism from philosophy, which, as a science and not a doctrine of faith, can give a place only to what is either empirically given or established by sound proofs. Naturally I mean philosophy genuinely and seriously pursued, directed toward truth and nothing else, not the sham philosophy of the universities. In that philosophy speculative theology still plays the leading role, as before; and the soul still appears without ceremony as if it were a familiar person. For it is a philosophy endowed with salaries, fees, and even the titles of court councillor. Looking proudly down from its heights, it has failed to notice people like me for forty years, and would gladly be rid of old *Kant* and his critiques too, so that it could loudly celebrate Leibniz once more. It should further be noted that, just as Kant admittedly was led to his teaching that the concept of causality is *a priori* by *Hume's* skepticism about that concept, Kant's critique of all speculative theology may likewise have been prompted by *Hume's* critique of all popular theology in his very readable *Natural History of Religion* and *Dialogues Concerning Natural Religion*. Kant may even have intended in a way to complete Hume's work. The former of Hume's writings is really a critique of popular theology: it seeks to expose its wretchedness while respectfully pointing instead to rational or speculative theology as the genuine article. Kant now exposes the groundlessness of the latter, while leaving popular theology untouched and even presenting it in an ennobled form as a faith supported by moral feeling. Later the philosophical dabblers distorted this into things supposedly heard by reason, consciousness of God, or intellectual intuitions of the supersensible or the deity, and the like. Kant, however, in pulling down venerable old errors and knowing the danger involved, had merely wanted to prop them up for the time being with a few weak supports from moral theology, so that the collapse would not fall on him and he could have time to get away.

As for the execution of the argument, no critique of reason was needed to refute the *ontological* proof of God's existence. Even without presupposing the Aesthetic and Analytic, it is easy to show clearly that this proof is nothing but a subtle play with concepts, devoid of all power to convince. Aristotle's *Organon* already contains a chapter that suffices so completely to refute the ontological proof that it might have been written for the purpose: chapter seven of the second book of the *Posterior Analytics*. Among other things, it expressly says: τὸ δὲ εἶναι οὐκ οὐσία οὐδενί (“being is the essence of nothing”); that is, *existentia nunquam ad essentiam rei pertinet* (“existence never belongs to the essence of a thing”).

The refutation of the *cosmological* proof applies the teaching of the Critique presented up to that point to a particular case, and calls for no objection. The *physico-theological* proof is a mere expansion of the cosmological proof, which it presupposes; its detailed refutation comes only in the *Critique of Judgment*. On this matter I refer my reader to the section “Comparative Anatomy” in my work *On the Will in Nature*.

As I have said, in his critique of these proofs *Kant* is concerned solely with speculative theology and confines himself to the schools. If, however, he had also considered life and popular theology, he would have had to add to the three proofs a fourth, the one that actually works on the great mass of people. In Kant's technical language the most fitting name for it might be the *ceraunological* proof. It rests on people's sense of needing help, of their powerlessness and dependence in the face of immensely superior, inscrutable, and usually threatening forces of nature. Added to this is their natural tendency to personify everything and, finally, the hope of achieving something through prayers and flattery, or even gifts. In every human undertaking there is something beyond our power and outside our calculation. The wish to win it over is the origin of the gods. *Primus in orbe Deos fecit timor* (“Fear first made gods in the world”) is an old and true saying of Petronius. It is chiefly this proof that *Hume*, who appears throughout as Kant's forerunner, criticizes in the writings mentioned above. But those whom Kant has put in lasting difficulty by his critique of speculative theology are the professors of philosophy. Paid by Christian governments, they cannot abandon the central article of faith.[^8] How do these gentlemen manage? They simply assert that God's existence is self-evident. So! After the ancient world performed miracles to prove it at the cost of its conscience, and the modern world deployed ontological, cosmological, and physico-theological proofs at the cost of its understanding, for these gentlemen it is self-evident. And from this self-evident God they then explain the world: that is their philosophy.

Until *Kant*, a genuine dilemma stood between materialism and theism: either blind chance or an intelligence ordering things from outside according to purposes and concepts must have brought the world about; *neque dabatur tertium* (“and there was no third alternative”). Hence atheism and materialism were the same thing. Hence, too, the doubt whether an atheist could really exist: a person who could actually credit blind chance with nature's extraordinarily purposive arrangement, especially in organic nature. See, for example, Bacon's *Essays, or Counsels Civil and Moral*, essay 16, “Of Atheism.” The opinion of the great mass and of the English—who in such matters belong entirely to the great mass, the *mob*—is still the same, even among their most celebrated scholars. One need only look at R. Owen's *Ostéologie comparée* (1855), preface, pp. 11–12, where he still confronts the old dilemma between Democritus and Epicurus on one side and an *intelligence* on the other, in which *la connaissance d'un être tel que l'homme a existé avant que l'homme fit son apparition* (“knowledge of a being such as man existed before man appeared”). All purposiveness must have proceeded from an *intelligence*: it has never even occurred to him to doubt this. Indeed, in the lecture delivered at the *Académie des sciences* on 5 September 1853, a somewhat different version of this preface, he said with childlike naïveté: *la téléologie, ou la théologie scientifique* (“teleology, or scientific theology”; *Comptes rendus*, September 1853). To him they are immediately one and the same! If something in nature is purposive, then it is the work of intention, deliberation, intelligence. Of course, why should an Englishman or the *Académie des sciences* care about the *Critique of Judgment*, let alone my book *On the Will in Nature*? These gentlemen do not look that far down. Those *illustres confréres* (“illustrious colleagues”) despise metaphysics and *philosopie allemande* (“German philosophy”): they adhere to the philosophy of the distaff. Yet the validity of that disjunctive major premise, that dilemma between materialism and theism, rests on the assumption that the world before us is the world of things in themselves, and thus that there is no order of things other than the empirical one. Once Kant had made the world and its order mere appearance, whose laws rest chiefly on the forms of our intellect, the existence and essence of things and of the world no longer had to be explained by analogy with changes we perceive or bring about in the world. Nor did what we apprehend as means and end have to have arisen through knowledge of that kind. Thus Kant, by his important distinction between appearance and thing in itself, deprived theism of its foundation while opening the way to quite different and more profound explanations of existence.

In the chapter on the ultimate aims of the natural dialectic of reason, it is claimed that the three transcendent ideas are valuable as regulative principles for the advance of our knowledge of nature. But Kant can hardly have meant this seriously. Every natural scientist will at least be certain of the opposite: that such assumptions obstruct and stifle all investigation of nature. As one test, consider whether the assumption of a soul as an immaterial, simple, thinking substance would have helped or, rather, greatly hindered the truths so beautifully set out by *Cabanis* and the discoveries of *Flourens*, Marshall *Hall*, and Charles *Bell*. Indeed, Kant himself says (*Prolegomena*, § 44) that “the ideas of reason run counter to and hinder the maxims of reason's knowledge of nature.”

It is certainly among Frederick the Great's merits that under his rule Kant could develop his thought and publish the *Critique of Pure Reason*. A salaried professor would hardly have dared do such a thing under any other government. Kant had to promise the great king's successor that he would write no more.

I might regard myself as excused from criticizing the ethical part of Kant's philosophy here, since twenty-two years after the present critique I provided a fuller and more thorough one in *The Two Fundamental Problems of Ethics*. Nevertheless, what is retained here from the first edition could not have been omitted, if only for completeness, and can serve as a suitable prelude to that later and much more thorough critique, to which I accordingly refer the reader for the main argument.


In keeping with his love of architectural symmetry, theoretical reason had to have a *pendant* (counterpart). The scholastics' *intellectus practicus* (practical intellect), itself descended from Aristotle's νοῦς πρακτικός (practical intellect; *De anima*, III, 10, and *Politics*, VII, ch. 14: μὲν γὰρ πρακτικός ἐστι λόγος, ὁ δὲ θεωρητικός, “one kind of reason is practical, the other theoretical”), supplied the name. Here, however, it denotes something quite different: not reason directed toward technique, as it did there. Practical reason now appears as the source and origin of the undeniable ethical significance of human action and of all virtue, all nobility of character, and every attainable degree of holiness. All this would therefore come from *reason* alone and require nothing but reason. To act rationally and to act virtuously, nobly, or holily would be one and the same thing; to act selfishly, maliciously, or viciously would merely be to act irrationally. Yet all ages, peoples, and languages have always distinguished the two and regarded them as entirely different things. So does everyone who knows nothing of the language of the modern school—that is, the whole world except a small group of German scholars. All of these people understand a virtuous way of life and a rational course of life as two quite different things. To call the exalted founder of the Christian religion, whose life is offered to us as the model of all virtue, the *most rational* of human beings would be considered a most unworthy, perhaps even blasphemous expression. Almost the same would be true of saying that his precepts contain merely the best instructions for a thoroughly *rational life*. Again, everyone rightly reveres the person who, following those precepts, does not think ahead of himself and his own future needs, but always relieves the greater present need of others without further concern, and even gives away all his possessions to the poor before going, stripped of every resource, to preach to others the virtue he himself has practiced. But who dares praise this as the height of *rationality*? And, finally, who praises as an exceptionally *rational* deed Arnold von Winkelried's supreme nobility in gathering the enemy's spears against his own body to secure victory and rescue for his countrymen? By contrast, suppose we see a man who from his youth takes exceptional care to secure the means of a life free from worry, support for his wife and children, a good name, and public honor and distinction. He does not let himself be diverted by the lure of present pleasures, the thrill of defying the arrogance of the powerful, the wish to avenge insults or undeserved humiliations, the attraction of useless aesthetic or philosophical pursuits, or travel to countries worth seeing. None of this makes him lose sight of his aim; with great consistency he works for it alone. Who would deny that such a philistine is extraordinarily *rational*, even if he has allowed himself some discreditable but risk-free means? More than this: suppose a villain, through calculating cunning and a carefully thought-out plan, helps himself to riches, honors, even thrones and crowns. Then with the finest guile he entangles neighboring states, subdues them one by one, and becomes a conqueror of the world. He is never distracted by considerations of justice or humanity but relentlessly tramples and crushes whatever opposes his plan. Without pity he plunges millions into every kind of misery and millions into bloodshed and death, yet rewards his followers and helpers royally and always protects them, forgetting nothing, until he achieves his aim. Who does not see that such a man must have proceeded with consummate rationality? The plans required a powerful understanding, but their execution required perfect command of *reason*, indeed *practical reason* in the strictest sense. Or are the counsels that the clever, consistent, reflective, and farsighted Machiavelli gives his prince perhaps *irrational*?[^9]

Just as malice is perfectly compatible with reason, and indeed becomes truly terrible in combination with it, nobility of character is sometimes found together with irrationality. The action of Coriolanus may be counted as such a case. After years spent exerting all his powers to take revenge on the Romans, he finally has his opportunity, yet allows the pleas of the Senate and the tears of his mother and wife to soften him. He gives up the revenge so long and laboriously prepared, and even draws the just anger of the Volsci upon himself, dying for the very Romans whose ingratitude he knows and had worked so hard to punish. For completeness, let me add that reason can also be combined with a lack of understanding. This happens when one chooses a foolish maxim but follows it consistently. An example was Princess Isabella, daughter of Philip II, who vowed not to put on a clean shirt until Ostend was conquered and kept her word for three years. All vows belong here in general: they originate in a failure to understand the law of causality, that is, in a lack of understanding. Even so, fulfilling them is rational once one has been so limited in understanding as to make them.

In keeping with these examples, writers appearing shortly before *Kant* set conscience, the seat of moral impulses, against reason. Thus *Rousseau* says in book four of *Émile*: *La raison nous trompe, mais la conscience ne trompe jamais* (“Reason deceives us, but conscience never deceives”); and a little later: *il est impossible d'expliquer par les conséquences de notre nature le principe immédiat de la conscience indépendant de la raison même* (“It is impossible to explain through the consequences of our nature the immediate principle of conscience, independent even of reason”). Later still: *Mes sentimens naturels parlaient pour l'intérêt commun, ma raison rapportait tout à moi. – – – On a beau vouloir établir la vertu par la raison seule, quelle solide base peut-on lui donner?* (“My natural feelings spoke for the common interest; my reason referred everything to myself. However much one wishes to establish virtue by reason alone, what solid basis can one give it?”). In the *Reveries of the Solitary Walker*, fourth walk, he says: *Dans toutes les questions de morale difficiles je me suis toujours bien trouvé de les résoudre par le dictamen de la conscience, plutôt que par les lumières de la raison* (“In all difficult moral questions I have always done well to settle them by the dictate of conscience rather than the lights of reason”). Indeed, *Aristotle* expressly says (*Magna Moralia*, I, 5) that the virtues have their seat in the ἀλόγῳ μορίῳ τῆς ψυχῆς, *in parte irrationali animi* (“irrational part of the soul”), not in the λόγον ἔχοντι, *in parte rationali* (“rational part”). Accordingly *Stobaeus*, speaking of the Peripatetics (*Eclogae*, II, ch. 7), says: Τὴν ἠθικὴν ἀρετὴν ὑπολαμβάνουσι περὶ τὸ ἄλογον μέρος γίγνεσθαι τῆς ψυχῆς, ἐπειδὴ διμερῆ πρὸς τὴν παροῦσαν θεωρίαν ὑπέθεντο τὴν ψυχήν, τὸ μὲν λογικὸν ἔχουσαν, τὸ δ’ ἄλογον. Καὶ περὶ μὲν τὸ λογικὸν τὴν καλοκαγαθίαν γίγνεσθαι, καὶ τὴν φρόνησιν, καὶ τὴν ἀγχίνοιαν, καὶ σοφίαν, καὶ εὐμάθειαν, καὶ μνήμην, καὶ τὰς ὁμοίους· περὶ δὲ τὸ ἄλογον, σωφροσύνην, καὶ δικαιοσύνην, καὶ ἀνδρείαν, καὶ τὰς ἄλλας τὰς ἠθικὰς καλουμένας ἀρετάς.[^stobaeus-text] (In Latin: *Ethicam virtutem circa partem, animae ratione carentem versari putant, cum duplicem, ad hanc disquisitionem, animam ponant, ratione praeditam, et ea carentem. In parte vero ratione praedita collocant ingenuitatem, prudentiam, perspicacitatem, sapientiam, docilitatem, memoriam et reliqua; in parte vero ratione destituta temperantiam, justitiam, fortitudinem, et reliquas virtutes, quas ethicas vocant.* “They hold that ethical virtue belongs to the part of the soul without reason, since for this inquiry they posit a soul with two parts, one endowed with reason and one without it. In the rational part they place nobility, prudence, quickness of insight, wisdom, teachability, memory, and the like; in the part without reason, temperance, justice, courage, and the other virtues called ethical.”) And *Cicero* argues at length (*De natura deorum*, III, chs. 26–31) that reason is the necessary means and instrument of every crime.


I have defined *reason* as the *faculty of concepts*. This distinctive class of general representations, which are not intuitive but are symbolized and fixed only by words, distinguishes human beings from animals and gives them mastery over the earth. The animal is the slave of the present. It knows no motives but those immediately given through the senses and, when they appear, is drawn to or repelled by them as necessarily as iron by a magnet. In human beings, by contrast, the gift of reason gives rise to reflection. It lets them look backward and forward, easily survey their own lives and the course of the world as a whole, become independent of the present, and act deliberately, according to a plan and with forethought, for evil as well as for good. Whatever a person does, however, he does with complete self-consciousness: he knows exactly how his will decides, what he chooses in each case, and what other choice was objectively possible. From his self-conscious willing he comes to know himself and sees himself reflected in his deeds. In all these relations to human action, reason may be called *practical*. It is theoretical only when the objects with which it deals bear no relation to the thinker's actions and have merely theoretical interest, of which very few people are capable. What in this sense is called *practical reason* is fairly well expressed by the Latin *prudentia* (prudence), which according to Cicero (*De natura deorum*, II, 22) is a contraction of *providentia* (foresight). *Ratio* (reason), by contrast, when used of a mental power, usually means theoretical reason proper, although the ancients did not observe the distinction strictly. In almost everyone reason has an almost exclusively practical direction. But if even this is abandoned, thought loses command over action; then one says, *scio meliora, proboque, deteriora sequor* (“I know and approve the better, but follow the worse”), or *le matin je fais des projets, et le soir je fais des sottises* (“in the morning I make plans, and in the evening I do foolish things”). When a person allows his actions to be guided by the impression of the present rather than by his thought, almost as an animal does, he is called *irrational*. This does not impute moral wickedness to him. What he really lacks is not reason but its application to his actions; one might say that his reason is merely theoretical, not practical. He may still be an excellent person, like someone who cannot see an unfortunate person without helping him, even at a sacrifice, yet leaves his debts unpaid. Such an irrational character is quite incapable of great crimes, because the planning, dissimulation, and self-control always needed for them are beyond him. He is also unlikely, however, to achieve a very high degree of virtue. However strongly disposed toward good he may be by nature, the occasional vicious and malicious impulses to which everyone is subject are bound to arise. Unless reason operates practically, opposing them with unchanging maxims and firm resolutions, those impulses must become actions.

Finally, *reason* shows itself as *practical* in the strictest sense in truly rational characters, whom ordinary life therefore calls practical philosophers. They are distinguished by extraordinary calm in unpleasant as well as pleasant events, an even temper, and steady adherence to decisions once made. What prevails in them is reason: knowledge that is more abstract than intuitive and therefore enables them, through concepts, to survey life generally and as a whole. It has taught them once and for all the deceptiveness of momentary impressions, the impermanence of all things, the brevity of life, the emptiness of pleasures, the changes of fortune, and the great and small tricks of chance. Nothing therefore comes unexpectedly to them. What they know *in abstracto* (in the abstract) does not surprise or disconcert them when they encounter it in reality and in particular cases. Less rational characters, by contrast, are so strongly affected by the present, the intuitive, and the actual that cold and colorless concepts recede entirely into the background of consciousness. Forgetting resolutions and maxims, they are at the mercy of feelings and passions of every kind. At the end of the first book I already explained that, in my view, Stoic ethics was originally nothing but instruction in a genuinely rational life in this sense. Horace repeatedly commends such a life in many places. His *Nil admirari* (“Be astonished at nothing”) belongs here, as does the Delphic Μηδὲν ἄγαν (“Nothing in excess”). To translate *Nil admirari* as “Admire nothing” is quite wrong. Horace's saying concerns practice more than theory. Its actual meaning is: “Do not value anything unconditionally; become infatuated with nothing; do not believe that possessing anything can confer happiness. Every inexpressible craving for an object is only a teasing chimera, which can be dispelled just as well, and much more easily, by clearer knowledge than by gaining possession.” Cicero too uses *admirari* in this sense in *De divinatione*, II, 2. What Horace means, then, is the ἀθαμβία and ἀκαταπληξία, also ἀθαυμασία (“freedom from alarm, from being overwhelmed, and from astonishment”), which Democritus already praised as the highest good (see Clement of Alexandria, *Stromata*, II, 21, and compare Strabo, I, pp. 98 and 105). Virtue and vice are not really at issue in a life rational in this way. But this practical use of reason brings into play the genuine advantage humans have over animals, and only in this respect does it make sense and become permissible to speak of human dignity.

In all the cases described, and in all conceivable cases, the distinction between rational and irrational action comes down to whether the motives are abstract concepts or intuitive representations. My account of reason therefore agrees exactly with the usage of all ages and peoples. No one should regard such usage as accidental or arbitrary: it has arisen from the distinction among mental faculties of which everyone is aware and in accordance with which everyone speaks, though without raising that awareness to the clarity of an abstract definition. Our ancestors did not make words without attaching definite meanings to them, as if setting them aside for philosophers centuries later to decide what they should mean. They used them to designate quite definite concepts. Words are therefore no longer ownerless; to assign them meanings altogether different from those they have previously had is to misuse them and introduce a license under which everyone could use every word in any sense he pleased, producing boundless confusion. *Locke* has already shown at length that most disagreements in philosophy arise from the misuse of words. For illustration, one need only glance at the disgraceful abuse that today's thoughtless philosophical dabblers make of such words as substance, consciousness, and truth. The statements and explanations of philosophers in every age, apart from the very latest, agree with my account of reason no less than do the prevailing views of this human advantage among all peoples. See what Plato calls the λόγιμον or λογιστικὸν τῆς ψυχῆς (“the reasoning part of the soul”) in the fourth book of the *Republic* and countless other passages; what Cicero says in *De natura deorum*, III, 26–31; and what Leibniz and Locke say in the passages on this point cited in the first book. There would be no end to quotations if one wished to show how all philosophers before Kant spoke of reason broadly in my sense, even if they did not explain its nature with complete precision and clarity by reducing it to a single point. What was understood by reason shortly before Kant appeared can be seen in two essays by Sulzer in the first volume of his miscellaneous philosophical writings: “Analysis of the Concept of Reason” and “On the Reciprocal Influence of Reason and Language.” Read, by contrast, how reason has been discussed in recent times under the influence of Kant's error, which subsequently grew like an avalanche, and one has to conclude that all the sages of antiquity, and indeed all philosophers before Kant, had no reason at all. For the immediate perceptions, intuitions, apprehensions, and premonitions of reason now discovered were as foreign to them as the sixth sense of bats is to us. For my part, I must confess that, in my limited capacity, I can conceive and imagine a reason that immediately perceives, apprehends, or intellectually intuits the supersensible, the absolute, and the long stories said to happen to it, only as I can imagine the sixth sense of bats. Still, one must credit the invention—or discovery—of a reason that immediately perceives whatever one likes with being an incomparable *expédient* (expedient) for extricating oneself and one's cherished fixed ideas in the easiest possible way, in spite of every *Kant* and his critiques of reason. The invention, and the reception it has found, do honor to the age.

Although the essential nature of *reason* (τὸ λόγιμον, ἡ φρόνησις, *ratio, raison, reason*—roughly, the rational faculty or prudence) has thus been recognized correctly in general by philosophers of all ages, though not defined sharply enough or reduced to a single point, they have been less clear about the nature of the *understanding* (νοῦς, διάνοια, *intellectus, esprit, intellect, understanding*—terms for intellect or understanding). They therefore often mix it with reason and consequently fail to give a perfectly clear, pure, and simple explanation of reason itself. Among Christian philosophers the concept of reason acquired an entirely foreign secondary meaning through its opposition to revelation. Starting from this, many rightly maintained that knowledge of the obligation to virtue is possible through reason alone, that is, without revelation. This consideration certainly influenced even Kant's account and choice of words. Yet that contrast has a positive, historical significance; it is therefore foreign to philosophy, which must be kept free of it.

One might have expected Kant, in his critiques of theoretical and practical reason, to begin with an account of the nature of reason in general and, having thus defined the *genus* (kind), proceed to explain its two *species* (forms). He should have shown how one and the same reason manifests itself in two such different ways while retaining its chief character and so proving itself the same faculty. Nothing of this is to be found. I have already shown how inadequate, wavering, and discordant are the explanations he gives here and there in passing, in the *Critique of Pure Reason*, of the faculty he is criticizing. *Practical* reason makes an unannounced appearance in that Critique and subsequently stands in the critique devoted to it as an established fact, without further accounting for it. Neither the usage of all ages and peoples, which is trampled underfoot, nor the definitions of the greatest earlier philosophers are allowed a voice. Taken together, individual passages suggest that Kant's view is this: knowledge of *a priori* principles is the essential character of reason. Since knowledge of the ethical significance of action is not of empirical origin, it too is an *a priori* principle and therefore stems from reason, which in this respect is *practical*. I have already said enough about the falsity of that definition of reason. Even setting this aside, how superficial and groundless it is to use the single feature of independence from experience to unite the most heterogeneous things, overlooking the fundamental, immeasurable distance between them in every other respect. For suppose, though I do not grant it, that knowledge of the ethical significance of action arose from an imperative within us, an unconditional *ought*. How fundamentally different such an imperative would be from those universal *forms of knowledge* that Kant shows in the *Critique of Pure Reason* to be known to us *a priori*, and by awareness of which we can state in advance an unconditional *must*, valid for every experience possible to us. The difference between this *must*, the necessary form of every object already determined in the subject, and the *ought* of morality is as vast as it is obvious. The fact that both share the character of nonempirical knowledge might serve as a witty comparison, but cannot furnish a philosophical warrant for identifying their origins.


The birthplace of this child of practical reason, the *absolute ought* or categorical imperative, is, moreover, not the *Critique of Practical Reason* but already the *Critique of Pure Reason*, p. 802; V, 830. The birth is difficult and succeeds only with the obstetric forceps of a *therefore* boldly, even shamelessly, placed between two utterly unrelated propositions to connect them as ground and consequence. Kant begins with the proposition that we are determined by abstract as well as intuitive motives, putting it as follows: “Not only what stimulates, that is, immediately affects the senses, determines human choice. We have a faculty of overcoming impressions on our sensuous faculty of desire through representations of what is useful or harmful even in a more distant way. These reflections on what is desirable, that is, good and useful, with regard to our condition as a whole rest on reason.” (Perfectly right: if only he always spoke so rationally about reason!) “*Therefore* reason also gives laws which are imperatives, that is, objective laws of freedom, and say what ought to happen, although perhaps it never does.” And so, with no further credentials, the categorical imperative leaps into the world to rule it with its unconditional *ought*: a scepter of wooden iron. For the concept of *ought* essentially and in every case includes reference to a threatened punishment or promised reward as a necessary condition. This cannot be separated from it without abolishing the concept itself and stripping it of all meaning. An *unconditional ought* is therefore a *contradictio in adjecto* (contradiction in terms). This error had to be criticized, closely related though it is to Kant's great achievement in ethics. That achievement is that he freed ethics from all principles of the empirical world, especially from every direct or indirect doctrine of happiness, and showed in a strict sense that the realm of virtue is not of this world. His achievement is all the greater because the ancient philosophers—except Plato alone, namely the Peripatetics, Stoics, and Epicureans—used very different devices to make virtue and happiness either depend on each other according to the principle of sufficient reason or identical according to the principle of contradiction. The same reproach applies to every modern philosopher up to Kant. His achievement here is therefore very great. Justice requires us to add, however, that his exposition and execution often fail to match the tendency and spirit of his ethics, as we shall see at once. Nor, even so, was he the first to cleanse virtue of every principle of happiness. Plato had already taught explicitly, especially in the *Republic*, whose chief purpose is precisely this, that virtue must be chosen for its own sake alone, even if misfortune and disgrace were inevitably joined to it. Christianity, still more, preaches a wholly disinterested virtue, practiced without payment out of love of God, and not even for reward in an afterlife, since works do not justify, but only faith, which virtue accompanies as its mere sign and so enters wholly without payment and of itself. Read Luther's *De libertate Christiana* (*On the Freedom of a Christian*). I shall not even count the Indians, whose sacred books everywhere portray the hope of reward for one's works as the path of darkness, which can never lead to salvation. Kant's doctrine of virtue is nevertheless not so pure; or rather, its exposition has fallen far behind its spirit and even into inconsistency. In his *highest* good, discussed later, virtue is married to happiness. The originally unconditional ought subsequently postulates a condition for itself, really to rid itself of the internal contradiction with which it cannot live. Happiness in the highest good is indeed not supposed actually to be the motive for virtue. Yet there it stands like a secret clause whose presence turns all the rest into a merely apparent agreement. It is not exactly the reward of virtue, but it is a voluntary gift toward which virtue, after finishing its work, furtively holds out its hand. One can see this in the *Critique of Practical Reason* (pp. 223–266 of the fourth edition, or pp. 264–295 of Rosenkranz's edition). His entire moral theology has the same tendency, and through it morality really destroys itself. For, I repeat, every virtue practiced for any kind of reward rests on a shrewd, systematic, farsighted egoism.

The content of the absolute ought, the basic law of practical reason, is the celebrated maxim: “Act so that the maxim of your will could always at the same time hold as a principle of universal legislation.” This principle gives someone seeking a rule for his own will the task of finding one for the wills of *everyone*. The question then is how to find it. To discover the rule for my conduct I am evidently meant to consider not myself alone but the entire aggregate of individuals. The welfare of everyone without distinction, rather than my own, then becomes my aim. But that aim remains welfare. I find that everyone can be equally well off only if each person sets the egoism of others as a limit to his own. It certainly follows that I should harm no one, because if this principle is accepted universally I too will not be harmed. But that is the only reason why I, as someone who does not yet have a moral principle but is seeking one, can wish for this to be a universal law. Plainly, then, the desire for welfare, that is, egoism, remains the source of this ethical principle. It would be excellent as a basis for political theory; it is unfit as a basis for ethics. For to establish, as this moral principle requires, a rule for the wills of everyone, the person seeking it necessarily needs a rule of his own; otherwise everything would be indifferent to him. That rule can only be his own egoism, since only this is affected by the conduct of others. Only through and with reference to it can he care about others' actions and have any will regarding them. Kant very naïvely reveals this himself at p. 123 of the *Critique of Practical Reason* (p. 192 of Rosenkranz's edition), where he describes the search for a maxim of the will: “If everyone viewed the distress of others with complete indifference, and *you too belonged* to such an order of things, would you consent to it?” *Quam temere in nosmet legem sancimus iniquam* (“How rashly we decree an unjust law against ourselves!”) would be the rule governing the consent in question. Likewise in the *Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals*, p. 56 of the third edition, p. 50 of Rosenkranz's: “A will that resolved to assist no one in need would contradict itself, since cases might occur in which *it needed the love and sympathy of others*,” and so on. This ethical principle is therefore, seen in the light, merely an indirect and veiled expression of the old, simple rule *quod tibi fieri non vis, alteri ne feceris* (“Do not do to another what you do not wish done to yourself”). It refers first and immediately to passivity, to *suffering*, and only through this to *doing*. As I have said, it would therefore be quite usable as a guide to founding the *state*, which seeks to prevent *the suffering of wrong* and to secure the greatest possible sum of welfare for all. But in ethics, where the subject of inquiry is *doing* as *doing* and its immediate significance for the *agent*, not its consequence as suffering or its relation to others, that consideration is altogether inadmissible. At bottom it leads back to a principle of happiness and thus to egoism.

We therefore cannot share Kant's delight that his ethical principle is not material—that is, it sets no object as motive—but merely formal, so that it corresponds symmetrically to the formal laws made known by the *Critique of Pure Reason*. Admittedly, instead of a law it is only a formula for finding one. But we already had this formula, more briefly and clearly, in *quod tibi fieri non vis, alteri ne feceris* (“Do not do to another what you do not wish done to yourself”). Moreover, analyzing it shows that concern for one's own happiness alone gives it content. It can therefore serve only rational egoism, to which every legal constitution also owes its origin.

Another defect, often criticized because it offends everyone's feelings and mocked by Schiller in an epigram, is Kant's pedantic stipulation that, to be truly good and meritorious, an act must be performed solely out of respect for the known law and the concept of duty, according to a maxim of which reason is conscious *in abstracto* (in the abstract). It must not be performed from inclination, felt goodwill toward others, tender sympathy, compassion, or warmth of heart. These, according to the *Critique of Practical Reason* (p. 213; Rosenkranz's edition, p. 257), are even highly troublesome to right-minded people because they confuse their carefully considered maxims. Rather, the action must be done reluctantly and with self-compulsion. Remember that the hope of reward is nevertheless supposed to play no part, and judge how absurd the demand is. More than that, it directly opposes the genuine spirit of virtue. Not the deed, but doing it gladly—the love from which it springs, without which it is a dead work—makes it meritorious. Christianity therefore rightly teaches that all outward works are worthless unless they spring from that genuine disposition consisting in a true willingness and pure love. It is not the works performed (*opera operata*), but faith, the genuine disposition granted only by the Holy Spirit and not produced by a free, deliberate will that keeps only the law in view, that brings salvation and redemption. Kant's requirement that every virtuous action occur from pure, considered respect for the law and according to its abstract maxims, coldly and without, indeed against, every inclination, is exactly like claiming that every genuine work of art must arise from the well-considered application of aesthetic rules. One claim is as perverse as the other. The question already discussed by Plato and Seneca, whether virtue can be taught, must be answered no. We must finally resolve to understand what also gave rise to the Christian doctrine of election by grace: in its main substance and inward core, virtue is in a sense innate, like genius. Just as all professors of aesthetics working together cannot endow anyone with the capacity for works of genius, that is, genuine works of art, so all professors of ethics and preachers of virtue cannot transform an ignoble character into a virtuous and noble one. That impossibility is far more obvious than the impossibility of turning lead into gold. The search for an ethics and a supreme principle of it that would have practical influence and really transform and improve humankind is exactly like the search for the philosopher's stone. The possibility of a complete change of a person's disposition, or rebirth, through intuitive rather than abstract knowledge (ethics)—the work of grace—is discussed at length at the end of our fourth book. Indeed, the contents of that book spare me the need to dwell on this any longer.

Finally, Kant also shows that he has by no means penetrated the real meaning of the ethical content of actions through his doctrine of the highest good as the necessary union of virtue and happiness, with virtue amounting to worthiness for happiness. Here he is already subject to the logical objection that the concept of worthiness, which supplies the standard, itself presupposes an ethics as its standard and therefore should not have been the starting point. Our fourth book has shown that all genuine virtue, once it reaches its highest degree, leads at last to complete renunciation, in which all willing ends. Happiness, by contrast, is satisfied willing. The two are therefore fundamentally incompatible. Anyone convinced by my account needs no further explanation of how wholly mistaken Kant's view of the highest good is. Independently of my positive account, I have no further negative criticism to offer here.


Kant's love of architectural symmetry appears again in the *Critique of Practical Reason*: he gives it the same arrangement as the *Critique of Pure Reason*, even repeating the same titles and forms with evident arbitrariness, especially apparent in the table of the categories of freedom.

The *Doctrine of Right* is among Kant's latest works and is so weak that, although I reject it entirely, I consider a polemic against it unnecessary. As though it were the product of an ordinary mortal rather than the work of this great man, it must die a natural death from its own weakness. I therefore forgo the negative procedure with respect to the doctrine of right and refer to the positive account: the brief outlines of it set out in our fourth book. I shall make only a few general observations here. The errors I criticized in my discussion of the *Critique of Pure Reason* as accompanying Kant everywhere are present in the doctrine of right to such an extent that one often thinks one is reading a satirical parody of Kant's manner, or at least hearing one of his followers. There are two principal errors. First, he wishes—and many have since wished—to separate the doctrine of right sharply from ethics, while also refusing to make it dependent on positive legislation, that is, arbitrary coercion. He would have the concept of right stand on its own, pure and *a priori*. But this is impossible. Apart from its ethical significance and its physical relation to others, and thus to external coercion, action admits no third point of view whatsoever. Accordingly, when he says, “A duty of right is one that *can* be enforced,” this *can* must be understood either physically or ethically. If physically, all right is positive and arbitrary, and, conversely, every arbitrary power that can impose itself is right. If ethically, we are back in the domain of ethics. Thus Kant's concept of right hangs between heaven and earth with no ground on which to stand. In my account it belongs to ethics. Second, his definition of the concept of right is entirely negative and therefore inadequate:[^10] “Right is what is compatible with the coexistence of the freedoms of individuals alongside one another according to a universal law.” Freedom here means empirical, that is, physical freedom, not moral freedom of the will. It means the absence of hindrance and is therefore a mere negation; coexistence has exactly the same character. We are left with negations throughout and obtain no positive concept, nor do we even learn what the matter is about unless we already know from elsewhere. As the exposition continues, the most perverse views emerge: that in the state of nature, outside the state, there is no right to property at all—which really means that all right is positive and makes natural right depend on positive right when the reverse should be the case; his account of rightful acquisition through taking possession; the ethical obligation to establish a civil constitution; the basis of criminal law; and so on. As I have said, none of these seems worth a separate refutation. Meanwhile these errors of Kant's have done much harm, confusing and obscuring truths long recognized and stated, and prompting strange theories and a great deal of writing and controversy. This cannot last, of course, and already we see truth and sound reason clearing a path again. An especially good example of the latter, in contrast to so many distorted theories, is I. C. F. Meister's *Natural Right*, although I do not therefore consider it a model of complete perfection.

After what has been said, I can also be very brief about the *Critique of Judgment*. It is remarkable that Kant, to whom art probably remained quite foreign, who seems to have had little receptivity to beauty, who probably never had a chance to see an important work of art, and who appears not even to have known of Goethe—his gigantic counterpart, the only figure in his century and nation who could be placed beside him—nevertheless made a great and lasting contribution to the philosophical consideration of art and beauty. His contribution lies in this: despite the many reflections on beauty and art, the matter had previously been considered almost always from an empirical standpoint. On the basis of facts, people investigated what quality distinguished an object called *beautiful* from other objects of its kind. They first arrived at quite specific propositions, then at more general ones. They sought to separate genuine artistic beauty from spurious beauty and discover marks of authenticity that could in turn serve as rules. What pleases as beautiful, what does not, what should therefore be imitated or sought and what avoided, what rules could be laid down, at least negatively—in short, what means produce aesthetic pleasure, or what conditions for it lie *in the object*: these had been almost the only themes of reflection on art. Aristotle took this path, and we still find Home, Burke, Winckelmann, Lessing, Herder, and others on it in more recent times. The generality of the aesthetic principles discovered did eventually lead back toward the subject. It became clear that, if the effect in the subject were properly known, one could then also determine *a priori* its cause in the object, and only thus could the inquiry attain the certainty of a science. This sometimes prompted psychological discussions. Alexander Baumgarten in particular sought to establish a general aesthetics of all beauty, starting with the concept of perfection in sensuous, and thus intuitive, knowledge. Yet with the introduction of this concept he immediately finished with the subjective aspect and moved to the objective and the practical aspect related to it. Kant, by contrast, was the one to investigate seriously and deeply *the very feeling* because of which we call its object *beautiful*, seeking, if possible, to find its components and conditions in our minds. His inquiry therefore took an entirely subjective direction. This was plainly the right path: to explain a phenomenon given in its effects and determine the nature of its cause thoroughly, one must first know those effects precisely. Kant's contribution here, however, scarcely extends beyond indicating the right path and offering a preliminary example of approximately how to follow it. What he actually gave cannot be regarded as objective truth and real gain. He indicated the method of the inquiry and cleared the way, but missed the goal.

In the Critique of Aesthetic Judgment, the first thing we notice is that Kant retained the method peculiar to his whole philosophy, which I discussed at length above. I mean his starting from abstract knowledge to fathom intuitive knowledge, as though the former were a *camera obscura* (dark chamber) in which to catch and survey the latter. In the *Critique of Pure Reason* the forms of judgment were supposed to teach him about our knowledge of the whole intuitive world. Likewise, in this Critique of Aesthetic Judgment he starts not from beauty itself, from immediate intuitive beauty, but from the *judgment* about beauty, the unfortunately named judgment of taste. This is his problem. What particularly catches his attention is that such a judgment plainly reports an occurrence in the subject but claims a general validity as though it concerned a property of the object. That struck him, not beauty itself. He proceeds only from what others say, from the judgment about beauty, not from beauty itself. He seems almost to know it only by hearsay, not directly. A very intelligent blind person might likewise construct a theory of colors from detailed reports heard about them. Indeed, we can regard Kant's philosophical claims about beauty almost only in this light. Then we find his theory very ingenious, and there are occasionally apt and true general observations. But his actual solution to the problem is so inadmissible, so far beneath the dignity of its subject, that it cannot occur to us to take it for objective truth. I therefore consider myself excused from refuting it and refer once more to the positive part of my work.


As for the form of his whole book, we should note that it arose from the idea of finding the key to the problem of beauty in the concept of *purposiveness*. The idea is duly deduced—which, as Kant's successors have taught us, is never hard to do. Hence the bizarre combination of knowledge of beauty with knowledge of the purposiveness of natural bodies under *one* cognitive faculty, called *judgment*, and the treatment of these two heterogeneous subjects in a single book. These three cognitive powers—reason, judgment, and understanding—then provide material for various exercises in architectural symmetry. A taste for these is visible throughout the book: in the forced adaptation of its whole arrangement to that of the *Critique of Pure Reason*, and especially in the far-fetched antinomy of aesthetic judgment. One might also charge Kant with gross inconsistency. After repeatedly saying in the *Critique of Pure Reason* that the understanding is the faculty of judgment, and making the forms of its judgments the cornerstone of all philosophy, he now introduces a wholly separate faculty of judgment, entirely distinct from the understanding. What I call judgment, by contrast—the ability to carry intuitive knowledge over into abstract knowledge and then apply the latter correctly back to the former—is explained in the positive part of this work.

By far the finest thing in the Critique of Aesthetic Judgment is the theory of the sublime. It succeeds incomparably better than the theory of beauty. It offers not only the general method of inquiry, as that theory does, but takes a step along the right path. Though it does not provide the actual solution, it comes very close.

In the Critique of *Teleological* Judgment, the simplicity of the subject perhaps reveals more clearly than anywhere else Kant's strange talent for turning an idea this way and that, and stating it in many different ways, until it becomes a book. The whole book intends just this: although organized bodies necessarily appear to us as if composed according to a concept of their purpose that preceded them, this does not entitle us to assume that they objectively arose in this way. Our intellect receives things from outside and indirectly. It never knows their inner nature, through which they arise and continue to exist, but only their outer side. It can therefore make sense of a certain characteristic peculiar to organic products of nature only by analogy with works deliberately made by human beings, whose character is determined by a purpose and a concept of that purpose. This analogy suffices to make the agreement of their parts with the whole intelligible to us and even to guide our investigation of them. But it must on no account be made the real explanatory ground of the origin and existence of such bodies. The necessity of conceiving them in this way has a subjective origin. That, approximately, is how I would summarize Kant's teaching on this matter. He had already presented the essentials of it in the *Critique of Pure Reason*, pp. 692–702; V, 720–730. Yet in recognizing *this* truth too we find *David Hume* as Kant's distinguished predecessor. He had already sharply disputed the assumption in part two of his *Dialogues Concerning Natural Religion*. The main difference between Hume's critique and Kant's is that Hume criticizes the assumption as one based on experience, while Kant criticizes it as an *a priori* assumption. Both are right, and their accounts complement one another. Indeed, the essentials of Kant's teaching here are already expressed in Simplicius's commentary on Aristotle's *Physics*: ἡ δὲ πλάνη γέγονεν αὐτοῖς ἀπὸ τοῦ ἡγεῖσθαι, πάντα τὰ ἕνεκά του γινόμενα κατὰ προαίρεσιν γενέσθαι καὶ λογισμόν, τὰ δὲ φύσει μὴ οὕτως ὁρᾶν γινόμενα. (Latin: *Error iis ortus est ex eo, quod credebant, omnia, quae propter finem aliquem fierent, ex proposito et ratiocinio fieri, dum videbant, naturae opera non ita fieri.* “Their error arose from supposing that everything done for some end was done by choice and reasoning, while they saw that the works of nature did not come about in this way”; *Scholia in Aristotelem*, Berlin edition, p. 354.) Kant is entirely right about the matter. Once it had been shown that the concept of effect and cause cannot be applied to nature as a whole, in respect of its existence, it was also necessary to show that, in respect of its constitution, nature cannot be conceived as the effect of a cause guided by motives or concepts of purpose. Consider the great plausibility of the physico-theological proof, which even *Voltaire* took to be irrefutable. It was of the greatest importance to show that the subjective element in our apprehension—to which Kant had assigned space, time, and causality—extends also to our judgment of natural bodies. Thus the compulsion we feel to think of them as premeditated and produced according to concepts of purpose, on a path *where their representation preceded their existence*, has as subjective an origin as the intuition of space, which presents itself so objectively. It may therefore not be passed off as objective truth. Apart from its tiresome length and repetition, Kant's exposition of the matter is excellent. He rightly maintains that we shall never succeed in explaining the nature of organic bodies by merely mechanical causes, by which he means the unintended and lawful operation of all general forces of nature. Yet I find a gap here. He denies the possibility of such an explanation only with regard to the purposiveness and apparent design of *organic* bodies. But even where these are absent, we find that explanatory grounds cannot be carried from *one* domain of nature into another. As soon as we enter a new domain, the earlier grounds leave us, and new fundamental laws appear that we cannot hope to explain by those of the earlier domain. In the strictly mechanical domain, for instance, the laws of gravity, cohesion, rigidity, fluidity, and elasticity prevail. In themselves, setting aside my explanation of all natural forces as lower grades of the will's objectivation, they stand as manifestations of forces not further explicable. They themselves provide the principles of every further explanation there, which consists simply in reducing things to them. But when we leave this domain for the phenomena of chemistry, electricity, magnetism, or crystallization, those principles are wholly unusable. Those laws no longer apply, other forces overpower them, and the phenomena proceed in direct opposition to them according to new fundamental laws. Like the former, these are original and inexplicable: they cannot be reduced to more general laws. All the laws of strict mechanics, for example, will never explain even the dissolution of salt in water, let alone more complex chemical phenomena. I discussed all this at greater length in the second book of this work. An inquiry of this kind would, I think, have been very useful in the Critique of Teleological Judgment and would have shed much light on what it says. It would especially have served Kant's excellent suggestion that a deeper knowledge of the being in itself whose appearances are the things of nature would find one and the same ultimate principle in both the mechanical, lawful operation of nature and its apparently purposeful operation, a principle that could explain them both. By presenting the will as the real thing in itself I hope to have supplied such a principle. Accordingly, in our second book and its supplements, and especially in my work *On the Will in Nature*, insight into the inner essence of the apparent purposiveness and the harmony and concord of nature as a whole has perhaps become clearer and deeper. I have therefore nothing more to say about it here.

One should therefore not expect everything I have said about a subject to be gathered in one place.
