# §. 46.

It is plain that Laocoön does not cry out in the famous sculptural group. The recurring astonishment at this must arise because all of us would cry out in his position. Nature demands it too: under the most violent physical pain and sudden, extreme bodily anguish, any reflection that might lead to silent endurance is driven entirely from consciousness. Nature finds relief by crying out, expressing pain and anguish, calling for a rescuer, and frightening the attacker at once.

Winckelmann, too, missed the expression of a cry. In trying to justify the artist, however, he effectively made Laocoön a Stoic. Laocoön supposedly finds it beneath his dignity to cry out *secundum naturam* (according to nature), and adds to his pain the useless constraint of suppressing its expression. Winckelmann thus sees in him “the tested spirit of a great man struggling with torments and trying to suppress and confine within himself the expression of what he feels. He does not burst into a loud cry, as in Virgil, but only anxious sighs escape him,” and so forth (*Works*, vol. 7, p. 98; more fully in vol. 6, p. 104 ff.).

Lessing criticized this view in his *Laocoön* and amended it in the way mentioned above: he replaced the psychological reason with a purely aesthetic one, namely that beauty, the principle of ancient art, does not permit the expression of a cry. He adds another argument, that an immobile artwork must not depict a wholly fleeting state incapable of duration. Yet a hundred excellent figures refute this: they are captured in fleeting movements, dancing, wrestling, seizing, and so on. Indeed, Goethe, in the essay on Laocoön that opens the *Propyläen* (p. 8), regards the choice of just such a fleeting moment as necessary.

In our own day *Hirt* (*Die Horen*, 1797, tenth issue), reducing everything to the utmost truth of expression, decided that Laocoön does not cry out because he is already about to die of suffocation and can no longer do so. Finally, *Fernow* (*Roman Studies*, vol. 1, p. 426 ff.) discussed and weighed all three views. He offered no new one, however, but tried to reconcile and combine them.

I cannot help wondering that such thoughtful and discerning men laboriously sought inadequate explanations far afield, resorting to psychological and even physiological arguments for something whose reason is close at hand and immediately evident to an unprejudiced observer. I am especially surprised that Lessing came so near the right explanation without reaching the essential point.

Before any psychological or physiological inquiry into whether Laocoön would cry out in his situation—and I, for my part, would answer with an unqualified yes—we must decide something about the sculpture itself. The cry could not be represented in it for the simple reason that representing a cry lies entirely outside sculpture's domain. Marble could give us no crying Laocoön, only one with his mouth wide open, struggling vainly to cry out, with his voice stuck in his throat: `vox faucibus haesit` (his voice stuck in his throat). The essence of a cry, and hence its effect on a spectator, lies entirely in its sound, not in an open mouth. The opening of the mouth necessarily accompanies a cry, but it must first be motivated and justified by the sound it produces. Then, as characteristic of the action, it is permissible and even necessary, despite its cost to beauty.

In visual art, however, depicting the cry itself is impossible. It would be foolish to depict only the violent opening of the mouth, which disturbs every feature and the rest of the expression. We would see the means, which already demands considerable sacrifices, while its end—the cry itself and its effect on the mind—would be absent. Worse, the result would be the invariably ridiculous sight of an effort producing no effect. It would be like the prankster who plugged a sleeping night watchman's horn with wax, woke him by shouting “Fire!”, and delighted in watching his fruitless efforts to blow it.

Where the representation of a cry does lie within an art's domain, by contrast, it is entirely permissible, since it serves truth: the complete presentation of the Idea. Poetry does this by calling on the reader's imagination for intuitive presentation. Thus Virgil's Laocoön cries out like a bull that has broken loose after being struck by an axe. Homer likewise has Mars and Minerva cry out terribly (*Iliad*, XX, 48–53), without harm to their divine dignity or beauty. The same holds in acting. Laocoön onstage would certainly have to cry out. Sophocles has Philoctetes cry out, and on the ancient stage he surely did so in fact. I recall a very similar case: in London I saw the celebrated actor *Kemble* play Rolla, an American in *Pizarro*, a play translated from German. Rolla is half-savage but of very noble character. When wounded, he cried out loudly and violently. The effect was great and excellent, for the cry was deeply characteristic and added much to the truth of the portrayal.

A painted or sculpted silent screamer, on the other hand, would be even more ridiculous than painted music, already censured in Goethe's *Propyläen*. A cry does far more damage to the rest of the expression and to beauty than music does. Music usually occupies only the hands and arms and can be regarded as an action characterizing the person. In that respect it can quite properly be painted, so long as it requires no violent bodily movement or distortion of the mouth: witness St Cecilia at the organ, Raphael's violinist in the Galleria Sciarra in Rome, and others.

Because the limits of sculpture ruled out expressing Laocoön's pain by a cry, the artist had to bring every other expression of it into play. He did this to the highest perfection, as Winckelmann describes so masterfully (*Works*, vol. 6, p. 104 ff.). His excellent description therefore retains its full value and truth once we set aside the Stoic attitude he attributes to Laocoön.[^1]
