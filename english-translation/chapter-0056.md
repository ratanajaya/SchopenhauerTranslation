# §. 54.

The first three books have, I hope, brought us to clear and certain knowledge that, in the world as representation, the will has acquired a mirror in which it knows itself with increasing degrees of clarity and completeness. The highest degree is the human being, whose nature finds its full expression only in the connected sequence of actions. Reason makes their self-conscious connection possible by allowing a person always to survey the whole *in abstracto* (in the abstract).

Viewed purely in itself, the will is without cognition and is only a blind, irresistible drive. We still see it appearing in inorganic and plant nature and their laws, as well as in the vegetative part of our own lives. Through the world as representation, added to it and developed for its service, it gains knowledge of its own willing and of what it wills: nothing other than this world, life exactly as it is. This is why we called the world of appearance its mirror, its objecthood. What the will wills is always life, since life is simply the presentation of that willing in representation. Thus to say “the will to live” rather than “the will” is to say the same thing, and is merely a pleonasm.

The will is the thing in itself, the inner content and essence of the world, while life, the visible world, appearance, is only its mirror. Life will therefore accompany the will as inseparably as a shadow accompanies a body: wherever there is will, there will be life and world. Life is thus certain for the will to live. So long as we are filled with the will to live, we need not be anxious for our existence, even at the sight of death.

Certainly we see the individual arise and pass away. But the individual is only an appearance, existing only for cognition bound to the principle of sufficient reason and the *principium individuationis* (principle of individuation). For this cognition the individual receives life as a gift, comes from nothing, then loses that gift through death and returns to nothing. Yet we intend to regard life philosophically, according to its Ideas. We shall then find that neither the will, the thing in itself in every appearance, nor the subject of cognition, spectator of every appearance, is touched by birth or death.

Birth and death belong to the will's appearance, and thus to life. Life essentially presents itself in individuals that arise and pass away, fleeting appearances entering the form of time of something that knows no time in itself but must present itself thus to objectivate its true nature. Birth and death belong equally to life. They balance one another as mutual conditions, or, if one prefers the expression, as the poles of life's entire appearance.

Indian mythology, the wisest of all, expresses this by giving the god who symbolizes destruction and death—Shiva—the *lingam*, a symbol of generation, along with his necklace of skulls. Brahma, the most sinful and lowest god of the Trimurti, symbolizes generation and coming into being, while Vishnu symbolizes preservation. Generation thus appears as a counterweight to death, indicating that the two are essential correlates, mutually neutralizing and cancelling one another.

The Greeks and Romans were moved by precisely the same outlook when they decorated costly sarcophagi, as we still see, with festivals, dances, weddings, hunts, animal combats, and bacchanals: depictions of the most vigorous urge to live. They showed it not only in festivities but in sensual groups, even in unions between satyrs and goats. Plainly their aim was to point emphatically, from the death of the individual being mourned, to nature's immortal life. Without abstract knowledge, they thus intimated that all nature is the appearance and fulfilment of the will to live.

The form of this appearance is time, space, and causality. These bring about individuation and therefore require the individual to arise and pass away. But this affects the will to live, of whose appearance an individual is just one instance or specimen, no more than an individual's death injures nature as a whole. Nature cares for the species alone, not the individual. It presses with utmost seriousness to preserve the species, providing for it lavishly through a vast excess of seeds and the powerful urge to fertilize. The individual has no value for nature and cannot have it, since her realm comprises infinite time and space and thus an infinite number of possible individuals. She is always ready to let an individual fall. It is exposed to destruction in a thousand ways through the slightest accidents, and indeed is destined for it from the outset. Nature herself leads it toward death as soon as it has served to preserve the species.

Quite plainly nature thereby speaks the great truth that only the Ideas, not individuals, have true reality: they alone are the complete objecthood of the will. The human being is nature herself at the highest degree of her self-consciousness, and nature is only the objectivated will to live. Someone who has grasped this standpoint and remains with it can therefore rightly console himself for his own death and the deaths of friends by looking back to nature's immortal life, which is himself. We should understand Shiva with the *lingam* and the ancient sarcophagi in this way. Through their images of life at its most ardent, the sarcophagi call to the grieving viewer: `Natura non contristatur` (Nature does not grieve).

Generation and death belong to life and are essential to this appearance of the will. We can see this also because both are intensified expressions of what makes up all the rest of life. Life is nothing but a constant exchange of matter while form persists. This is precisely the transience of individuals and the permanence of the species. Continual nutrition and reproduction differ from generation only in degree, and continual excretion differs from death only in degree.

This is simplest and clearest in the plant. It is wholly a constant repetition of the same drive, of its simplest fibre grouped into leaf and branch. It is a systematic aggregate of similar plants supporting one another, whose sole drive is to reproduce themselves continually. By ascending the stages of metamorphosis, the plant finally reaches flower and fruit for the fullest satisfaction of this drive. The fruit is the compendium of its existence and striving. By a shorter path it now achieves its sole end, performing a thousandfold in one stroke what it had previously done individually: repeating itself. The plant's activity leading to fruit is related to the fruit as writing is to printing.

The animal plainly shows the same thing. Nutrition is continual generation; generation is nutrition raised to a higher power. Sexual pleasure is the intensified well-being of the feeling of life. Conversely, excretion—the continual breathing out and casting off of matter—is the same thing raised to a higher power as death, the opposite of generation. We are always content to retain the form without mourning the matter cast off. We should therefore respond in the same way when, at death, the same process occurs in a heightened form and as a whole that takes place daily and hourly in individual acts of excretion. As we are indifferent to the latter, we ought not recoil from the former.

From this standpoint, demanding the continuation of one's individuality, which other individuals replace, is as misguided as demanding that the matter of one's body remain, though it is constantly replaced by new matter. Embalming corpses seems as foolish as carefully preserving one's excretions. Individual consciousness bound to an individual body is completely interrupted by sleep every day. During its duration deep sleep does not differ from death, into which it often passes seamlessly—as when someone freezes to death. The difference lies only in the future, namely waking. Death is a sleep in which individuality is forgotten. Everything else wakes again—or rather, has remained awake.[^1]

Above all we must clearly recognize that the form of the will's appearance, thus the form of life or reality, is properly only the *present*, not the future or the past. These exist only in concepts, only in the connections made by cognition insofar as it follows the principle of sufficient reason. No human being has lived in the past, nor will anyone ever live in the future. The *present* alone is the form of all life. It is also life's secure possession and can never be taken away. The present and its content are always there, standing firm without wavering, like the rainbow over a waterfall. Life is sure and certain for the will, and the present for life.

When we think back over past millennia and the millions who lived in them, we ask: What were they? What became of them? But we need only recall our own past and vividly renew its scenes in imagination, then ask again: What was all that? What became of it? What holds for our past holds for the lives of those millions. Should we suppose the past gains a new existence because death has sealed it? Our own past, even the recent past of yesterday, is now only an empty dream of the imagination; so is the past of all those millions. What was? What is? The will, whose mirror is life, and cognition free of the will, which sees it clearly in that mirror.

Anyone who has not recognized this, or refuses to recognize it, must add another question to the one about the fate of past generations. Why is he, the questioner, so fortunate as to possess this precious, fleeting, sole real present, while hundreds of earlier generations, including their heroes and sages, have sunk into the night of the past and become nothing? Why does his insignificant self really exist? Or, more briefly, though strangely: why is this “now,” his now, *now*, and not long since *past*? In asking so strangely he treats his existence and his time as independent and imagines the former thrown into the latter. He effectively assumes two nows, one belonging to the object and one to the subject, and marvels at the fortunate accident of their meeting.

In truth, as shown in my essay on the principle of sufficient reason, only the point of contact between the object, whose form is time, and the subject, which has no form of the principle of sufficient reason, constitutes the present. Every object, however, is the will insofar as it has become representation, and the subject is the object's necessary correlate. Real objects exist only in the present. The past and future contain merely concepts and phantoms. Thus the present is the essential form of the will's appearance and cannot be separated from it. The present alone is always there and stands immovably firm. Under empirical consideration it is the most fleeting of all things. To the metaphysical gaze that looks beyond the forms of empirical intuition, it proves the only enduring thing: the Scholastics' `Nunc stans` (standing now, or eternal present). The source and bearer of its content is the will to live, the thing in itself—which we are.

Whatever continually becomes and passes away because it either has been or is yet to come belongs to appearance as such. Its forms make arising and passing away possible. So let us think: `Quid fuit? – Quod est. – Quid erit? – Quod fuit` (What was? What is. What will be? What was). Take the words strictly: understand not `simile` (the similar) but `idem` (the same). Life is certain for the will, and the present for life. Everyone can therefore say: “I am master of the present once and for all, and it will accompany me through all eternity like my shadow. I need not wonder where it has come from or how it happens to exist just now.”

We can compare time with a wheel turning endlessly. The half continually descending would be the past, the half continually rising the future; but at the top the indivisible point touching the tangent would be the present without extension. The tangent does not roll along with the wheel; neither does the present. It is the point of contact between the object, whose form is time, and the subject, which has no form because it does not belong to what can be known, but is the condition of everything knowable. Or time is like an irresistible stream and the present a rock against which it breaks without carrying the rock away.

The will as thing in itself is no more subject to the principle of sufficient reason than is the subject of cognition, which in a certain respect is ultimately the will itself or its expression. As life, its own appearance, is certain for the will, so is the present, the only form of actual life. We should therefore inquire neither into the past before life nor into the future after death. Instead we must recognize the *present* as the sole form in which the will appears to itself.[^2] The present will not escape the will, nor, indeed, will the will escape the present.

Anyone satisfied with life as it is, who affirms it in every way, may confidently regard it as endless. Such a person can banish the fear of death as an illusion suggesting the absurd fear that the present could ever be lost, or imagining a time with no present in it. With respect to time this illusion resembles another about space: each person imagines the place on earth he now occupies as “above” and all the rest as “below.” In the same way each connects the present to his own individuality, supposing that all present time would end with it, leaving a past and future without a present. Yet every point on earth is “above,” and the form of all life is *present*. To fear death because it takes the present away is no wiser than fearing that one might slide off the spherical earth on whose upper surface one happens fortunately to stand.

The present is essential to the will's objectivation. Like a point without extension, it cuts infinite time in both directions and stands still, an everlasting noon without a cooling evening. The actual sun likewise burns without cease and only seems to sink into night's lap. Hence a person fearing death as annihilation is like someone who imagines the sun lamenting at sunset: “Alas, I am sinking into eternal night!”[^3]

Conversely, consider someone burdened by life, who wants and affirms life but detests its torments, and in particular can no longer bear the harsh fate that has fallen to him. Such a person must not hope for release through death and cannot save himself by suicide. The dark, cool underworld lures him toward a harbour of rest only by a false appearance. The earth turns from day into night; the individual dies; but the sun itself burns on at eternal noon. Life is certain for the will to live, and life's form is a present without end, no matter how individuals, appearances of the Idea, arise and pass away in time like fleeting dreams. Suicide therefore already appears to us here as a futile and thus foolish act. Later in our inquiry it will appear in a still less favourable light.

Dogmas change and our knowledge is fallible, but nature does not err. Her course is sure, and she does not conceal it. Everything exists wholly within her, and she is wholly within everything. She has her centre in every animal. It found its way into existence securely and will as securely find its way out. Meanwhile it lives without fear of annihilation or anxiety, borne up by consciousness that it is nature herself and is as imperishable as she is. Only the human being carries certainty of death around in abstract concepts. Strangely, that certainty can trouble him only at particular moments, when some occasion brings it before his imagination. Reflection can do little against nature's powerful voice.

As in the animal that does not think, there prevails in the human being a lasting confidence arising from innermost consciousness that he is nature, the world itself. Consequently the thought of certain and never distant death does not markedly trouble anyone; each lives as though he would live forever. One might even say that no one has a truly vivid conviction of the certainty of his own death. Otherwise there could not be such a wide difference between his state of mind and that of a condemned criminal. Everyone admits this certainty *in abstracto* (in the abstract), theoretically, but sets it aside like other theoretical truths not applicable in practice, never taking it into living consciousness. Anyone who carefully observes this trait of the human mind will see that psychological explanations appealing to habit or acceptance of the inevitable are insufficient. Its reason lies deeper, as just stated.

This also explains why doctrines of some continuation of the individual after death are found and held in honour among every people in every age. The proofs for them have always had to be highly inadequate, while the proofs of the contrary are strong and numerous. Indeed, the contrary needs no proof: sound understanding recognizes it as a fact. This recognition is confirmed by confidence that nature neither lies nor errs, but openly displays and even ingenuously declares her conduct and nature. It is we ourselves who obscure them through delusion, interpreting them to suit our limited view.

We have now brought something else clearly to consciousness. An individual appearance of the will begins and ends in time, but neither the will itself as thing in itself nor the correlate of every object—the knowing subject, never itself known—is affected by this. Life is always certain for the will to live. This is not, however, to be counted among doctrines of personal survival. Considered as thing in itself, the will, like the pure subject of cognition, the eternal eye of the world, neither persists nor passes away. These are determinations valid only in time, whereas will and pure subject lie outside time.

The individual's egoism—the egoism of this particular appearance of the will illuminated by the subject of cognition—therefore gains no support or comfort for its wish to endure through infinite time from our view. It gains as little as it would from knowing that the rest of the external world will continue in time after its death. That is only the same view expressed objectively, hence under the form of time. Everyone is transient as appearance, but timeless and therefore without end as thing in itself. Yet only as appearance is one distinct from the other things in the world. As thing in itself each is the will that appears in everything. Death cancels the illusion separating one's consciousness from the consciousness of others: *this* is continuance. One's immunity to death belongs only to oneself as thing in itself. As appearance it coincides with the continuation of the rest of the external world.[^4]

The inward, merely felt consciousness of what we have just made explicit prevents the thought of death from poisoning the life even of a rational being. It is the basis of the courage to live that sustains every living thing and lets it go on briskly as though there were no death, so long as its eyes are on life. But this does not prevent the individual from being seized by the fear of death and trying by every means to escape when death approaches in an individual, actual case, or even in imagination, and it must look death in the face. So long as its cognition was directed toward life as such, it had to recognize life's imperishability. With death before it, however, it must recognize death for what it is: the temporal end of an individual, temporal appearance.

What we fear in death is by no means pain. Pain plainly lies on this side of death. We also often flee from pain toward death, just as, conversely, we sometimes accept the most terrible pain to escape death a little longer, even when death would be quick and easy. We distinguish pain and death as two quite different evils. What we actually fear in death is the destruction of the individual, which it openly announces. Since the individual is the will to live itself in one particular objectivation, its whole being resists death.

Where feeling abandons us helplessly in this way, reason can intervene and largely overcome its adverse impressions. It raises us to a higher standpoint from which we see the whole rather than the individual. A philosophical understanding of the world's essence that reached the point of our inquiry so far but went no further could already overcome the terrors of death to the extent that reflection had power over immediate feeling in a given person.

Imagine someone who had firmly incorporated the truths explained so far into his disposition but had not, through personal experience or deeper insight, come to recognize enduring suffering as essential to every life. Suppose he found satisfaction in life and felt wholly at ease in it. On calm reflection he would wish his life as he had known it to last endlessly or recur forever. His courage for life would be great enough for him gladly to accept all its hardships and pain in exchange for its pleasures. Such a person would stand “with firm, sturdy bones upon the well-rounded, enduring earth” and have nothing to fear. Armed with the knowledge we attribute to him, he would look indifferently at death approaching on the wings of time. He would regard it as a false appearance, a powerless spectre that frightens the weak but has no power over someone who knows himself to be the will whose objectivation or image is the whole world. Life, and with it the present—the true, sole form of the will's appearance—remains certain for him. Neither the infinite past nor the infinite future in which he is absent could frighten him: he sees them as the empty illusion and veil of Maya. He would have as little reason to fear death as the sun has to fear the night.

In the *Bhagavad Gita*, Krishna places his young pupil Arjuna at this standpoint. At the sight of the armies ready for battle, Arjuna is overcome with sorrow, somewhat as Xerxes was. He loses courage and wishes to withdraw from the fight to prevent the deaths of so many thousands. Krishna gives him the higher standpoint; the deaths of those thousands no longer stop him, and he signals the battle to begin. Goethe's Prometheus also expresses this standpoint, especially when he says:

> “Here I sit and fashion people<br>
> In my own image,<br>
> A race to be like me:<br>
> To suffer and to weep,<br>
> To enjoy and rejoice,<br>
> And to pay no heed to you,<br>
> As I do!”

Bruno's and Spinoza's philosophies could also lead someone to this standpoint, provided their errors and imperfections did not disturb or weaken his conviction. Bruno's has no ethics proper. The ethics in Spinoza's philosophy does not follow from the essence of his teaching; although praiseworthy and beautiful in itself, it is attached to it only by weak and obvious sophisms. Many people, finally, would occupy this standpoint if their cognition kept pace with their willing: if, free of every delusion, they could see themselves clearly. For cognition, this is the standpoint of the complete *affirmation of the will to live*.

The will affirms itself when its own essence is given to it completely and clearly as representation in its objecthood, the world and life, and this cognition in no way checks its willing. It wills the life thus known as such: until now blindly, as mere drive, and now with cognition, consciously and reflectively. The opposite, the *denial of the will to live*, occurs when willing ceases upon such cognition. Individual appearances known to it no longer act as *motives* for willing. Rather, the entire cognition of the world's essence that has grown from apprehending the *Ideas* mirrors the will and becomes its *quietive*. The will thus freely cancels itself.

These notions, unfamiliar and hardly intelligible in such general language, will, I hope, become clear in the account soon to follow of the phenomena—here, modes of action—in which affirmation at its several degrees and denial find expression. Both arise from *cognition*, but not from an abstract cognition expressed in words. It is a living cognition expressed only in deeds and conduct, independent of the doctrines that occupy reason as abstract knowledge. My sole aim is to present both and bring them to clear rational cognition, not to prescribe or recommend either. Such advice would be as foolish as it would be fruitless, since the will in itself is absolutely free and determines itself entirely on its own; there is no law for it. Before turning to this account, however, we must discuss and define this *freedom* and its relation to necessity more precisely. We must also make a few general observations about life—whose affirmation and denial are our problem—with reference to the will and its objects. This will help us understand the innermost ethical significance of different modes of action.

As I have said, this whole work unfolds a single thought. It follows that all its parts are most intimately connected. Each does not merely stand in a necessary relation to the part immediately preceding it and presuppose that part to be fresh in the reader's mind, as happens in philosophies consisting only of a sequence of deductions. Every part of the work is related to and presupposes every other. Readers must therefore remember not only what immediately precedes but everything earlier as well, so that they can connect it to the present point however much else intervenes. Plato made a similar demand on readers through the winding paths of his dialogues, which return to the principal thought only after long episodes, illuminating it thereby. Here the demand is necessary. Dividing our single thought into many inquiries was the only way to communicate it, but belongs not essentially to the thought itself; it is an artificial form.

The division into four chief viewpoints in four books, and the most careful linking of related and homogeneous matters, help the exposition and its understanding. The material nevertheless does not permit progress in a straight line, as a historical account does. It requires a more interwoven presentation and thus repeated study of the book. Only then does the connection of each part with every other become clear, and all the parts illuminate one another and become fully intelligible.[^5]
