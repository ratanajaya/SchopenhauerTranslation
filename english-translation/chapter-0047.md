The great task of history painting and sculpture, finally, is to present directly to intuition the Idea in which the will reaches the highest grade of its objectivation. Here the objective side of pleasure in beauty decidedly predominates; the subjective side recedes. At the next grade down, in animal painting, the characteristic and the beautiful are still wholly one: the most characteristic lion, wolf, horse, sheep, or bull is always the most beautiful one. The reason is that animals have only a species character, not an individual character. In depicting human beings, however, the character of the species separates from that of the individual. The former is now called beauty (in a wholly objective sense), while the latter retains the name character or expression. A new difficulty arises: presenting both perfectly in the same individual.

*Human beauty* is an objective expression for the most perfect objectivation of the will at the highest grade at which it can be known: the Idea of the human being in general, fully expressed in the form we behold. Yet however prominent the objective side of beauty is here, the subjective side always accompanies it. No object draws us into purely aesthetic intuition so quickly as a beautiful human face and figure. At their sight, an inexpressible pleasure immediately seizes us and lifts us above ourselves and everything that torments us. This is possible only because the will's clearest and purest knowability also brings us most easily and swiftly into the state of pure cognition. In that state our personality and our willing, with its constant pain, disappear for as long as purely aesthetic pleasure lasts. Thus Goethe says: “Nothing ill can touch one who beholds human beauty: one feels in harmony with oneself and the world.”

When nature succeeds in producing a beautiful human form, we must explain this as follows. As the will objectivates itself in an individual at this highest grade, favourable circumstances and its own strength enable it to overcome completely the hindrances and resistance posed by appearances of the will at lower grades. Among these are the natural forces from which it must continually wrest the matter that belongs to them all. Moreover, the will's appearance at higher grades always has manifold form. Even a tree is a systematic aggregate of countless repeatedly sprouting fibres. This composition grows increasingly complex at higher grades, and the human body is a highly combined system of very different parts. Each has a life of its own, *vita proprio* (its own life), subordinate to the whole. Beauty, the perfectly expressed character of the species, results only when all these parts are subordinated to the whole and coordinated with one another in just the right way, harmoniously working together to present the whole, with nothing excessive or stunted. These are rare conditions.

So much for nature. What, then, of art? It is said to imitate nature. But how could an artist recognize nature's successful work and pick it out among her failures without anticipating beauty *before experience*? Has nature, moreover, ever produced a human being perfectly beautiful in every part? Some have supposed that an artist must collect the beautiful parts dispersed among different people and assemble them into a beautiful whole. This is a confused and thoughtless opinion. Once again we must ask how the artist is to recognize that these forms are beautiful and those are not. We can also see how far imitation of nature brought the old German painters in beauty: just look at their nude figures.

Knowledge of beauty is altogether impossible *a posteriori* (from experience alone). It is always, at least in part, *a priori* (prior to experience), though of an entirely different kind from the forms of the principle of sufficient reason known to us *a priori*. Those forms concern the universal form of appearance as such, which grounds the very possibility of cognition: the universal, exceptionless *how* of appearing. Mathematics and pure natural science arise from such cognition. The other kind of *a priori* cognition, which makes the presentation of beauty possible, concerns the *content* rather than the form of appearances: the *what*, rather than the *how*, of appearing.

We all recognize human beauty when we see it. In the true artist this recognition is so clear that he shows beauty as he has never seen it, surpassing nature in his portrayal. This is possible only because *we ourselves* are the will whose adequate objectivation at its highest grade is to be judged and discovered here. For that reason alone we truly anticipate what nature—the very will constituting our own essence—is striving to present. In genuine genius this anticipation is accompanied by such a degree of reflective awareness that, on recognizing the *Idea* in an individual thing, the artist seems to *understand nature before she has finished speaking*. He then states clearly what she can only stammer. He stamps upon hard marble the beauty of form that eludes her in a thousand attempts, holds it up before her, and seems to cry, “This is what you meant to say!” The connoisseur echoes, “Yes, that was it!”

Only thus could the Greek genius discover the original type of the human figure and establish it as a canon for the school of sculpture. Only through such anticipation can any of us recognize beauty where nature has actually succeeded in an individual case. This anticipation is the *ideal*: the *Idea* insofar as it has been cognized, at least in part, *a priori* and, supplementing what nature gives *a posteriori*, becomes of practical use to art. The artist's capacity to anticipate beauty *a priori*, and the connoisseur's capacity to recognize it *a posteriori*, rest on the fact that artist and connoisseur are themselves nature's in-itself, the will objectivating itself. For, as Empedocles said, only like knows like: only nature can understand herself; only nature can fathom herself. But only spirit, too, can perceive spirit.[^1]

The mistaken[^2] view that the Greeks discovered their ideal of human beauty purely empirically, by selecting individual beautiful parts here and there—a knee in one place, an arm in another—has an exact parallel in poetry. This is the assumption that Shakespeare, for example, remembered from his own worldly experience the innumerable, varied, true, consistent, deeply worked out characters of his plays and then reproduced them. The impossibility and absurdity of this need no explanation. It is evident that genius produces works of visual art through an anticipatory intuition of beauty, and works of poetry through a like anticipation of what is characteristic. Both nevertheless need experience as a model through which what they know dimly *a priori* can be brought to full clarity, making deliberate presentation possible.

Human beauty was explained above as the most perfect objectivation of the will at the highest grade at which it can be known. It is expressed through form, which exists in space alone and has no necessary relation to time as movement does. To that extent we may say that the will's adequate objectivation through a merely spatial appearance is beauty in the objective sense. A plant is just such a merely spatial appearance of the will. No movement, and hence no relation to time, belongs to the expression of its nature (apart from its development). Its mere shape expresses and openly discloses its whole nature. An animal and a human being, by contrast, also need a series of actions to reveal fully the will appearing in them. Their appearance thereby acquires an immediate relation to time.

All this was discussed in the preceding book. It bears on the present discussion as follows. A merely spatial appearance of the will can objectivate it more or less perfectly at each particular grade; this constitutes beauty or ugliness. Likewise, the will's objectivation in time—action, and specifically immediate action or movement—can correspond purely and perfectly to the will objectivating itself in it. It can express just the particular act of will, with nothing foreign mixed in, nothing superfluous, and nothing lacking. Or it can do the opposite. In the first case, the movement has *grace*; in the second, it lacks grace. Beauty is thus the fitting presentation of the will in general through its merely spatial appearance. *Grace* is its fitting presentation through appearance in time: the perfectly correct and appropriate expression of each act of will in the movement and posture that objectivate it. Since movement and posture already presuppose a body, Winckelmann's formulation is very apt: “Grace is the distinctive relation of the acting person to the action” (*Works*, vol. 1, p. 258).

It follows that plants can be called beautiful, but graceful only figuratively. Animals and human beings can be both beautiful and graceful. Grace consists in each movement and posture being carried out in the easiest, most appropriate, and most effortless way, so that it expresses the intention or act of will exactly. It contains nothing superfluous, which appears as purposeless, meaningless fussing or a contorted posture, and lacks nothing, which appears as wooden stiffness. Grace requires as its condition a proper balance among all the limbs and a regular, harmonious bodily structure; only this makes perfect ease and evident fitness possible in every posture and movement. Grace is therefore never found without some degree of bodily beauty. Together and perfected, the two are the clearest appearance of the will at the highest grade of its objectivation.

As mentioned above, humanity is distinguished by the separation of species character and individual character. In the preceding book I said that every human being thus presents, in a certain sense, an entirely distinctive Idea. The arts whose purpose is to present the Idea of humanity therefore have another task beside beauty, the character of the species: they must also present the character of the individual, which is particularly called *character*. But they must do so only insofar as this is not regarded as something accidental, belonging exclusively to that one individual. Rather, it is a side of the Idea of humanity that emerges with particular prominence in that individual, whose depiction is therefore suited to revealing it.

Character, though individual, must accordingly be apprehended and represented ideally: its significance for the Idea of humanity as a whole, to whose objectivation it contributes in its own way, must be brought out. Otherwise the depiction is a portrait, a repetition of the individual as such with all its accidents. And even a portrait, as Winckelmann says, ought to be the ideal of the individual.

This *character*, ideally apprehended as the prominence of one particular side of the Idea of humanity, becomes visible partly in lasting physiognomy and bodily build, and partly in transient emotion and passion, in the mutual modification of knowing and willing. All this is expressed in facial expression and movement. The individual always belongs to humanity; conversely, humanity always reveals itself in an individual, even in that individual's distinctive ideal significance. Hence beauty must not be cancelled by character, nor character by beauty. If individual character abolishes the species character, the result is caricature; if the species character abolishes individuality, the result is insignificance.

Accordingly, a depiction chiefly seeking beauty, as sculpture does, will still modify that beauty—the species character—to some degree through individual character. It will always express the Idea of humanity in a particular individual way, bringing out a particular side of it. For the human individual as such has, in a sense, the dignity of an Idea of his own; and it is essential to the Idea of humanity that it present itself in individuals with distinctive significance. In ancient works we therefore find beauty, which the artists clearly apprehended, expressed not through a single figure but through many figures bearing different characters. They grasp it, as it were, from different sides: differently in Apollo, Bacchus, Hercules, and Antinous. Indeed, the characteristic may limit the beautiful and even emerge as ugliness, as in the drunken Silenus or the faun. If it goes so far as actually to abolish the species character and become unnatural, it turns into caricature.

Still less than beauty may grace be impaired by what is characteristic. Whatever posture or movement a character's expression demands, it must be performed in the way most appropriate, fitting, and easy for that person. This is observed not only by sculptors and painters but by every good actor. Otherwise caricature appears here too, in distortion and contortion.

In sculpture, beauty and grace remain paramount. The true character of the mind—shown in emotion and passion, in the interplay of cognition and will—can be presented only through expression of the face and gesture. It belongs above all to *painting*. Eyes and colour, which lie outside sculpture's domain, contribute much to beauty, but are still more essential to character. Moreover, beauty unfolds more fully when viewed from several standpoints, whereas expression or character can be grasped perfectly from *one* standpoint.

Since beauty is plainly sculpture's principal aim, *Lessing* tried to explain the fact that *Laocoön does not cry out* by saying that a cry is incompatible with beauty. This issue became the subject, or at least the starting point, of a book of his own, and much has been written on it both before and after him. I may therefore be allowed to give my opinion of it here as a digression, although such a particular discussion does not strictly belong to our inquiry, which is directed throughout toward the general.
