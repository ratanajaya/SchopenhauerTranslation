# §. 48.

Alongside beauty and grace, *history painting* takes character as a principal subject. By character I mean the presentation of the will at the highest grade of its objectivation. Here the individual, bringing out a particular side of the Idea of humanity, has a distinctive significance. This becomes known not through bodily form alone, but through every kind of action and the changes in cognition and willing that occasion and accompany it, visible in facial expression and gesture.

To present the Idea of humanity in this breadth, painting must show its many-sided development in significant individuals. These individuals in turn can become visible in their significance only through a variety of scenes, events, and actions. History painting meets this endless task by putting scenes from every kind of life before our eyes, whether their significance is great or small. No individual and no action can be devoid of significance: through all of them the Idea of humanity unfolds ever further. Hence no event of human life must be excluded from painting.

It is therefore very unjust to the excellent painters of the Dutch school to value only their technical skill while looking down on them because they mostly depicted ordinary life, as if only events from world history or biblical history were significant. We should begin by remembering that an action's *inner* significance is quite different from its *outer* significance, and that the two often occur separately. Outer significance is the importance of an action in relation to its consequences in and for the actual world: it is significance according to the principle of sufficient reason. Inner significance is the depth of insight it opens into the Idea of humanity. It brings to light sides of that Idea that seldom emerge by letting distinct and decisively expressive individuals unfold their peculiarities under appropriately arranged circumstances. Only inner significance matters in art; outer significance matters in history. The two are entirely independent. They can occur together, but either can also occur alone.

An action of the highest historical importance can be quite commonplace in its inner significance. Conversely, a scene from everyday life can be of great inner significance if it shows human individuals, their acting, and their willing clearly and brightly, down to their most hidden folds. Inner significance can even be the same where outer significance is very different. It makes no difference to it whether ministers dispute over countries and peoples above a map, or peasants argue over their rights in a tavern above playing cards and dice—just as chess is the same game with gold or wooden pieces. Moreover, the scenes and events that make up the lives of so many millions of people—their activities, distress, and joys—are already important enough to be subjects of art. Their rich variety must provide ample material for unfolding the many-sided Idea of humanity.

The transience of the moment fixed by art in such a picture (now called a *genre painting*) even stirs a quiet feeling of a special kind. Painting preserves in an enduring image the fleeting world, which constantly changes, through particular events that nevertheless stand for the whole. It seems thereby to bring time itself to a halt, raising the individual occurrence to the Idea of its kind.

Historical subjects of outward importance often have a disadvantage for painting: precisely what makes them important cannot be presented to intuition and must instead be supplied by thought. We must therefore distinguish a picture's *nominal* significance from its *real* significance. The former is its outer meaning, attached only as a concept. The latter is the side of the Idea of humanity revealed by the picture to intuition. Suppose, for example, the nominal subject is Moses found by the Egyptian princess, a moment of the greatest historical importance. Its real significance, what is actually given to intuition, is a foundling rescued from a floating cradle by a noblewoman—an event that might have happened often enough. Only the costume can identify the particular historical event for a learned viewer. Costume is relevant to nominal significance, however, and irrelevant to real significance, which knows only the human being as such, not arbitrary conventions.

Subjects taken from history have no advantage over subjects taken from mere possibility, which can be named generically rather than individually. What really matters in a historical subject is not the individual event as such but the universal element in it, the side of the Idea of humanity it expresses. This does not mean that specific historical subjects should be rejected. It means that the truly artistic view of them, for painter and viewer alike, is directed not to the individual particular that makes them historical but to the universal Idea expressed in them. Historical subjects should also be chosen only when the main thing can actually be depicted and need not merely be supplied by thought. Otherwise nominal and real significance move too far apart: what is merely thought about the picture becomes most important and detracts from what is seen. It is already a fault onstage when, as in French tragedy, the main event occurs behind the scenes. In a painting the fault is plainly greater still.

Historical subjects become decidedly harmful only when they confine the painter to a field chosen arbitrarily, for purposes other than art. This is especially so when the field is poor in painterly and significant subjects: for example, the history of a small, isolated, obstinate people in a corner of the world, governed by a hierarchy—that is, by delusion—and despised by the great contemporary peoples of East and West, as the Jews were. The migration of peoples lies between us and all ancient nations, just as an ancient change in the seabed lies between the present surface of the earth and the earlier one whose organisms appear to us only as fossils. It must therefore be regarded as a great misfortune that the people whose former culture was to serve chiefly as the foundation of our own were neither the Indians nor the Greeks nor even the Romans, but precisely the Jews. For the gifted Italian painters of the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries in particular, it was an ill fate that the narrow range to which they were arbitrarily confined in choosing subjects forced them to resort to wretched material of every kind. In its historical part the New Testament is almost still less favourable to painting than the Old, and the later history of martyrs and Church Fathers is an altogether unfortunate subject.

Yet we must sharply distinguish paintings whose subject is the history or mythology of Judaism and Christianity from those that reveal the true, ethical spirit of Christianity to intuition by depicting human beings filled with it. These, in fact, are painting's highest and most admirable achievements. Only its greatest masters, especially Raphael and Correggio—the latter particularly in his earlier paintings—have succeeded in them. Such paintings do not really belong among historical pictures. They usually present no event or action, but simply groups of saints, the Redeemer himself, often as a child, his mother, angels, and so on.

In their faces, especially their eyes, we see the expression and reflection of the most perfect cognition. It is directed not toward individual things but has wholly grasped the Ideas, and thus the whole nature of the world and life. Reacting upon the will in them, this cognition supplies it no *motives*, as ordinary cognition does. Instead, it has become a *quietive* of all willing. From it arise complete resignation—the innermost spirit of Christianity and of Indian wisdom alike—the abandonment of all willing, the turning back and cancellation of the will and with it the entire essence of this world: redemption. In their works these masters, worthy of eternal praise, gave intuitive expression to the highest wisdom. Here all art reaches its summit. It has followed the will in its adequate objecthood, the Ideas, through every grade: from the lowest, where causes move it, then where stimuli move it, and finally where motives move it in so many ways and unfold its nature. It ends by presenting the will's free self-cancellation through the one great quietive that arises for it from the most perfect cognition of its own nature.[^1]
