Morality without a foundation, mere moralizing, cannot work, because it supplies no motive. But a morality that *does* supply a motive can do so only by appealing to self-love, and what springs from self-love has no moral worth. It follows that morality and abstract cognition generally cannot produce genuine virtue. Virtue must arise from intuitive cognition that recognizes in another individual the same being as in oneself.

Virtue does arise from cognition, but not from abstract cognition communicable in words. If it did, it could be taught, and by stating here in abstract terms its nature and the cognition underlying it, we would morally improve everyone who understood us. This plainly does not happen. Ethical lectures and sermons can no more create a virtuous person than all works on aesthetics, beginning with Aristotle's, have ever made a poet. The concept is as unfruitful for virtue's real inner nature as for art. It can serve only as a subordinate tool in carrying out and preserving what has been otherwise known and resolved. *Velle non discitur* (“Willing is not learned”). Abstract dogmas in fact have no influence on virtue, that is, goodness of disposition: false ones do not spoil it, and true ones scarcely advance it. It would indeed be terrible if the chief concern of human life, its ethical worth valid for eternity, depended on something so much a matter of chance as acquiring dogmas, religious doctrines, or philosophical propositions. Dogmas have value for morality only in that a person already virtuous through another kind of cognition, to be discussed shortly, finds in them a scheme or formula. By it he gives his own reason an account, mostly fictitious, of his unselfish action, whose nature his reason—or he himself—does not *understand*. He has accustomed his reason to be satisfied with that account.

Dogmas can indeed powerfully influence *action*, outward conduct, as can habit and example. Example works because the ordinary person knows the weakness of his own judgment, distrusts it, and follows only his own or another's experience. Yet the disposition remains unchanged.[^1] Abstract cognition supplies only motives; motives, as shown above, can change only the direction of the will, never the will itself. All communicable cognition can affect the will only as a motive. Thus even when dogmas direct the will, what a person really and generally wills remains the same. He has acquired different ideas only about the means of obtaining it, and imaginary motives guide him just as real ones do. For instance, it makes no difference to his ethical worth whether he gives large sums to the needy because he firmly expects to receive tenfold in a future life or spends the same sum improving an estate, which will yield returns later but more securely and substantially. A person who in orthodox faith consigns a heretic to the flames is a murderer just like the bandit who murders for a reward. Depending on the inner circumstances, so is the person who slaughters Turks in the Promised Land, if he does it, like the first, because he thinks he will earn a place in heaven. They all seek their own advantage, their own egoism, as the bandit does, differing from him only in the absurdity of their means. As I said, motives are the only external means of reaching the will, but they change only how it expresses itself, never the will itself. *Velle non discitur* (“Willing is not learned”).

When a person appeals to dogmas to explain his good deeds, we must distinguish whether these were actually the motives or merely the apparent account with which, as I said, he tries to satisfy his reason. He may have done a good deed from quite another source simply because he *is good*, yet cannot properly explain it, since he is no philosopher and still wishes to think something about it. The distinction is very hard to discover, for it lies within the mind. We can therefore almost never judge others' actions morally with accuracy, and seldom our own. Dogmas, example, and habit can greatly modify the acts and conduct of an individual or a people. Yet all acts in themselves, *opera operata* (works performed), are mere empty images; only the disposition leading to them gives them moral significance. That disposition can remain exactly the same beneath widely different external appearances. People equally wicked may die, one on the breaking wheel and the other peacefully among his family. The same degree of wickedness may appear in one people in bold strokes as murder and cannibalism, and in another quietly and delicately *en miniature* (in miniature) as court intrigue, oppression, and every kind of subtle trick. The essence remains the same. Conceivably a perfect state, or even a wholly and firmly believed dogma about rewards and punishments after death, might prevent every crime. That would achieve much politically but nothing morally; it would merely obstruct the portrayal of the will through life.

Genuine goodness of disposition, unselfish virtue, and pure nobility of character do not spring from abstract cognition, then, but they do spring from cognition. This cognition is immediate and intuitive, cannot be argued away or argued into anyone, and, precisely because it is not abstract, cannot be communicated. It must dawn on each person for himself. Its proper and adequate expression lies not in words but only in deeds, action, and the course of a person's life. We seek a theory of virtue here and therefore must express the nature of its underlying cognition in abstract terms. Yet such an expression cannot deliver that cognition itself, only a concept of it. We must always begin from action, where alone it becomes visible, and refer back to action as its only adequate expression. We can only interpret it and state abstractly what actually takes place in it.

Before discussing *goodness* proper as the opposite of the *evil* already described, we must touch on a middle stage, the mere negation of evil: *justice*. We have adequately explained right and injustice above. We can therefore say briefly that a person is *just* if he voluntarily recognizes and honors the merely moral boundary between injustice and right even where no state or other power protects it. According to our account, he never carries affirmation of his own will so far as to deny the will appearing in another individual. He will not inflict suffering on others to increase his own well-being: he will commit no crime and will respect everyone's rights and property. We see that the *principium individuationis* is no longer an absolute partition for this just person as it is for the evil person. Unlike the latter, he does not affirm only his own appearance of will and deny all others, nor are other people mere masks of a wholly different essence to him. His conduct shows that he *recognizes again* his own essence, the will to live as thing in itself, in another's appearance, given to him only as representation. To a certain degree, then, he finds himself there: to the degree of not committing injustice, of not injuring others. To that extent he sees through the *principium individuationis*, Maya's veil. He treats the being outside him as equal to his own and does not injure it.

At the heart of this justice already lies a resolve not to carry affirmation of one's own will so far that it denies others' appearances of will by compelling them to serve it. One is therefore willing to provide to others as much as one receives from them. The highest degree of this just disposition, already joined with goodness proper, which is no longer merely negative, goes so far as to question one's right to inherited property. One wishes to sustain one's body only through one's own mental or physical powers, feels every service rendered by another and every luxury as a reproach, and finally embraces voluntary poverty. Thus *Pascal*, when he took an ascetic turn, would no longer tolerate servants attending him, though he had plenty. Despite his constant illness, he made his own bed and fetched his own food from the kitchen, and so forth (*Vie de Pascal par sa soeur*, p. 19; *Life of Pascal by His Sister*). Correspondingly, some Hindus, even rajas, are said to use their great wealth only to support their household, court, and servants, while observing with strict scruple the maxim of eating only what they have themselves sown and harvested. There is nevertheless a misunderstanding here. Precisely because an individual is rich and powerful, he can render services to society as a whole that equal the inherited wealth whose security he owes society. Strictly speaking, the excessive justice of these Hindus is already more than justice: it is real renunciation, denial of the will to live, asceticism, which we shall discuss at the end. Conversely, doing absolutely nothing and living by others' efforts on inherited property, without rendering anything in return, may already be considered morally unjust, though it must remain lawful under positive law.

We have found that voluntary justice originates inwardly in a certain degree of seeing through the *principium individuationis*, while the unjust person remains entirely captive to it. This insight can occur not only to the degree justice requires but to a higher degree, prompting positive benevolence, beneficence, and love of humanity. It can do so however strong and energetic the will appearing in such an individual may be. Cognition can always counterbalance it, teach resistance to the temptation to injustice, and even bring about every degree of goodness, indeed resignation. The good person should therefore by no means be taken as an originally weaker appearance of will than the evil person. Cognition masters the blind urge of will in him. Some individuals do seem good-natured merely because the will appearing in them is weak. What they really are soon shows itself in their inability to overcome themselves substantially in order to perform a just or good deed.

Suppose we meet the rare person with a considerable income who uses little of it for himself, gives the rest to those in need, and forgoes many pleasures and conveniences. In trying to understand his action, we may set aside the dogmas by which he might explain it to his own reason. Its simplest and most general expression, and its essential feature, is that he *makes less of a distinction between himself and others than is usual*. For some people that distinction is so great that another's suffering gives the malicious person immediate pleasure and the unjust person a welcome means of his own well-being; the merely just person stops at not causing it. Most people know of countless sufferings close to them, but will not resolve to relieve them because doing so would require some deprivation of their own. To each of these people a great gulf seems to lie between his own self and another's. For the noble person we have imagined, however, this difference is not so considerable. The *principium individuationis*, the form of appearance, no longer holds him as firmly captive. Others' suffering affects him almost as deeply as his own, and he therefore seeks to restore the balance, giving up pleasures and accepting deprivations to relieve it. He realizes that the difference between himself and others, so wide a gulf to the evil person, belongs only to a transient, deceptive appearance. Immediately, without inference, he recognizes that the in-itself of his own appearance is also that of another's: the will to live that constitutes the essence of every thing and lives in everything. Indeed, this extends to animals and all of nature. He will therefore torment no animal either.[^2]

He is now no more able to let others go hungry while he possesses what is superfluous and dispensable than anyone is able to go hungry for a day in order to have more on the next than he can consume. Maya's veil has become transparent to the person who practices acts of love, and the illusion of the *principium individuationis* has left him. He recognizes himself, his self and his will, in every being and therefore in the sufferer. He is free of the perversity by which the will to live, failing to recognize itself, enjoys fleeting, illusory pleasures in one individual and in exchange suffers and starves in *another*. It imposes torment and endures torment, not recognizing that, like Thyestes, it greedily devours its own flesh, and then laments undeserved suffering here and commits outrage there without fear of Nemesis. It does so only because it does not recognize itself in the other's appearance and therefore does not perceive eternal justice, captive to the *principium individuationis* and, generally, to the mode of cognition governed by the principle of sufficient reason. To be cured of this delusion and Maya's deception and to practice acts of love are one and the same. The latter is an unfailing sign of the former cognition.

The opposite of the torment of conscience explained above is *a good conscience*, the satisfaction we feel after every unselfish act. The act arises from immediately recognizing our own essence in itself in another appearance, and in turn confirms that cognition: our true self exists not only in our own person, this one appearance, but in everything that lives. This expands the heart, as egoism contracts it. Egoism concentrates all our concern on the single appearance of our own individual, while cognition constantly sets before us the innumerable dangers threatening it; anxiety and care thus become the underlying tone of our mood. Knowledge that everything living is just as much our own essence in itself as our person extends our concern to everything living and thereby expands the heart. As concern for one's own self diminishes, anxious care for it is attacked at its root and limited. Hence the calm, confident serenity given by a virtuous disposition and a good conscience, which comes out still more clearly after each good deed, as it confirms the basis of that mood to us. The egoist feels surrounded by alien and hostile appearances, and all his hope rests on his own welfare. The good person lives among friendly appearances; the welfare of each is his own. Knowledge of the human lot in general may keep his mood from becoming cheerful, but lasting knowledge of his own essence in all living things gives his mood a certain evenness and serenity. Concern spread over countless appearances cannot produce the anxiety of concern concentrated on *one*. The chances affecting individuals collectively balance out, whereas those affecting one individual bring fortune or misfortune.

Others have laid down moral principles as commands to virtue and laws that must be obeyed. I cannot do that, as I have said, since I have no “ought” or law to present to the eternally free will. Yet within my account something analogous to such a project is the purely theoretical truth of which this whole exposition may be seen as an elaboration: the will is the in-itself of every appearance and, as such, is free of the forms of appearance and therefore of multiplicity. In relation to action I know no worthier way to express it than the already mentioned Vedic formula, *Tat twam asi!*—“This is you!” Whoever can say this to himself about every being he meets, with clear knowledge and firm, heartfelt conviction, has thereby the assurance of every virtue and blessedness and is on the direct road to redemption.

Before I go further and show, as the last part of my account, how love—which we have found originates in seeing through the *principium individuationis*—leads to redemption, that is, to the complete surrender of the will to live and of all willing, and how another, less gentle but more common road takes people to the same end, I must state and explain a paradox. I do so because it is true and belongs to the completeness of the thought I am presenting: “All love (ἀγάπη, *caritas*—charity) is compassion.”

[^2]: The human right to use animals' lives and powers rests on this: suffering grows in proportion as consciousness becomes clearer.
