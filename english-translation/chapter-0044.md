# §. 42.

I return to our account of the aesthetic impression. Cognition of the beautiful always involves, together and inseparably, the pure knowing subject and the cognized Idea as its object. Yet the source of aesthetic enjoyment lies sometimes more in apprehending the Idea, and sometimes more in the bliss and peace of mind belonging to pure cognition, freed from all willing and thus from all individuality and the pain arising from it. Which element predominates depends on whether the Idea intuitively apprehended is a higher or lower grade of the will's objecthood.

Thus, when we contemplate the beauty of inorganic nature, plants, or works of fine architecture aesthetically—whether in reality or through art—the enjoyment of pure, will-less cognition will predominate. The Ideas apprehended here are only low grades of the will's objecthood, and their appearances do not have deep significance or a richly expressive content.

When animals and human beings are the objects of aesthetic contemplation or depiction, on the other hand, enjoyment lies more in the objective apprehension of their Ideas. These are the clearest revelations of the will. They display the greatest variety of forms, the richest and most deeply significant appearances, and reveal the will's nature to us most fully: in its vehemence, its terribleness, its satisfaction, and its being broken—the last in tragic representations. They reveal, finally, even its turning against itself or cancelling itself, which is especially the theme of Christian painting. History painting and drama, more generally, take as their object the Idea of the will illuminated by full cognition.

We shall now examine the arts one by one. This will bring completeness and clarity to the theory of the beautiful set out above.
