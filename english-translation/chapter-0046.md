# §. 44.

What the two arts just discussed achieve for the lowest grades of the will's objecthood is achieved, in a certain measure, for the higher grade of plant life by fine gardening. The scenic beauty of a place rests largely on the variety of natural objects assembled there, and then on their standing out clearly and distinctly while appearing in fitting connection and alternation. Fine gardening helps to meet these two conditions. But it is far less master of its material than architecture is of its own, so its effect is limited. Almost all the beauty it presents belongs to nature; gardening itself has contributed little. Conversely, it can do very little against nature's disfavour. Where nature works against it instead of helping it, its achievements are slight.

Insofar as the plant world, which offers itself everywhere for aesthetic enjoyment without art's intervention, becomes an object of art, it belongs chiefly to landscape painting. All the rest of nature that lacks cognition belongs there too. In still life, and in paintings of architecture alone—ruins, church interiors, and the like—the subjective side of aesthetic enjoyment predominates. Our pleasure lies chiefly not in directly apprehending the Ideas represented, but in the subjective correlate of that apprehension: pure, will-less cognition. As the painter lets us see these things through his eyes, we also share and feel anew the profound peace of mind and complete silence of the will needed to immerse cognition so thoroughly in lifeless objects and to apprehend them with such love, meaning here such a degree of objectivity.

The effect of landscape painting proper is generally similar. But because the Ideas represented, as higher grades of the will's objecthood, are already more significant and expressive, the objective side of aesthetic pleasure comes further forward and balances the subjective side. Pure cognition as such is no longer quite the main thing. The cognized Idea, the world as representation at a significant grade of the will's objectivation, acts on us with equal force.

Animal painting and sculpture reveal a still higher grade. Important ancient examples of the latter survive: horses in Venice and on the `Monte Cavallo` (Horse Hill), on the Elgin reliefs, and in Florence in bronze and marble; also in Florence the ancient boar and the howling wolves; then the lions at the Arsenal in Venice, and an entire room in the Vatican filled mostly with ancient animal sculptures, among others. In these representations the objective side of aesthetic pleasure decidedly outweighs the subjective side. The peace of the subject who cognizes these Ideas and has quieted his own will is present, as it is in every aesthetic contemplation, but we do not feel its effect. We are occupied instead by the restlessness and vehemence of the will represented.

The willing that constitutes our own nature comes before our eyes in shapes whose appearance, unlike ours, is not governed and softened by reflection. It shows itself in stronger features and with a clarity bordering on the grotesque and monstrous, but also without pretence: innocent, open, and plainly exposed. This is precisely the basis of our interest in animals. The character of the species already emerged in depictions of plants, but there it appeared only in forms. Here it becomes much more significant and is expressed not only in shape but also in action, posture, and gesture—though still only as the character of the species, not the individual.

We can also gain this cognition of Ideas at higher grades directly, without a painter's mediation, by purely contemplative intuition of plants and observation of animals, especially animals in their free, natural, and contented state. Objective contemplation of their varied, wonderful forms and their ways of acting is an instructive lesson from the great book of nature, a deciphering of the true `Signatura rerum` (signature of things).[^1] In them we see the many grades and modes in which the will is manifested. One and the same will is present in every being, willing everywhere the same thing, which objectivates itself as life, as existence, in an endless variety of different shapes. These are all adaptations to different external conditions, like many variations on a single theme. If we wished to tell the observer their inner essence in reflective thought and in a single phrase, we could best use the Sanskrit formula found so often in the sacred books of the Hindus and called the *Mahavakya* (great saying): `Tat twam asi` (That living being is you).

[^1]: Jakob Böhme says in his book *De Signatura Rerum*, chapter 1, §§ 15–17: “There is nothing in nature that does not also reveal its inner form outwardly, for what is within is always working toward revelation. ... Every thing has its mouth for revelation. ... This is nature's language, in which each thing speaks from its own quality and always reveals and presents itself. ... For every thing reveals its mother, *which thus gives the essence and the will* their form.”
