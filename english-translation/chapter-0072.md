Someone might think our account of what I call denial of the will, now complete, incompatible with the earlier explanation of the necessity governing motivation just as it governs every other form of the principle of sufficient reason. According to that account, motives, like all causes, are merely occasions on which character unfolds its nature and reveals it with the necessity of a natural law. We therefore absolutely denied freedom as *liberum arbitrium indifferentiae* (an indifferent freedom of choice). Far from revoking that denial, I recall it here. True freedom, independence of the principle of sufficient reason, belongs only to the will as thing in itself, not to its appearance, whose essential form everywhere is that principle, the element of necessity. There is only one case in which this freedom can become immediately visible in appearance: when it brings what appears to an end. Yet the mere appearance—the animate body as a link in the chain of causes—continues in time, which contains appearances alone. The will manifesting itself through this appearance therefore contradicts it, denying what it expresses. In such a case the genitals, the visible expression of sexual desire, exist and are healthy, yet no sexual satisfaction is willed, even inwardly. The whole body is the visible expression of the will to live, yet motives corresponding to that will no longer work. Indeed, dissolution of the body, the end of the individual and thus the greatest hindrance of the natural will, is welcomed and desired.

The apparent contradiction between our claim that motives necessarily determine the will in accordance with character and our claim that the will can be completely abolished, leaving motives powerless, is therefore only philosophy's reflective repetition of that *real* contradiction. It arises when the freedom of the will in itself, which knows no necessity, directly intervenes in the necessity of its appearance. The key to reconciling the claims is that the condition in which character is withdrawn from the power of motives does not come immediately from the will, but from a changed mode of cognition. So long as cognition remains captive to the *principium individuationis* (principle of individuation) and follows the principle of sufficient reason unreservedly, motives have irresistible force. But when one sees through the *principium individuationis* and immediately knows the Ideas and even the essence of things in themselves as the same will in everything, that knowledge becomes a universal quietive of willing. Individual motives then lose their effect, because the mode of cognition corresponding to them has retreated and been eclipsed by an entirely different one. Character can therefore never change in part. In individual cases it must carry out, with the consistency of a natural law, the will of which it is an appearance as a whole. Yet that whole, character itself, can be abolished completely through the change in cognition just described. Claudius called this the “comprehensive, transcendental change” and marveled at it. It is aptly called *rebirth* in the Christian Church, and the cognition from which it springs was called the *action of grace*. We speak of character's complete abolition rather than a change in it. This is why people whose characters differed widely before it occurred show great similarity of conduct afterward, though each still *speaks* very differently according to his concepts and dogmas.

In this sense the old philosophical doctrine of free will, forever disputed and forever affirmed, is not groundless. Nor is the Church's doctrine of grace and rebirth without meaning. Unexpectedly, the two now coincide, and we can understand the sense in which the excellent Malebranche could rightly say, *La liberté est un mystère* (“Freedom is a mystery”). What Christian mystics call the *action of grace* and *rebirth* is for us the only immediate expression of the *freedom of the will*. It occurs only when the will, having come to know its essence in itself, receives from that knowledge a *quietive* and is thereby withdrawn from the effect of *motives*. These belong to another mode of cognition, whose objects are appearances alone. The possibility of freedom expressing itself in this way is humanity's greatest advantage, forever denied to animals. It requires reason's reflective awareness, which enables a survey of life as a whole independently of the present impression. An animal has no possibility of freedom. Indeed, it cannot even make a genuine, considered choice after a full conflict among motives, which would need to be abstract representations. A hungry wolf sinks its teeth into its prey with the same necessity as a stone falls to the ground, without any possibility of knowing that it is both the creature torn apart and the one doing the tearing. *Necessity is the realm of nature; freedom is the realm of grace.*

As we have seen, this *self-abolition of the will* arises from cognition. Cognition and insight as such are independent of deliberate choice. Denial of willing, the entrance into freedom, therefore cannot be forced by a resolution. It springs from the innermost relation of cognition to willing in a person, arriving suddenly, as though from outside. That is precisely why the Church called it the *action of grace*. Yet just as the Church makes its operation depend on receiving grace, the quietive's effect is ultimately an act of freedom by the will. After this action of grace, the person's whole being is changed and turned about from its foundations. He wants none of what he formerly wanted so fiercely; a new person, as it were, takes the old one's place. The Church therefore called the result *rebirth*. What it calls the *natural person*, denying him every capacity for good, is the will to live itself. That will must be denied if redemption from an existence such as ours is to be attained. Something else lies behind our existence, accessible to us only when we shake off the world.

Christian doctrine does not consider individuals according to the principle of sufficient reason, but the Idea of humanity in its unity. It therefore symbolizes *nature*, the *affirmation of the will to live*, in *Adam*. His sin inherited by us, that is, our unity with him in the Idea, represented in time through the bond of procreation, makes us all partake of suffering and eternal death. In contrast it symbolizes *grace*, *denial of the will*, and *redemption* in God become human. Free of all sinfulness, that is, of all will to live, he cannot have arisen, as we have, from the strongest affirmation of the will, nor have a body that is through and through concrete will, an appearance of will. Born of a pure virgin, he has only an apparent body. This last is the doctrine of the Docetists, certain Church Fathers who were very consistent on this point. Apelles especially taught it, opposed by Tertullian and his followers. Even Augustine, however, comments on Romans 8:3, *Deus filium suum misit in similitudinem carnis peccati* (“God sent his son in the likeness of sinful flesh”): *Non enim caro peccati erat, quae non de carnali delectatione nata erat: sed tamen inerat ei similitudo carnis peccati, quia mortalis caro erat* (“It was not sinful flesh, since it was not born of carnal pleasure; yet it had the likeness of sinful flesh, since it was mortal flesh”) (*Liber 83 quaestionum*, question 66). In his *Opus imperfectum*, I, 47, Augustine also teaches that original sin is both sin and punishment. It is present even in newborns, though it manifests only when they grow up. Its origin must nevertheless be traced to the will of the sinner. That sinner was Adam, but we all existed in him: Adam became unhappy, and in him we all became unhappy.

In fact, the doctrines of original sin, the affirmation of the will, and redemption, its denial, are the great truth at Christianity's core. Most of the rest is clothing, covering, or accessory. Jesus Christ should accordingly always be understood generally as the symbol or personification of denial of the will to live, rather than as an individual, whether on the basis of his mythical story in the Gospels or of the probable historical reality behind it. Neither account will readily satisfy completely. They serve only as vehicles of that general understanding for the people, who always demand something factual. The fact that Christianity has lately forgotten its true meaning and declined into shallow optimism is not our concern here.

Another original evangelical Christian teaching was defended by Augustine, with the Church leaders' agreement, against the platitudes of the Pelagians. *Luther* made cleansing it of errors and restoring it the chief aim of his efforts, as he expressly says in *De servo arbitrio* (*On the Bondage of the Will*). It teaches that the *will is not free*, but originally subject to an inclination to evil; its works are therefore always sinful and defective, never adequate to justice. Consequently salvation comes not from works at all but from faith alone, and even this faith arises not from deliberate choice and free will but comes to us through the *action of grace*, without our doing, as though from outside. Not only the doctrines already mentioned but this last genuinely evangelical doctrine is among those now rejected as absurd, or concealed, by a crude and shallow view. Despite Augustine and Luther, that view favors the Pelagians' homely common sense, today's rationalism. It declares obsolete precisely the profound doctrines most distinctive and essential to Christianity in the strict sense, while retaining only the doctrine from Judaism, attached to Christianity by historical circumstance,[^1] and making that its chief concern.

We, however, recognize in the doctrine just described a truth wholly agreeing with the result of our inquiry. Genuine virtue and holiness of disposition arise first not from considered choice, or works, but from *cognition*, or faith, just as we derived them from our central thought. If works arising from motives and deliberate resolutions led to salvation, virtue would always be merely a prudent, methodical, far-sighted egoism, however one put it. The faith to which the Christian Church promises salvation says that through the first human's fall we all partake of sin and are given over to death and ruin, and that all can be redeemed only through grace and the divine mediator's assumption of our immense guilt, wholly without merit on our part as persons. What can arise from a person's intentional action determined by motives—works—can never justify us. By its very nature it cannot, precisely because it is *intentional* action prompted by motives, an *opus operatum* (a work performed).

This faith thus says, first, that our condition is originally and essentially without salvation and we need *redemption* from it. Second, we ourselves essentially belong to evil and are so firmly bound to it that our works according to law and precept, that is, according to motives, can never satisfy justice or redeem us. Redemption can be won only through faith, a changed mode of cognition, and even that faith can come only through grace, as though from outside. This means that salvation is something entirely foreign to our person and points to the need for that very person's denial and surrender. Works, obedience to law as such, can never justify, since they are always action on motives. *Luther* demands in *De libertate Christiana* (*On Christian Liberty*) that, once faith has arrived, good works arise from it of themselves as its signs and fruits. They must not claim merit, justification, or reward for themselves, but occur wholly freely and without payment. Similarly we derived from an increasingly clear insight through the *principium individuationis* first freely chosen justice, then love up to the complete abolition of egoism, and finally resignation or denial of the will.

I have drawn on these Christian doctrines, foreign as they are in themselves to philosophy, only to show that the ethics arising from and closely agreeing with all parts of our inquiry, however new and unheard of its expression might be, is by no means new in substance. It fully agrees with Christianity's most characteristic doctrines and was already essentially contained in them. It agrees just as closely with the teachings and ethical precepts of India's sacred books, expressed in entirely different forms. Recalling Church doctrine also explained and clarified the apparent contradiction between the necessity of every expression of character when motives are presented, the realm of nature, and the freedom of the will in itself to deny itself and abolish character together with all the necessity of motives grounded in it, the realm of grace.

[^1]: How true this is becomes clear from the fact that all the contradictions and incomprehensibilities contained in the Christian dogmatics systematized consistently by Augustine, which led to the opposite Pelagian shallowness, disappear as soon as we set aside the fundamental Jewish dogma and recognize that the human being is the work of his own will, not of another's. Everything then becomes clear and correct at once. No freedom is needed in *operari* (acting), for it lies in *esse* (being), where sin too lies as original sin; the action of grace, however, is our own. Under today's rationalist view, many doctrines in Augustinian dogmatics based on the New Testament, such as predestination, appear wholly untenable, even offensive. Their rejection discards what is truly Christian and returns to crude Judaism. Yet the error in calculation, the original defect in Christian dogmatics, lies where no one looks: in precisely the premise removed from scrutiny as certain. Once it is set aside, the whole dogmatics is rational. For that dogma spoils theology as it spoils every other science. Studying Augustine's theology in *De civitate Dei* (*The City of God*), especially book 14, is like trying to make an object stand when its center of gravity lies outside it: turn and place it as one likes, and it keeps toppling over. Despite all Augustine's efforts and sophistry, the guilt of the world and its torments likewise keep falling back on the God who made everything, made all things in all, and knew how they would turn out. I have already shown in my prize essay on freedom of the will (chapter 4, pp. 66–68 of the first [and second] edition) that Augustine himself noticed the difficulty and stumbled over it. The conflict between God's goodness and the world's misery, as well as that between free will and God's foreknowledge, was likewise the inexhaustible subject of a nearly century-long controversy among the Cartesians, Malebranche, Leibniz, Bayle, Clarke, Arnauld, and others. The one dogma all sides held fixed was God's existence and attributes. They turned in circles without end, trying to reconcile these things, to solve an equation that never works out: its remainder appears first here, then there, after being concealed elsewhere. No one thinks to seek the source of the difficulty in the fundamental premise itself, although it is obvious. Only Bayle gives a hint that he notices it.
