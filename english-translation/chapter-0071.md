Nothing differs more from denial of the will to live, which has now been adequately explained within the limits of our approach, than *suicide*, the actual abolition of one of the will's individual appearances. Denial is the one act of the will's freedom that enters appearance and thus, as Claudius calls it, the transcendental change. Far from being denial of the will, suicide is a phenomenon of its strong affirmation. For denial consists in abhorring life's pleasures, not its sufferings. The person who kills himself wants life; he is merely dissatisfied with the conditions on which it has come to him. He therefore gives up not the will to live but only life, by destroying an individual appearance. He wants life, the unhindered existence and affirmation of the body, but the entanglement of circumstances prevents it and causes him great suffering. The will to live finds itself so constrained in this one appearance that it cannot unfold its striving. It accordingly decides in keeping with its nature in itself, outside the forms of the principle of sufficient reason and thus indifferent to any particular appearance. It remains untouched by all arising and passing away and is the inner life of everything.

The same firm inward certainty that lets us all live without constant dread of death—the certainty that the will can never lack its appearance—also supports the suicidal act. The will to live thus appears as much in self-killing (Shiva) as in the satisfaction of self-preservation (Vishnu) and the pleasure of procreation (Brahma). This is the inner meaning of the *unity* of the *Trimurti*, which every person wholly is, though in time he displays first one and then another of its three heads. Suicide stands to denial of the will as an individual thing stands to an Idea: the person who kills himself denies only the individual, not the species. We found above that life is always assured to the will to live and suffering is essential to life. Suicide is the voluntary destruction of one appearance while the thing in itself remains undisturbed, as a rainbow stands fast though the drops that support it for moments change rapidly. It is therefore a futile and foolish act.

Moreover, it is Maya's masterpiece, the most blatant expression of the will to live's conflict with itself. We recognized that conflict in the lowest appearances of will, in the constant struggle among expressions of natural forces and among organic individuals for matter, time, and space. We saw it emerge ever more terribly at higher grades of the will's objectivation. Finally, at the highest grade, the human Idea, it reaches the point where not only individuals presenting the same Idea destroy one another, but the very same individual declares war on himself. He wills life so fiercely and assails its hindrance, suffering, so forcefully that he destroys himself. The individual will abolishes the body, its own visibility, by an act of will rather than let suffering break the will. Precisely because the person who kills himself cannot cease willing, he ceases living. The will here affirms itself by abolishing its appearance, since it can no longer affirm itself otherwise.

Yet the suffering he thus evades could have mortified the will and led him to denial and redemption. In this respect he resembles a sick person who refuses to complete a painful operation already begun that could cure him thoroughly, preferring to keep the disease. Suffering approaches and opens the possibility of denying the will, but he wards it off by destroying the will's appearance, the body, so that the will remains unbroken. This is why almost all philosophical and religious ethical systems condemn suicide, though they can give only oddly sophistical reasons. If someone has ever refrained from suicide from a purely moral impulse, the innermost meaning of that self-conquest, whatever concepts his reason used for it, was this: “I will not evade suffering, so that it can help abolish the will to live whose appearance is so wretched. It can strengthen the knowledge of the world's true nature already dawning on me until that knowledge finally quiets my will and redeems me forever.”

It is well known that from time to time a parent kills children he dearly loves and then himself. Conscience, religion, and every inherited concept tell the father that murder is the gravest crime, yet he commits it in the hour of his own death, without any egoistic motive for doing so. This can be explained only because the individual's will immediately recognizes itself in the children. Captive, however, to the illusion that appearance is the essence in itself and deeply struck by knowledge of the misery of all life, he thinks he can abolish the essence along with its appearance. He thus wants to rescue himself and the children in whom he sees himself living again from existence and its misery. An exactly analogous error would be to imagine that one could achieve through preventing conception what voluntary chastity achieves, or even to hasten a newborn's death in view of life's inevitable suffering instead of doing everything to secure the life of each being pressing into it.

For if the will to live exists, no force can break it as the one metaphysical reality, the thing in itself. Force can only destroy its appearance here and now. The will itself can be abolished only through *cognition*. Thus the sole path to salvation is to allow the will to appear unhindered so that in that appearance it can *know* its own essence. Only as a result of such knowledge can the will abolish itself and end the suffering inseparable from its appearance. Physical violence—the destruction of an embryo, the killing of a newborn, or suicide—cannot do this. Nature leads the will into the light precisely because only in the light can it find redemption. Nature's purposes must therefore be advanced in every way once the will to live, her inner essence, has made its decision.

A special kind of voluntary death, perhaps not yet sufficiently established, seems wholly different from ordinary suicide: death by starvation freely chosen in the highest degree of asceticism. Its occurrence has always been attended by much religious enthusiasm and even superstition, obscuring it. It seems possible, however, for complete denial of the will to reach a degree where even the will needed to preserve the body's vegetative life by taking nourishment falls away. Far from arising from the will to live, a completely resigned ascetic ceases living only because he has ceased willing altogether. No manner of death other than hunger is readily conceivable here, unless it arose from some particular superstition. An intention to shorten the torment would already be a degree of affirmation of the will. The dogmas filling such a penitent's reason present him with the illusion that a higher being commanded the fasting to which his inward inclination drives him.

Older examples appear in the *Breslau Collection of Natural and Medical Histories*, September 1799, p. 363 ff.; Bayle's *Nouvelles de la république des lettres* (*News of the Republic of Letters*), February 1685, p. 189 ff.; Zimmermann's *On Solitude*, vol. 1, p. 182; and a report by Houttuyn in the *Histoire de l'académie des sciences* (*History of the Academy of Sciences*) for 1764, reprinted in the *Collection for Practical Physicians*, vol. 1, p. 69. Later reports appear in Hufeland's *Journal for Practical Medicine*, vol. 10, p. 181, and vol. 48, p. 95; Nasse's *Journal for Psychiatric Physicians*, 1819, issue 3, p. 460; and the *Edinburgh Medical and Surgical Journal*, 1809, vol. 5, p. 319. All the newspapers reported in 1833 that the English historian Dr. Lingard had voluntarily starved himself to death in Dover that January; later accounts said it was not he but a relative. These reports generally call the people insane, and it is now impossible to establish how far that was true. But I will reproduce a more recent report, if only to preserve one of the rare examples of this striking and extraordinary phenomenon of human nature. It at least appears to belong in this category and would otherwise be hard to explain. The report appeared in the *Nürnberger Korrespondent* of July 29, 1813:

> From Bern it is reported that a hut has been discovered in a thick wood near Thurnen. It contained the body of a man dead and decomposing for about a month, dressed in clothes that gave little indication of his station. Two very fine shirts lay nearby. The most important object was a Bible with blank pages bound in, some written on by the dead man. He records the day of his departure from home, though not his place of origin, and then says he was driven by the spirit of God into a wilderness to pray and fast. He had already fasted for seven days on his journey, then eaten again. After settling there he began to fast once more, for a certain number of days. Each day is then marked with a stroke. There are five strokes, after which the pilgrim presumably died. A letter addressed to a pastor concerning a sermon he had heard from him was also found, but it too lacked an address.

There may be many intermediate degrees and mixtures between voluntary death arising from the extreme of asceticism and ordinary suicide arising from despair. These are hard to explain, but the human mind has depths, darknesses, and intricacies that are exceedingly difficult to illuminate and disentangle.
