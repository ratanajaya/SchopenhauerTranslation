We have found *two inseparable elements* in aesthetic contemplation: cognition of the object, not as an individual thing but as a Platonic *Idea*, the enduring form of this whole kind of thing; and the knowing subject's consciousness of itself, not as an individual but as the *pure, will-less subject of cognition*. Both arise together when we leave behind the mode of cognition bound to the principle of sufficient reason, the only mode suited to the service of the will and to science. We shall see that the *pleasure* aroused by contemplating beauty likewise springs from these two elements, with one or the other contributing more according to the object contemplated.

All *willing* arises from need, and thus from lack and suffering. Fulfilment ends a particular suffering, but for every wish fulfilled at least ten remain unsatisfied. Desire lasts a long time, and its demands have no end; fulfilment is brief and scant. Even the satisfaction finally attained is only apparent. A fulfilled wish gives way at once to another: the first was an error we recognized, the second an error we have yet to recognize. Nothing attained as an object of willing can provide lasting satisfaction. It is like alms thrown to a beggar: they keep him alive today only to prolong his misery until tomorrow.

So long as our consciousness is filled with our will, so long as we are given over to the pressure of desires and their constant hope and fear, we are subjects of willing and can find neither lasting happiness nor rest. Whether we pursue or flee, fear misfortune or seek enjoyment, makes essentially no difference. Care for the ever-demanding will, whatever form it takes, continually fills and stirs consciousness; and without rest no true well-being is possible. The subject of willing thus lies forever on Ixion's turning wheel, draws water in the Danaids' sieve, and thirsts eternally like Tantalus.

But an outward occasion or an inward mood can suddenly lift us out of the endless current of willing. Cognition escapes its servitude to the will; attention is no longer directed toward motives for willing, but apprehends things apart from their relation to the will. We contemplate them without interest or subjectivity, purely objectively, absorbed in them as representations rather than as motives. Then the peace we always sought along the path of willing, and which always fled from us, arrives of itself, and we feel entirely at ease. This is the painless state Epicurus praised as the highest good and the condition of the gods. For that moment we are freed from the wretched pressure of the will. We keep a Sabbath from the prison labour of willing, and Ixion's wheel stands still.

This is precisely the state described above as necessary for cognition of the Idea: pure contemplation, absorption in intuition, losing oneself in the object and forgetting all individuality. The mode of cognition that follows the principle of sufficient reason and apprehends only relations is suspended. At once and inseparably, the individual thing contemplated rises into the Idea of its kind, while the knowing individual becomes the pure subject of will-less cognition. As such, neither stands any longer in the current of time or in any other relation. It then makes no difference whether one watches the sunset from a prison or a palace.

An inward disposition, in which cognition has the upper hand over willing, can bring about this state in any surroundings. The admirable Dutch painters show this. They directed a purely objective gaze toward the most insignificant objects and left, in their *still lifes*, an enduring record of their objectivity and peace of mind. The aesthetic viewer cannot look at such a painting without being moved: it brings to mind the artist's calm, quiet, will-free state, which was needed to see such humble things so objectively, observe them so attentively, and reproduce that intuition with such care. As the picture invites the viewer into the same state, the feeling may be intensified by contrast with the viewer's own restless mood, clouded just then by intense willing. Landscape painters, especially Ruisdael, have often painted equally unremarkable landscapes in the same spirit, with an even more pleasing effect.

So much can be achieved by the inner power of an artistic mind alone. Yet a purely objective disposition is helped and encouraged from outside by receptive objects, above all by the abundance of beautiful nature, which invites, even presses, us to contemplate it. When it suddenly opens before our gaze, it almost always succeeds, if only for moments, in drawing us away from subjectivity and the will's servitude into the state of pure cognition. Thus a person tormented by passion, need, or care can be refreshed, cheered, and restored by one free look at nature. The storm of passion, the pressure of desire and fear, and all the torment of willing are at once wonderfully quieted.

For the moment we are detached from willing and given over to pure, will-less cognition, it is as if we had entered another world, in which everything that moves our will and shakes us so violently no longer exists. This freeing of cognition removes us from all of it as completely as sleep and dreams do. Happiness and unhappiness disappear. We are no longer the individual, who has been forgotten, but only the pure subject of cognition: the *one* eye of the world that looks out through every knowing being, yet can become fully free of the will's service only in a human being. Every difference of individuality then vanishes so thoroughly that it makes no difference whether the seeing eye belongs to a powerful king or a tormented beggar. Neither good fortune nor misery crosses that boundary.

At every moment, then, a realm lies close at hand in which we escape all our misery. But who has the strength to remain there long? As soon as some relation between the objects contemplated and our will, our person, returns to consciousness, the spell ends. We fall back into cognition governed by the principle of sufficient reason. We no longer cognize the Idea, but the individual thing, a link in a chain to which we too belong, and we are given back to all our misery.

Most people almost always remain at this standpoint because they largely lack objectivity, that is, genius. For this reason they do not like to be alone with nature; they need company, or at least a book. Their cognition remains in the will's service, so they look in objects only for a possible relation to their will. When they find none, a steady, desolate refrain sounds within them like a bass note: “It is no use to me.” Thus even the most beautiful surroundings, in solitude, take on a barren, dark, alien, hostile look for them.

Finally, the blessedness of will-less contemplation is also what casts such a strange spell over the past and over distant places, presenting them to us in such flattering light through a self-deception. When we recall days long past in a faraway place, imagination brings back only the objects, not the subject of willing that carried its incurable sufferings then just as it does now. Those sufferings are forgotten because so many others have since taken their place. Objective intuition acts in memory as it would in the present if we could bring ourselves to surrender to it free of willing. Thus, especially when some distress weighs on us more heavily than usual, a sudden memory of scenes from the past or far away passes before us like a lost paradise.

Imagination recalls only what is objective, not the individually subjective part. We imagine that these objects then stood before us as purely and as free of relation to the will as their images do now in memory. In fact, their relation to our willing caused us torment then, just as it does now. We can escape all suffering through present objects just as well as through distant ones, if we rise to contemplate them purely objectively. We then produce the impression that these objects alone are present and we ourselves are not. Freed from our burdensome self, we become, as the pure subject of cognition, entirely one with those objects; their indifference to our distress becomes, in those moments, our own. Only the world as representation remains. The world as will has vanished.

I hope these reflections have made clear the nature and extent of the *subjective* condition of aesthetic pleasure: cognition's release from service to the will, forgetfulness of oneself as an individual, and the elevation of consciousness to the pure, will-less, timeless subject of cognition, independent of all relations. This subjective side of aesthetic contemplation always has its necessary correlate in an objective side: intuitive apprehension of the Platonic Idea. Before examining that side more closely and considering what art accomplishes in relation to it, we should remain a little longer with the subjective side. We can complete its treatment by explaining the impression of the *sublime*, which depends on this side alone and arises from a modification of it. Our account of aesthetic pleasure will then be completed by considering its objective side.

First, a few further remarks belong to what has gone before. Light is the most delightful of things. It has become the symbol of everything good and healing. In every religion it signifies eternal salvation, while darkness signifies damnation. Ormuzd dwells in purest light, Ahriman in eternal night. Dante's Paradise looks rather like the Vauxhall in London: all the blessed spirits appear as points of light arranged in regular figures. The absence of light saddens us directly; its return makes us happy. Colours give immediate, lively delight, most of all when they are transparent.

This is because light is the correlate and condition of the most perfect mode of intuitive cognition, the only one that does not immediately affect the will at all. Unlike the other senses, sight cannot, through its mere sensory effect on the organ, produce pleasure or displeasure *as sensation*. It has no immediate link to the will. Only the intuition arising in the understanding can have such a link, and this lies in the object's relation to the will. Hearing is already different: sounds can directly cause pain or give sensory pleasure, even apart from harmony or melody. Touch, being one with the feeling of the whole body, is still more subject to this direct influence on the will, although there is also touch without pain or pleasure. Smells are always pleasant or unpleasant, and tastes still more so. These last two senses are therefore most entangled with the will; accordingly they have always been regarded as the least noble, and Kant called them the subjective senses.

Joy in light is thus joy in the objective possibility of the purest and most perfect intuitive cognition. Its source is that pure cognition, released from all willing, is deeply gratifying in itself and already makes a large contribution to aesthetic enjoyment. This account of light also explains the astonishing beauty we find in objects reflected in water. The lightest, swiftest, finest way in which bodies act on one another—the action of reflected rays of light, to which we owe by far the most perfect and purest of our perceptions—is here presented to our eyes clearly, completely, and on a large scale, in both cause and effect. Hence our aesthetic pleasure in it. For the most part, this pleasure has its root in the subjective ground of aesthetic enjoyment: delight in pure cognition and in its ways.[^1]
