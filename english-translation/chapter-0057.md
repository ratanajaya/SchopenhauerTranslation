The will as such is *free*, as follows already from our view that it is the thing in itself, the content of every appearance. Appearances, by contrast, are subject throughout to the principle of sufficient reason in its four forms. Necessity is simply the relation of a consequent to a given ground; the two are reciprocal concepts. Everything belonging to appearance—that is, everything that is an object for a subject knowing as an individual—is therefore a ground in one respect and a consequent in another. As a consequent it is necessarily determined throughout and cannot in any respect be otherwise than it is. All of nature and all its appearances are thus wholly necessary. We can demonstrate the necessity of every part, appearance, and event by finding the ground on which it depends as a consequent. There is no exception; this follows from the unrestricted validity of the principle of sufficient reason.

Yet we also know this same world in all its appearances as the objecthood of the will. The will itself is no appearance, representation, or object, but the thing in itself. It is therefore not subject to the principle of sufficient reason, the form of every object, and is not determined as a consequent by a ground. It knows no necessity: it is *free*. Freedom is thus properly a negative concept. Its content is merely the denial of necessity, the relation of consequent to ground governed by the principle of sufficient reason. Here the point of unity in that great opposition, the union of freedom and necessity, lies most clearly before us. This has often been discussed recently but, so far as I know, never clearly or properly.

Every thing as appearance, as object, is necessary throughout. That same thing *in itself* is will, wholly free for all eternity. Appearance is necessarily and unalterably determined in the unbroken chain of grounds and consequents. Yet the existence of the object in general and the kind of existence it has—the Idea revealed in it, or, in other words, its character—are immediate appearances of the will. In keeping with the will's freedom, the object might not exist at all, or might originally and essentially be quite different. In that case the entire chain of which it is a link, itself an appearance of the same will, would also be different. Once it exists, however, it has entered the series of grounds and consequents. It is always necessarily determined within it and can therefore neither become something else, that is, change, nor leave the series, that is, disappear.

Like every other part of nature, the human being is objecthood of the will, so everything just said applies to him. Each thing in nature has forces and qualities that respond in definite ways to definite influences and make up its character. A human being likewise has a *character*, from which motives necessarily elicit actions. His empirical character reveals itself in this mode of action, and his intelligible character in turn reveals itself in the empirical character. The latter is the will in itself, of which he is the determinate appearance.

But the human being is the most complete appearance of the will. As shown in Book Two, it could exist only if illuminated by such a high degree of cognition that cognition could even reproduce the world's essence in a fully adequate way under the form of representation: the apprehension of Ideas, the pure mirror of the world, which we met in Book Three. In the human being, therefore, the will can reach complete self-consciousness, a clear and exhaustive cognition of its own essence as reflected in the whole world. The actual presence of this degree of cognition gives rise to art, as we saw in the preceding book.

At the end of our inquiry we shall find that the same cognition can also make the will cancel and deny itself in its most complete appearance when it turns that cognition upon itself. Freedom, which otherwise belongs only to the thing in itself and can never appear, then emerges even in appearance. It cancels the essence underlying the appearance while the appearance itself continues in time. This produces a contradiction within appearance and thereby presents the phenomena of holiness and self-denial. All this can be fully understood only at the end of this book. For now it indicates in general how human beings differ from all other appearances of the will. Freedom—independence from the principle of sufficient reason—belongs only to the will as thing in itself and contradicts appearance. Yet in a human being it can possibly enter appearance, necessarily showing itself there as a contradiction within appearance. In this sense not only the will in itself but even the human being can be called free and distinguished from all other beings. What this means will become clear only from what follows; for now we must set it aside.

First we must prevent the mistake of thinking that an individual person's actions lack necessity, as though the force of a motive were less certain than that of a cause or than a conclusion following from premises. Apart from the exceptional case just mentioned, the freedom of the will as thing in itself does not pass directly to its appearance, even at its highest grade of visibility: the rational animal with an individual character, or the person. A person is never free, although the appearance of a free will. For the person is the already determined appearance of that will's free willing. Entering the form of every object, the principle of sufficient reason, it develops the unity of willing into a multitude of actions. Yet because willing in itself is one and outside time, those actions appear with the regularity of a natural force.

Nevertheless, it is that free willing that becomes visible in the person and his whole conduct, related to it as a concept is to its definition. Every individual act must therefore be attributed to the free will and announces itself immediately to consciousness as such. Hence, as noted in Book Two, each person holds himself free *a priori* (here, in his original feeling) even in individual actions, believing that any action is possible to him in a given case. Only *a posteriori* (after experience), by experience and reflection on it, does he recognize that his actions arise entirely of necessity from the meeting of character with motives. This is why even the crudest person, following feeling, vehemently defends complete freedom in individual actions, while the great thinkers of every age, and even the more profound religious doctrines, have denied it.

Once someone understands that a human being's whole essence is will and that he is only an appearance of it, while the principle of sufficient reason is the necessary form of appearance, knowable even from the subject's standpoint and here taking the form of the law of motivation, he will no more doubt that an act follows inevitably from a given character and motive than he will doubt that a triangle's angles sum to two right angles. Priestley has demonstrated the necessity of individual action very adequately in his *Doctrine of Philosophical Necessity*. Kant, whose achievement here is especially great, was the first to demonstrate how this necessity coexists with the freedom of the will in itself, outside appearance.[^1] He distinguished intelligible from empirical character, a distinction I retain completely. Intelligible character is the will as thing in itself insofar as it appears at a definite grade in a definite individual. Empirical character is that appearance itself as it shows itself in conduct over time, and already in bodily organization in space.

The clearest way to understand their relation is the formulation used in the introductory essay: each person's intelligible character should be regarded as an act of will outside time, thus indivisible and unchangeable. Empirical character is its appearance unfolded and spread out in time, space, and all the forms of the principle of sufficient reason, as experience shows it in a person's conduct and life course. The whole tree is only the constantly repeated appearance of a single drive, appearing most simply in the fibre and repeated recognizably in combinations as leaf, stem, branch, and trunk. Likewise all a person's deeds are the repeatedly expressed intelligible character, varying somewhat in form. An induction from their sum gives us his empirical character. I shall not rework and repeat Kant's masterly account here; I assume it is known.

In 1840 I treated the important question of freedom of the will thoroughly and at length in my prize-winning essay on it. In particular, I uncovered the source of the illusion that makes people think they find in self-consciousness an empirically given absolute freedom of the will, a `liberum arbitrium indifferentiae` (freedom to choose indifferently), as a fact of consciousness. The prize question had perceptively focused on just this point. I refer readers to that essay and to § 10 of my prize essay on the basis of morality, published with it under the title *Two Fundamental Problems of Ethics*. I now omit the still imperfect account of the necessity of acts of will given here in the first edition. Instead I shall briefly clarify the illusion just mentioned. This clarification presupposes chapter 19 of our second volume and could therefore not be given in the prize essay.

The will as the true thing in itself is genuinely original and independent. A feeling of originality and one's own power must therefore accompany its acts in self-consciousness, even though these acts are already determined there. Apart from that, the appearance of an *empirical* freedom of the will—a freedom of individual acts—in place of the *transcendental* freedom that alone belongs to it arises from the intellect's separate and subordinate position relative to the will. This was explained in chapter 19 of the second volume, especially under point 3.

The intellect learns the will's decisions only *a posteriori*, empirically. When faced with a choice, therefore, it has no data telling it how the will will decide. The intelligible character, by virtue of which only *one* decision is possible and necessary under given motives, is not available to the intellect's cognition. It learns the empirical character only successively through individual acts. To knowing consciousness, the intellect, two opposed decisions thus seem equally possible for the will in a given case. This is like saying of an upright rod that has lost its balance and begun to waver, “It can fall to the right or to the left.” That *can* has only a subjective meaning: “as far as our data show.” Objectively, the direction of the fall is necessarily determined as soon as the wavering begins.

Similarly, a decision of one's own will is undetermined only for its observer, one's own intellect. It is undetermined only relatively and subjectively, for the subject of cognition. In itself and objectively, the decision is determined and necessary as soon as any choice arises. Only the resulting decision brings this determination into consciousness. We even have empirical evidence of this when faced with a difficult, important choice that depends on a condition not yet fulfilled but merely hoped for. We can do nothing for the present and must remain passive. We then consider what we will decide once circumstances permit action and decision.

Far-sighted rational reflection usually favours one decision, while immediate inclination favours the other. So long as we must remain passive, reason seems likely to prevail, yet we foresee how strongly the other side will pull once there is an opportunity to act. Meanwhile we strive, through cool consideration of the *pro et contra* (for and against), to bring the opposing motives into the clearest light. We want each to act with its full force on the will when the moment arrives, so that an error by the intellect does not lead the will to decide differently than it would if everything acted upon it equally. This clear development of opposed motives is all the intellect can do in a choice. It awaits the actual decision passively, with the same tense curiosity with which it would await a stranger's decision. From its standpoint both decisions must appear equally possible. This is precisely the appearance of empirical freedom of the will.

The decision enters the intellect's sphere empirically, as the matter's eventual outcome. Yet it has arisen with complete necessity from the inner constitution, the intelligible character, of the individual will in conflict with given motives. The intellect can do no more than illuminate the motives fully and sharply from every side. It cannot determine the will itself, which is wholly inaccessible to it and, as we have seen, inscrutable.

If a person could act one way at one time and another way at another time under identical circumstances, the will itself would have had to change in between, and so would have to lie in time, where alone change is possible. Either the will would then be a mere appearance, or time would be a determination of the thing in itself. The dispute over freedom in individual action, over `liberum arbitrium indifferentiae` (indifferent freedom of choice), thus turns on whether the will lies in time. If, as Kant's teaching and my whole account require, it is the thing in itself, outside time and every form of the principle of sufficient reason, then an individual must always act the same way in the same situation. Each evil act is a firm guarantee of countless others that the person *must* commit and *cannot* omit. As Kant says, if the empirical character and the motives were given completely, a person's future conduct could be calculated like an eclipse of the sun or moon.

Character is as consistent as nature. Every individual act must come from it as each natural phenomenon comes from natural law. A cause in the latter case and a motive in the former are only occasional causes, as Book Two showed. The will, whose appearance is a person's entire being and life, cannot deny itself in a particular case. What the person wills as a whole, he will also always will in the particular.

The claim of empirical freedom of the will, a `liberum arbitrium indifferentiae`, is closely connected with placing human nature in a *soul* conceived as originally a *knowing* being, indeed an abstractly *thinking* one, and only consequently a *willing* being. This makes the will secondary when in truth cognition is secondary. The will was even treated as an act of thought and identified with judgment, particularly by Descartes and Spinoza. On that view, a person becomes what he is only in consequence of his *cognition*. He arrives in the world a moral zero, cognizes things there, and then decides to be one sort of person or another and to act in one way or another. New cognition could lead him to a new mode of conduct and make him a different person again. He would also first cognize something as *good* and then will it, whereas he actually first *wills* it and consequently calls it *good*.

According to my whole fundamental view, all this reverses the true relation. Will is first and original; cognition is only added as an instrument belonging to the will's appearance. Each person is what he is through his will, and his character is original, for willing is the basis of his being. Through the cognition added to it, he learns from experience what he is: he comes to know his character. He *knows* himself as a consequence of and in accord with the constitution of his will. On the old view, by contrast, he *wills* as a consequence of and in accord with his cognition. He would need only consider *how* he would most like to be, and he would be that: this is its freedom of the will. It means that a person is his own work in the light of cognition. I say, however, that he is his own work before all cognition; cognition merely comes afterward to illuminate it. He cannot decide to be this or that kind of person, nor can he become someone else. He *is* once and for all and learns successively *what* he is. On their view he *wills* what he knows; on mine he *knows* what he wills.

The Greeks called character `ἦθος` (*ethos*), and its expressions, customs, `ἤθη`. The word derives from `ἔθος` (habit). They chose it to express the constancy of character metaphorically through the constancy of habit. Aristotle says: `Τὸ γὰρ ἦθος ἀπὸ τοῦ ἔθους ἔχει τὴν ἐπωνυμίαν. ἠθικὴ γὰρ καλεῖται διὰ τὸ ἐθίζεσθαι` (`a voce` ἦθος `i. e. consuetudo` ἦθος `est appellatum: ethica ergo dicta est` ἀπὸ τοῦ ἐθίζεσθαι, `sive ab assuescendo`): character is named from habit, and ethics from becoming accustomed (*Magna Moralia*, I, 6, p. 1186; *Eudemian Ethics*, p. 1220; *Nicomachean Ethics*, p. 1103, Bekker edition). Stobaeus quotes the followers of Zeno: `οἱ δὲ κατὰ Ζήνωνα τροπικῶς· ἦθός ἐστι πηγὴ βίου, ἀφ’ ἧς αἱ κατὰ μέρος πράξεις ῥέουσι` (`Stoici autem, Zenonis castra sequentes, metaphorice ethos definiunt vitae fontem, e quo singulae manant actiones`—the Stoics, following Zeno, metaphorically define character as the source of life from which individual actions flow; II, chapter 7).

In Christian doctrine we find predestination resulting from the election and rejection of souls (Romans 9:11–24). It plainly arose from the insight that a human being does not change: his life and conduct, his empirical character, merely unfold the intelligible character. Firm and unchangeable dispositions, already discernible in a child, develop throughout life. Conduct seems therefore to be fixed essentially from birth to death. We agree with this. I will not, however, defend the consequences of joining this sound insight to doctrines found in Jewish religion. That union has produced the greatest difficulty, an eternally insoluble Gordian knot around which most of the Church's disputes turn. Even the apostle Paul can hardly have succeeded with the potter analogy he devised for the purpose, for its result would still be no more than:

> “Let humanity fear the gods!<br>
> They hold dominion<br>
> In eternal hands<br>
> And can wield it<br>
> As they please.”

Such matters, however, are foreign to our subject. It will be more useful to examine the relation between character and cognition, in which all its motives lie.

Motives determine action, the appearance of character, by acting upon it through cognition. Cognition can change. It often fluctuates between error and truth but is generally corrected more and more as life proceeds, though to very different degrees in different people. A person's conduct can thus change noticeably without justifying the inference that his character has changed. What the person wills in general, the striving of his innermost being and the goal pursued in accord with it, can never be changed by instruction or external influence. Otherwise we could remake him. Seneca says excellently, `velle non discitur` (willing cannot be taught), preferring truth to his fellow Stoics, who taught `διδακτὴν εἶναι τὴν ἀρετήν` (`doceri posse virtutem`—virtue can be taught).

The will can be affected from outside only by motives. But they can never change the will itself. They have power over it only on the assumption that it is exactly as it is. All they can do is change the direction of its striving, making it seek what it unchangeably seeks by a different path. Instruction and improved cognition, as external influences, can teach a person that he has mistaken the means. They can cause him to pursue the end to which his inner nature is already directed by a quite different route or even in a quite different object. They can never make him truly will something other than he has willed before. He remains unchanged, for he is nothing but that willing, which would otherwise have to be cancelled.

Cognition and therefore action are modifiable to a remarkable extent. A person may seek his unchanging end—Mohammed's paradise, for example—first in the actual world and then in an imagined one. He measures his means accordingly, using prudence, force, and deceit in the first case, abstinence, justice, alms, and pilgrimage to Mecca in the second. His striving itself has not changed, still less has he. His actions may therefore look very different at different times while his willing remains quite the same. `Velle non discitur` (willing cannot be taught).

For a motive to be effective it must not merely exist; it must also be *known*. As the Scholastics aptly put it, `causa finalis movet non secundum suum esse reale; sed secundum esse cognitum` (a final cause moves not according to its real being, but according to its being known). If the relation between egoism and compassion in a particular person is to emerge, it is not enough for him to possess wealth and see others' distress. He must know what wealth can do both for himself and for others; suffering must be presented to him, but he must also know what suffering and pleasure are. Perhaps he knew all this less well on a first occasion than on a second. If he acts differently under what seems the same occasion, the circumstances were actually different in the part that depended on his cognition of them.

Just as ignorance deprives real circumstances of their effect, imagined circumstances may act like real ones, not only in a single deception but generally and persistently. Suppose someone is firmly persuaded that every benefit he bestows will be repaid a hundredfold in a future life. Such a belief works like a secure bill payable at a very distant date. He can give out of egoism just as, with different beliefs, he would take out of egoism. He has not changed: `velle non discitur` (willing cannot be taught).

Because cognition influences action so greatly while the will remains unchanged, character develops only gradually and its different traits emerge over time. It therefore appears different at different ages. A wild and violent youth may be followed by a settled and moderate maturity. The evil in a character in particular may become more pronounced with time. Sometimes, however, passions indulged in youth are later restrained voluntarily, simply because opposing motives have only now entered cognition. This is also why all of us are innocent at first: neither we nor others yet know the evil of our own nature. It emerges only with motives, and motives enter cognition only with time. In the end we come to know ourselves as quite different from what we took ourselves to be *a priori*, often to our own horror.

*Remorse* never arises because the will has changed, which is impossible, but because cognition has changed. I must still will the essential and genuine content of whatever I have ever willed, for I myself am this will, outside time and change. I can therefore never regret what I willed, but can regret what I *did*: guided by false concepts, I acted otherwise than my will called for. Seeing this through more accurate cognition is *remorse*. This applies not only to prudence in life, the choice of means, and judging whether an end suits my real will, but also to ethics proper.

I may, for example, have acted more selfishly than accords with my character because I was misled by an exaggerated view of my own need or of another's cunning, dishonesty, or wickedness. Or I may have acted hastily, without deliberation, moved not by motives clearly known *in abstracto* (in the abstract) but by merely intuitive ones: the immediate impression and the emotion it aroused, so strong that I could not properly use my reason. When reflection returns, cognition is corrected and remorse may follow. It always shows itself by making amends so far as possible. We should note, though, that people deceive themselves by arranging an apparent act of haste that was secretly considered in advance. No one receives our subtler tricks of deceit and flattery than we ourselves do.

The reverse case can also occur. Excessive trust in others, ignorance of the relative worth of life's goods, or an abstract doctrine in which I no longer believe may have led me to act less selfishly than accords with my character, causing a different kind of remorse. Remorse is always corrected cognition of the relation between a deed and the true intention. When the will reveals its Ideas in space alone, through mere form, it encounters resistance from matter already ruled by other Ideas, natural forces. Matter seldom allows the form striving for visibility to emerge completely pure and distinct, that is, beautiful. In an analogous way, when the will reveals itself in time alone, through deeds, it encounters an obstacle in cognition. Cognition seldom supplies the data quite correctly, so a deed fails to correspond exactly to the will and prepares remorse. Remorse thus always arises from corrected cognition, never from a change in the will, which is impossible.

The anguish of conscience over a deed is something quite different from remorse. It is pain at knowing oneself as one is in oneself, that is, as will. It rests precisely on certainty that one still has the same will. Were the will changed, making anguish of conscience mere remorse, that anguish would cancel itself: a past act could no longer trouble us because it would express a will no longer belonging to the person who regrets it. We shall examine the significance of anguish of conscience more fully below.

Cognition, as the medium of motives, influences not the will itself but its emergence in action. This establishes the chief difference between human and animal action, for the modes of cognition differ. Animals have only intuitive representations; through reason, humans also have abstract representations, concepts. Animals and humans are determined by motives with equal necessity. Yet humans have a complete *capacity to choose* that animals lack. This is often mistaken for freedom of the will in individual actions, though it is merely the possibility of a fully fought-out conflict among several motives, the strongest of which then necessarily determines the person.

For such deliberation the motives must take the form of abstract thoughts. Only thus can there be a genuine weighing of opposed grounds for action. An animal can choose only among motives intuitively present, restricting its choice to the narrow sphere of its current intuitive apprehension. The necessity with which a motive determines the will, equal to that with which a cause determines its effect, can therefore be displayed immediately to intuition only in animals: the observer sees motives and effect directly together. Human motives, by contrast, are almost always abstract representations unavailable to an observer. Even to the acting person their conflict conceals the necessity of their operation. Only *in abstracto* can several representations, as judgments and chains of inference, lie together in consciousness and act on one another free of any fixed temporal order until the strongest overcomes the rest and determines the will.

This is the complete *capacity to choose*, or power of deliberation, that distinguishes humans from animals. It led people to attribute freedom of the will to human beings. They supposed that willing resulted merely from operations of the intellect, without a determinate drive beneath it. In truth, motivation acts only on the basis of and presupposing such a determinate drive, which in a human being is individual: a character. A fuller account of the power of deliberation and the difference it makes between human and animal choice appears in *Two Fundamental Problems of Ethics* (first edition, p. 35 ff.; second edition, p. 33 ff.), to which I refer readers.

This power of deliberation also makes human existence much more tormented than animal existence. Our greatest pains generally lie not in the present, as intuitive representations or immediate feeling, but in reason, as abstract concepts and tormenting thoughts. Animals, living only in the present and therefore in enviable freedom from care, are wholly free of them.

The dependence of human deliberation on thinking *in abstracto*, hence on judging and inferring, seems to have misled Descartes and Spinoza into identifying decisions of the will with the capacity to affirm and deny, the power of judgment. Descartes concluded that the will, indifferent and free in his account, is also responsible for every theoretical error. Spinoza concluded that motives determine the will necessarily, just as grounds determine judgment.[^2] The latter is correct in itself, but emerges as a true conclusion from false premises.

The difference between the ways motives move animals and humans has far-reaching effects on their natures and contributes greatly to the pervasive, obvious difference between their lives. An animal is moved only by an intuitive representation. A human being tries to exclude this kind of motivation altogether and let abstract representations alone determine action. This makes the best possible use of reason's advantage. Independent of the present, one considers the consequences of passing pleasure and pain rather than choosing or avoiding them in themselves. Apart from quite insignificant acts, it is usually abstract, thought-out motives rather than present impressions that determine us.

Hence a single deprivation is fairly easy to bear for the moment, but renunciation is terribly hard. The former concerns only the fleeting present; the latter concerns the future and so contains innumerable deprivations, for which it stands. Our suffering and joy therefore usually have their cause not in the actual present but in abstract thoughts. These often become unbearable and create torments beside which all animal suffering is small. We may not even feel our own physical pain over them. Indeed, amid violent mental suffering we sometimes cause ourselves physical pain simply to draw attention away from the former. In the greatest mental distress people tear out their hair, beat their breasts, scratch their faces, or roll on the ground. These are violent ways of distracting themselves from an unbearable thought.

Because mental pain is so much greater that it dulls physical pain, suicide becomes easy for a person in despair or consumed by morbid despondency, even if the thought made him recoil when he was at ease. Care and passion, the play of thoughts, also wear down the body more often and more severely than physical hardships. Epictetus therefore rightly says: `Ταράσσει τοὺς ἀνθρώπους οὐ τὰ πράγματα, ἀλλὰ τὰ περὶ τῶν πραγμάτων δόγματα` (`Perturbant homines non res ipsae, sed de rebus decreta`—People are disturbed not by things themselves but by their judgments about things; V). Seneca likewise says: `Plura sunt, quae nos terrent, quam quae premunt, et saepius opinione quam re laboramus` (More things frighten us than oppress us, and we suffer more often from opinion than from reality; letter 5).

Till Eulenspiegel offered an excellent parody of human nature: he laughed while walking uphill but wept while going downhill. Children who have hurt themselves often begin to cry not over the pain but only when someone pities them, because that arouses the thought of pain. Such great differences in action and life flow from the differing modes of cognition in animals and human beings.

The emergence of a distinct and definite individual character, another principal difference between humans and animals, which have almost only a species character, likewise depends on the choice among several motives made possible only by abstract concepts. Only after a choice do decisions that vary among individuals indicate their individual characters. An animal's action depends only on whether an impression is present, assuming it is a motive at all for that species.

In a human being, therefore, only a *decision*, not a mere wish, is a valid sign of character, both to oneself and to others. A decision itself becomes certain only in the deed. A wish is simply the necessary result of a present impression, whether an external stimulus or a passing inner mood. It is as immediate, necessary, and unconsidered as an animal's action. Like that action, it therefore expresses only the species character, not the individual's: it indicates what a *human being in general* could do, not what the *individual* feeling the wish is capable of doing.

The deed alone is a human action requiring some deliberation. A person usually has command of reason and thus acts reflectively, deciding according to abstract motives. The deed expresses the intelligible maxim of conduct, the result of innermost willing. It stands as a letter in the word that spells out empirical character, itself merely the expression in time of intelligible character. In a healthy mind, therefore, deeds alone burden the conscience, not wishes and thoughts. Only our deeds hold up the mirror of our will. An act committed without any reflection in a truly blind passion, mentioned above, lies somewhere between a mere wish and a decision. Genuine remorse, itself manifested in a deed, can therefore erase it from the picture of our will—our life course—like an incorrectly drawn line. One curious comparison may be added: the relation between wish and deed has an accidental but exact analogy to the relation between electrical induction and electrical transmission.

This whole discussion of freedom and its related questions leads to the following conclusion. The will in itself, outside appearance, may be called free and even omnipotent. Yet in its individual appearances illuminated by cognition—human beings and animals—motives determine it, and each character always reacts to them in the same lawful, necessary way. Through abstract, rational cognition, human beings have a *capacity to choose* beyond the animal's. This makes a person the battleground of conflicting motives without releasing him from their rule. It permits full expression of individual character, but is in no way freedom of individual willing, independence from the law of causality, whose necessity extends over human beings as over every other appearance. That, and no more, is the difference reason or conceptual cognition brings between human and animal willing.

There can, however, be a wholly different phenomenon of the human will, impossible for animals. It occurs when a human being leaves behind cognition of individual things as such, which is governed by the principle of sufficient reason, and through cognition of the Ideas sees through the *principium individuationis* (principle of individuation). Then the will's true freedom as thing in itself may actually emerge. The appearance enters a certain contradiction with itself, called self-denial; in the end the in-itself of its essence even cancels itself. This true and sole immediate expression of the will's freedom in itself, even in appearance, cannot yet be presented clearly here. It will be the final subject of our inquiry.

Having established the unchangeability of empirical character as merely the unfolding of the timeless intelligible character, and the necessity with which actions follow from its encounter with motives, we must first dispel an inference easily drawn in favour of reprehensible inclinations. Our character can be regarded as the unfolding in time of a timeless and therefore indivisible and unchangeable act of will, the intelligible character. This fixes unchangeably everything essential, the ethical content of our conduct, which must find expression in empirical character. Only the inessential, outer shape of our life course depends on the forms in which motives present themselves. One might therefore conclude that trying to improve one's character or resist evil inclinations is vain, and that one would do better to submit to the unalterable and immediately indulge every inclination, however evil. But this is exactly like the inference drawn from the theory of unavoidable fate, known as the `ἀργὸς λόγος` (idle argument), more recently called “Turkish fatalism.” Cicero presents its proper refutation, attributed to Chrysippus, in *On Fate*, chapters 12–13.

Everything may be regarded as irrevocably determined by fate, but only through the chain of causes. It can never be determined that an effect will occur without its cause. The event is not predestined in isolation, but as the result of preceding causes. Fate determines both the outcome and the means whose outcome it is. If the means do not occur, the outcome certainly will not either. Both follow fate's decree, which we always learn only afterward.

As events always follow fate, the endless chain of causes, our deeds will always accord with our intelligible character. Yet as we do not know fate beforehand, we have no *a priori* insight into this character. Only *a posteriori*, through experience, do we learn to know ourselves as we learn to know others. If our intelligible character required a long struggle with an evil inclination before we could reach a good decision, the struggle must precede it and run its course. Reflection on character's unchangeability and the single source of all our deeds must not lead us to anticipate its decision in favour of either side. We shall see what sort of people we are in the decision that follows and behold ourselves reflected in our deeds.

This explains the satisfaction or anguish with which we look back on our life. It is not that past deeds still exist; they are over and gone. Their great importance to us lies in their meaning. They are the imprint of character, the mirror of will in which we recognize our innermost self, the core of our willing. Since we learn this only afterward, not beforehand, it is for us to strive and struggle in time so that the picture formed by our deeds may, as far as possible, calm us rather than cause us anguish. The meaning of that calm or anguish will be examined later. Here another, independent consideration belongs.

Beside intelligible and empirical character, we must mention a third, different from both: *acquired character*. One acquires it only in life, through experience of the world. It is what people mean when they praise someone for having character or blame someone for lacking it. One might think that since empirical character, as an appearance of intelligible character, is unchangeable and consistent within itself like any natural phenomenon, a person would always appear consistent and true to himself, with no need to acquire a character artificially through experience and reflection. But matters are otherwise. One is always the same person, yet does not always understand oneself. One often misconstrues oneself until some degree of genuine self-knowledge has been attained.

Empirical character as a mere natural drive is without reason in itself. Its expressions are also disturbed by reason, and all the more so in someone with greater reflection and powers of thought. Reason continually presents what belongs to *the human being in general*, as the character of the species, and what is possible for a human being to will or accomplish. This makes it harder to see what, from among all these possibilities, one alone wills and can do by virtue of one's individuality. A person finds in himself the capacity for many widely different human strivings and abilities, but cannot know their relative strength in his own individuality without experience. Even if he chooses pursuits suited to his character, he still feels at particular moments and in particular moods impulses toward opposite, incompatible ones. To pursue the former without disturbance, he must entirely suppress the latter.

Our physical path on earth is only a line, not a surface. Likewise, if we want to take and possess one thing in life, we must leave countless others to the right and left, renouncing them. If we cannot decide to do so but grasp, like children at a fair, everything that attracts us in passing, we make the misguided attempt to turn the line of our path into a surface. We then run in zigzags, wander back and forth like will-o'-the-wisps, and get nowhere.

Another comparison comes from Hobbes's theory of right. Originally each person has a right to every thing, but to no thing exclusively. One can gain an exclusive right to particular things by giving up one's right to all the others, while others do the same with respect to what one has chosen. Life works similarly. We can seriously and successfully follow any particular pursuit—pleasure, honour, wealth, science, art, or virtue—only when we abandon all claims foreign to it and renounce everything else. Mere willing and ability are therefore insufficient. A person must also *know* what he wills and *know* what he can do. Only then will he show character and accomplish something worthwhile.

Until he reaches this point, he lacks character in the ordinary sense, despite the natural consistency of his empirical character. On the whole he must remain true to himself and follow his course, drawn by his daemon, but he traces no straight line. His path trembles and turns unevenly; he hesitates, deviates, doubles back, and causes himself remorse and pain. He sees, in great and small matters, so much that is possible and attainable for human beings, without knowing what alone suits him, is feasible for him, or is even enjoyable to him. He envies others their positions and circumstances, though these suit their characters and not his. In them he would be unhappy and perhaps unable to endure them.

As a fish is at ease only in water, a bird in air, and a mole beneath the earth, each person is at ease only in an atmosphere suited to him; courtly air, for example, is not breathable for everyone. Lacking sufficient insight into this, many undertake experiments that fail. They force their character in particular instances, only to yield to it again overall. What they laboriously gain against their nature gives them no enjoyment, and what they learn remains dead. Even ethically, an act too noble for their character, arising from a concept or doctrine rather than pure, immediate impulse, loses all merit afterward through selfish remorse, even in their own eyes. `Velle non discitur` (willing cannot be taught).

We learn the inflexibility of other people's characters only through experience. Until then we childishly believe we can use rational arguments, requests, pleading, example, or generosity to make someone give up his nature, change his conduct or thinking, or even expand his abilities. The same is true of ourselves. We must learn from experience what we will and what we can do. Until then we do not know; we lack acquired character and often need hard blows from outside to throw us back onto our own path.

When at last we have learned this, we have gained what the world calls character: *acquired character*. It is simply the most complete possible knowledge of our own individuality. It is abstract, hence clear, knowledge of the unchangeable properties of our empirical character and of the extent and direction of our mental and physical powers: all the strengths and weaknesses of our individuality. We can then carry out the inherently unchangeable role of our own person deliberately and methodically, instead of improvising it without rules. We can fill the gaps that moods or weaknesses produce in it by following firm concepts.

We have brought the mode of action already necessary to our individual nature under clearly conscious maxims that remain before us. We carry it out as reflectively as though it were learned, without being led astray by the passing influence of a mood or an impression of the moment, checked by the bitterness or sweetness of a particular incident on the way, or subject to hesitation, wavering, or inconsistency. No longer beginners waiting, experimenting, and groping to see what we really will and can do, we know this once and for all. In every choice we need only apply general principles to particular cases and reach a decision at once. We know our will in general and do not let mood or external appeals lure us into deciding in a particular case what opposes it as a whole.

Likewise, we know the kind and degree of our strengths and weaknesses and spare ourselves much pain. There is really no enjoyment except in using and feeling one's own powers, while the greatest pain is perceiving their lack when they are needed. Once we have discovered where our strengths and weaknesses lie, we cultivate and use our outstanding natural aptitudes and seek to profit from them in every way. We turn toward work where they have a place and value. Yet with self-command we avoid pursuits for which nature has given us little aptitude, and take care not to attempt what we cannot accomplish. Only someone who has come this far will always be fully and reflectively himself. He will never be abandoned by himself, because he always knows what he can ask of himself.

He will often enjoy feeling his strengths and rarely suffer the pain of being reminded of his weaknesses. The latter is humiliation and perhaps causes the greatest mental pain; it is much easier to face one's misfortune than one's incompetence clearly. If we know our strengths and weaknesses completely, we shall not pretend to powers we lack or pass false coin, for such play-acting ultimately fails. The whole human being is only the appearance of his will. Nothing could therefore be more perverse than to begin from reflection and want to be something other than one is: the will would contradict itself directly. Imitating someone else's qualities and peculiarities is more disgraceful than wearing someone else's clothes, for it pronounces a judgment of worthlessness upon oneself. Knowing one's own disposition, abilities of every kind, and their unchangeable limits is the surest way in this respect to the greatest possible contentment with oneself.

What holds for external circumstances also holds for inner ones: no comfort is more effective than complete certainty of unalterable necessity. The evil that has struck us torments us less than thoughts of how it might have been prevented. Nothing calms us more effectively than viewing what happened from the standpoint of necessity. From it all accidents appear as instruments of a ruling fate, and we recognize the evil as unavoidably brought about by the conflict of inner and outer circumstances: this is fatalism. We lament and rage only so long as we hope thereby either to affect others or to spur ourselves into extraordinary effort. Children and adults alike settle down once they see clearly that it could not possibly be otherwise:

> `θυμὸν ἐνὶ στήθεσσι φίλον δαμάσαντες ἀνάγκῃ.`<br>
> (`Animo in pectoribus nostro domito necessitate.`)
>
> Translation: Having subdued the dear spirit in our breasts by necessity.

We are like captured elephants that rage and struggle terribly for days until they see it is fruitless. Then they suddenly and calmly offer their necks to the yoke, tamed forever. We are like King David, who ceaselessly besieged Jehovah with pleas and behaved despairingly while his son still lived, but thought no more of it once the son was dead. Thus countless people bear lasting evils—disability, poverty, low rank, ugliness, an unpleasant place to live—with complete indifference. They no longer feel them, like scarred wounds, simply because they know inner or outer necessity leaves nothing to be changed. More fortunate people cannot understand how anyone bears them.

Clear knowledge of necessity reconciles us as firmly to inner circumstances as to outer ones. Once we have recognized our faults and weaknesses as clearly and finally as our good qualities and strengths, set our aims accordingly, and accepted what is unattainable, we most securely escape, as far as our individuality permits, the bitterest suffering: dissatisfaction with ourselves. That follows inevitably from ignorance of our individuality, false self-esteem, and the presumption arising from it. Ovid's lines apply excellently to the painful chapters of self-knowledge I have recommended:

> `Optimus ille animi vindex laedentia pectus`<br>
> `Vincula qui rupit, dedoluitque semel.`
>
> Translation: The best avenger of his spirit is he who breaks the bonds that wound his heart and grieves once and for all.

So much for *acquired character*. It matters less to ethics proper than to worldly life, but its discussion naturally took its place as a third kind beside intelligible and empirical character. We had to examine the first two at some length to understand clearly how the will in all its appearances is subject to necessity while in itself it may still be called free, even omnipotent.
