# §. 58.

All satisfaction, or what is commonly called happiness, is in its true nature always merely *negative*, never positive. It is not a blessing that comes to us originally and of itself, but always the satisfaction of a wish. A wish, which means a lack, is the prior condition of every enjoyment. With satisfaction the wish ceases, and so does the enjoyment. Satisfaction or happiness can therefore be no more than release from some pain or need. This includes not only manifest suffering but every wish whose insistence disturbs our peace, and even deadening boredom, which makes existence a burden.

It is difficult to attain or accomplish anything. Endless obstacles and exertions stand against every undertaking, and difficulties pile up at every step. Yet once everything has been overcome and attained, nothing has been gained except release from some suffering or wish: one is simply as one was before it arose. What is immediately given to us is always lack, and thus pain. Satisfaction and enjoyment can be known only indirectly, by recalling the preceding suffering and deprivation that ended when they began.

That is why we are hardly aware of, or value, the goods and advantages we actually possess. We think they are simply how things should be, because they make us happy only negatively, by keeping suffering away. Only after losing them do we feel their worth: lack, deprivation, and suffering are positive, announcing themselves immediately. Thus recalling need, illness, or privation we have overcome pleases us, because it is our only way of enjoying our present advantages. From this standpoint of egoism, the form of the will to live, we must also admit that seeing or hearing of another's suffering gives us satisfaction by the same route, as Lucretius says beautifully and frankly at the beginning of his second book:

> `Suave, mari magno, turbantibus aequora ventis,`<br>
> `Et terra magnum alterius spectare laborem:`<br>
> `Non, quia vexari quemquam est jucunda volupta;`<br>
> `Sed, quibus ipse malis careas, quia cernere suave est`
>
> Translation: It is pleasant, when winds trouble the great sea, to watch from land another's great struggle—not because anyone's distress is a pleasure, but because it is pleasant to see what evils one has escaped.

We shall see later, however, that pleasure arising from this indirect awareness of one's own well-being comes very close to the source of actual, positive malice.

Art, especially poetry, faithfully mirrors the essence of the world and life and also confirms that all happiness is negative rather than positive. It cannot be a lasting satisfaction; it releases us from one pain or lack only to be followed by another, or by *languor* (listlessness), empty longing, and boredom. Every epic or drama can depict only the struggle, striving, and conflict to gain happiness, never permanent, complete happiness itself. It leads its hero through a thousand difficulties and dangers to a goal, then drops the curtain as soon as the goal is reached. The only thing left to show would be that the shining goal where the hero expected to find happiness had merely teased him and that he is no better off after reaching it. Since genuine, lasting happiness is impossible, it cannot be a subject of art.

The idyll does, admittedly, aim to portray such happiness. Yet the idyll cannot remain one. In the poet's hands it becomes either an epic—a very slight one made up of small sufferings, joys, and strivings, which is the usual case—or merely descriptive poetry. It then portrays nature's beauty, in fact pure, will-free cognition. This is indeed the only pure happiness: it is preceded by neither suffering nor need, and is necessarily followed by neither remorse, pain, emptiness, nor tedium. But it can fill only moments of life, never the whole of it.

What we see in poetry we find again in music. In melody we recognized the inner history of the self-conscious will expressed universally: the secret life, longing, suffering, and joy, the ebb and flow of the human heart. Melody continually departs from its key note, wandering by a thousand curious paths as far as the most painful dissonance. At last it finds the key note again, expressing the will's satisfaction and repose. There is then nothing more to do with it; holding it longer would produce only tiresome, empty monotony corresponding to boredom.

The endless striving of the will explains what these reflections were intended to show: the impossibility of lasting satisfaction and the negative nature of all happiness. As shown at the end of Book Two, the will objectivated in human life, as in every appearance, strives without goal or end. We find the mark of this endlessness stamped on every part of its appearance, from its most general form, boundless time and space, to the most complete appearance of all, human life and striving.

We can distinguish three theoretical extremes of human life and regard them as elements within actual life. First is intense willing and great passion (*rajas-guna*). This emerges in great historical characters and is portrayed in epic and drama. It can also show itself in a small sphere, for the magnitude of the objects here is measured only by how strongly they move the will, not by their external relations. Second is pure cognition, apprehension of the Ideas, requiring cognition's release from the service of the will: the life of genius (*sattva-guna*). Third is the greatest lethargy of will and of the cognition bound to it: empty longing and life-paralyzing boredom (*tamas-guna*).

An individual's life is far from remaining in any one of these extremes. It rarely even touches them. Most often it is a feeble, wavering approach now to one and now to another: poor willing for petty objects, endlessly repeated to escape boredom. It is truly incredible how empty and meaningless most lives appear from outside, and how dim and thoughtless they feel from within. There is a faint longing and torment, a dreamlike staggering through the four ages of life toward death, accompanied by a series of trivial thoughts. People resemble clockworks wound up and set going without knowing why. Each conception and birth winds the clock of a human life anew to play once more a tune already played countless times, phrase by phrase and beat by beat, with insignificant variations.

Each individual, each human face and life course, is one more brief dream of the infinite spirit of nature, the enduring will to live. It is one more fleeting figure that the will playfully sketches on its infinite page of space and time, allows to stand for a moment vanishingly small beside them, and then erases to make room for a new one. Yet here lies life's troubling side: each such fleeting figure, each such insipid idea, must be paid for by the whole will to live in all its vehemence, with many deep sufferings and at last a bitter death, long dreaded until it arrives. That is why the sight of a corpse makes us suddenly serious.

Every individual's life, viewed as a whole with only its most significant features, is a tragedy; examined detail by detail, it has the character of a comedy. The activities and troubles of each day, the relentless annoyances of the moment, the hopes and fears of a week, and the mishaps of every hour brought about by chance, always ready for mischief, are all comic scenes. Yet desires never fulfilled, striving frustrated, hopes mercilessly trampled by fate, and the wretched errors of a whole life, ending with growing suffering and death, always make a tragedy. As if fate wished to add mockery to the misery of our existence, our lives must contain all the pains of tragedy, while in life's sprawling details we cannot even retain the dignity of tragic figures and must inevitably be silly comic characters.

Great and small afflictions fill every life and keep us continually restless and moving. Even so, they cannot conceal life's inadequacy for fulfilling the mind, the emptiness and insipidity of existence, or shut out boredom, always ready to fill any pause left by care. Hence the human mind, unsatisfied with the cares, worries, and occupations imposed by the real world, creates an imaginary one in a thousand kinds of superstition. It then busies itself with this world in every way, wasting time and energy on it as soon as the real world offers a peace it cannot enjoy. This is especially common at first among peoples for whom a mild climate and fertile soil make life easier: above all Hindus, then Greeks and Romans, later Italians, Spaniards, and others.

Human beings make demons, gods, and saints in their own image. They must continually offer them sacrifices and prayers, adorn temples, make and discharge vows, undertake pilgrimages, pay respects, decorate images, and so on. Their service becomes interwoven everywhere with reality and even obscures it. Every event of life is taken as a response from these beings. Dealing with them fills half of life, constantly sustains hope, and through the appeal of illusion often becomes more interesting than dealing with real beings. It expresses and reveals humanity's double need: partly for help and support, partly for occupation and diversion. It often works against the first need, wasting precious time and strength in prayer and sacrifice instead of warding off actual misfortunes and dangers. It serves the second need all the better through imaginary dealings with a dreamed-up spirit world. This is the far from negligible benefit of every superstition.
