# §. 53.

## Book Four

The world as will, second consideration:<br>
With self-knowledge attained, affirmation and denial of the will to live.

> `Tempore quo cognitio simul advenit, amor e medio supersurrexit.`<br>
> `Oupnek'hat, studio Anquetil Duperron, vol. II. p. 216.`
>
> Translation: At the time when knowledge appeared, love arose from the midst. *Oupnek'hat*, edited by Anquetil-Duperron, vol. 2, p. 216.

The final part of our inquiry announces itself as the most serious, for it concerns human actions: a subject that immediately concerns everyone and can be foreign or indifferent to no one. Indeed, it accords so closely with human nature to relate everything else to action that, in any sustained inquiry, people will regard the part concerning action as the result of all its contents, at least insofar as those contents interest them. They will therefore give this part serious attention, even if they have given none to the rest.

In ordinary language one might, in this sense, call the part that now follows “practical philosophy,” as opposed to the theoretical philosophy treated so far. In my view, however, all philosophy is always theoretical. Whatever its immediate subject of investigation, it must essentially maintain a purely contemplative attitude and inquire, not prescribe. The old claims that philosophy should become practical, guide action, or reform character are claims it should at last give up with mature understanding. Where the worth or worthlessness of an existence, salvation or damnation, is at stake, its lifeless concepts do not decide the matter. What decides it is the human being's innermost nature: the daemon who guides him and whom he himself, rather than the daemon, chose, as Plato puts it; his intelligible character, as Kant calls it. Virtue cannot be taught, any more than genius can. Concepts are as barren for virtue as for art, and can be used only as tools. We would be as foolish to expect our moral systems and ethics to produce virtuous, noble people and saints as to expect our aesthetics to produce poets, sculptors, and musicians.

Philosophy can do no more anywhere than interpret and explain what exists. It can bring before reason, in clear abstract knowledge, the essence of the world that speaks intelligibly to everyone *in concreto* (in the concrete), that is, as feeling, and do so in every possible relation and from every standpoint. The preceding three books sought to do this from other standpoints, with the generality proper to philosophy. This book will likewise consider human action—perhaps the most important side of the world not only in subjective estimation, as I remarked above, but also by objective judgment. I shall remain wholly faithful to the method used so far and rely on what has already been established. In fact, I shall do no more than develop in relation to human action the one thought that forms the content of this whole work, as I have developed it in relation to every other subject. This is the last thing I can do to communicate that thought as fully as possible.

The standpoint and method announced here should already make clear that this ethical book will contain no prescriptions or doctrine of duties, still less a universal moral principle, a recipe for producing every virtue. Nor shall we speak of an “*unconditional ought*,” which, as explained in the appendix, is a contradiction, or of a “law for freedom,” which contains the same contradiction. We shall not speak of an ought at all. That is how one speaks to children and to peoples in their childhood, not to those who have acquired the full cultivation of an adult age. It is surely a palpable contradiction to call the will free and yet prescribe laws according to which it *ought to will*: “ought to will”—wooden iron!

According to our whole view, the will is not only free but even omnipotent. Not only its action but its world proceeds from it. As it is, so its action appears and so its world appears. Both are its self-knowledge and nothing else. It determines itself and thereby determines both, for nothing exists outside it, and they are the will itself. Only thus is it truly autonomous; on every other view it is heteronomous. Our philosophical effort can aim only to interpret and explain human action and the widely different, even opposed, maxims of which it is the living expression. We must explain their innermost nature and content in connection with the inquiry so far, just as we have tried to interpret the other appearances of the world and bring their innermost essence to clear, abstract cognition.

Here our philosophy will maintain the same *immanence* as before. Contrary to Kant's great teaching, it will not use the forms of appearance, whose general expression is the principle of sufficient reason, as a pole vault over appearance itself—the only thing that gives those forms meaning—and land in a boundless realm of empty inventions. This actual world of what can be known, in which we exist and which exists in us, remains both the material and the limit of our inquiry. It is so rich that even the deepest investigation of which the human mind is capable could not exhaust it.

The real, knowable world will supply our ethical inquiry, as it supplied the preceding inquiries, with more than enough substance and reality. We need not seek refuge in empty, negative concepts and then persuade ourselves that we have said something when we speak with raised eyebrows of “the Absolute,” “the Infinite,” “the Supersensible,” and similar bare negations: `οὐδέν ἐστι, ἢ τὸ τῆς στερήσεως ὄνομα, μετὰ ἀμυδρᾶς ἐπινοίας`—`nihil est, nisi negationis nomen, cum obscura notione. Jul. or. 5` (it is nothing but the name of a negation accompanied by a dim conception; Julian, Oration 5). We could say “Cloud-cuckoo-land” (`νεφελοκοκκυγία`) more briefly. We shall not serve up such covered, empty dishes.

Nor shall we tell stories here any more than before and pass them off as philosophy. In our view, anyone who thinks the world's essence can be grasped *historically*, however subtly disguised the historical account may be, remains immeasurably far from philosophical knowledge of the world. This is so whenever a view of the world as it is in itself includes any *becoming*, having-become, or future becoming, or whenever “before” or “after” has the slightest significance. It then seeks and finds, openly or covertly, a beginning and an end of the world and a path between them, and the philosopher may even locate himself on that path.

Such *historical philosophizing* usually produces a cosmogony, which admits many varieties. It may also produce a system of emanation or a doctrine of falling away. Driven to a final path by despair over the failure of those attempts, it may reverse them and teach perpetual becoming, sprouting, arising, and emergence into light from darkness, from a dark ground, original ground, groundless ground, and more such babble. The shortest reply is that an entire eternity—an infinite time—has already passed before the present moment. Everything that can and is supposed to become must therefore have become already.

For all such historical philosophy, however grandly it behaves, takes *time* to be a determination of things in themselves, as if Kant had never existed. It thus remains with what Kant calls appearance as opposed to the thing in itself, what Plato calls that which becomes but never is as opposed to that which is but never becomes, or what Indians call the veil of Maya. This is cognition governed by the principle of sufficient reason. It never reaches the inner essence of things; it only pursues appearances without end, moving without goal like a squirrel in a wheel. Eventually, perhaps, the thinker tires, stops at an arbitrary point above or below, and then tries to force others to respect it.

The genuine philosophical way of regarding the world teaches us to know its inner essence and thus leads beyond appearance. It asks not where the world comes from, where it goes, or why, but always and everywhere *what* it is. It does not consider things by any relation, as becoming and passing away, or according to one of the four forms of the principle of sufficient reason. Instead it takes as its subject precisely what remains when every mode of inquiry following that principle has been set aside: the world's ever-identical essence, which appears in all relations but is itself subject to none of them—its Ideas. This cognition is the source of art and philosophy alike. As we shall find in this book, it is also the source of the state of mind that alone leads to true holiness and redemption from the world.
