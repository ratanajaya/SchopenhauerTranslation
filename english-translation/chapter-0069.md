# §. 67.

We have seen how justice arose from seeing through the *principium individuationis* (principle of individuation) to a lesser degree, and genuine goodness of disposition to a greater degree. The latter showed itself as pure, that is, unselfish, love toward others. Where it becomes complete, it places another individual and his fate wholly on a level with oneself and one's own fate. It can never go further, for there is no reason to prefer another individual to oneself. Yet concern for the welfare or lives of a great many others may outweigh concern for the welfare of a single individual. In such a case a character that has attained the highest goodness and perfect nobility will wholly sacrifice his own welfare and life for many others. Thus died Codrus, Leonidas, Regulus, Decius Mus, and Arnold von Winkelried. So does anyone who willingly and knowingly goes to certain death for his people or his country. This level also includes anyone who willingly accepts suffering and death to defend what rightly belongs to and benefits humanity as a whole—important general truths and the removal of great errors. Thus Socrates died; thus Giordano Bruno and many other champions of truth found death at the stake at the hands of priests.

With regard to the paradox stated above, I must now recall that we found suffering essential to life as a whole and inseparable from it. We saw that every wish springs from a need, a lack, a suffering, and that every satisfaction therefore removes a pain rather than bringing a positive happiness. Pleasures falsely promise desire that they are a positive good; in truth they are negative, merely the end of an ill. Whatever goodness, love, and nobility do for others can therefore only relieve their suffering. The only thing that can move them to good deeds and acts of love is *knowledge of another's suffering*, understood immediately from one's own and put on an equal footing with it. Pure love (ἀγάπη, *caritas*) is consequently compassion by its very nature, whether it relieves great suffering or a small suffering such as any unsatisfied wish.

In direct opposition to *Kant*, who recognizes something as truly good or virtuous only if it arises from abstract reflection, particularly from the concept of duty and the categorical imperative, and calls felt compassion weakness rather than virtue, I say: a mere concept is as unfruitful for genuine virtue as for genuine art. Every true and pure love is compassion, and every love that is not compassion is self-love. Self-love is ἔρως (*eros*); compassion is ἀγάπη (*agape*). The two are often mixed. Even genuine friendship is always a mixture of self-love and compassion. The former lies in pleasure at the friend's presence, whose individuality corresponds to our own, and almost always makes up the larger part; compassion appears in sincere concern for his welfare and distress and in unselfish sacrifices for his sake. Even Spinoza says, *Benevolentia nihil aliud est, quam cupiditas ex commiseratione orta* (*Ethics*, III, proposition 27, corollary 3, scholium; “Benevolence is nothing other than desire arising from compassion”). To confirm our paradoxical claim, notice that the tone and words of the language and endearments of pure love exactly coincide with those of compassion. Incidentally, the same Italian word, *pietà*, denotes compassion and pure love.

This is also the place to discuss one of the most striking peculiarities of human nature, *weeping*, which, like laughter, is among the expressions distinguishing humans from animals. Weeping is by no means a direct expression of pain: people weep over very few pains. In my view, one never even weeps directly over a pain one feels, but only over its recurrence in reflection. One passes from the felt pain, even when bodily, to a mere representation of it. One then finds one's own state so deserving of pity that one is firmly and sincerely convinced one would help if someone else were suffering it, out of compassion and love. Yet one is oneself the object of one's sincere compassion: with the most helpful disposition, one is the person needing help. One feels that one suffers more than one could bear to see another suffer. In this strangely interwoven mood, immediately felt suffering comes back to perception only by a double detour. It is imagined as someone else's, shared as such through compassion, and suddenly perceived again as immediately one's own. Nature then gives itself relief through the peculiar physical convulsion of weeping.

*Weeping* is thus *compassion for oneself*, or compassion turned back on its starting point. It requires a capacity for love and compassion as well as imagination. Hard-hearted people and people without imagination therefore seldom weep. Weeping is even always taken as a sign of some goodness of character, and disarms anger, because we feel that someone who can still weep must also be capable of love, that is, compassion for others. Compassion enters in precisely the way described into the mood that leads to tears. Petrarch's description of the rise of his own tears, expressing his feeling simply and truthfully, exactly fits this account:

*I vo pensando: e nel pensar m' assalo*<br>
*Una pietà si forte di me stesso,*<br>
*Che mi conduce spesso,*<br>
*Ad alto lagrimar, ch' i non soleva.*[^1]

Our account is also confirmed by the fact that children who have suffered pain usually begin to weep only when someone pities them. They weep not over the pain but over the representation of it. When another person's suffering moves us to tears, we vividly put ourselves in the sufferer's place in imagination. Or we see in that person's fate the lot of all humanity, above all our own. By a long detour we thus still weep over ourselves and feel compassion for ourselves. This also seems a principal reason for the universal and therefore natural weeping at deaths. The mourner is not weeping for his loss; he would be ashamed of such egoistic tears, whereas he is sometimes ashamed of *not* weeping. At first he does weep over the deceased person's fate. Yet he also weeps when death, after long, severe, incurable suffering, was a welcome release for that person. What principally moves him is compassion for the lot of humanity as a whole, condemned to finitude: every life, however full of striving and often of deeds, must go out and become nothing. In humanity's lot he sees above all his own, all the more strongly the closer the deceased was to him, and most of all when it was his father. Even if old age and illness had made the father's life a torment and his helplessness a heavy burden to the son, the son still weeps intensely at his death, for the reason given.[^2]
